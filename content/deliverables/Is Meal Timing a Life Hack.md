The short answer the held evidence supports: **meal-timing is mostly a delivery system for eating less, not
an independent lever on your health — with one narrow, preliminary exception.** Time-restricted eating
(TRE), one-meal-a-day (OMAD), 16:8, skipping breakfast — these help the people they help almost entirely
because a shorter eating window quietly cuts calories and is easy to follow, not because the *clock* does
something to your metabolism. The one place timing might act on its own is **eating earlier in the day**,
where a single tiny trial found real improvements in blood pressure and insulin sensitivity with no weight
change at all. That signal is genuine but fragile: eight men, five weeks, surrogate markers only. Everything
else marketed as a *metabolic hack* fails the one test that matters — does it beat a calorie-matched
control? — or has never been tested against one ([[Time-Restricted Eating]]).

This tracks the popular framing on one point and diverges on another. Popular culture is right that a
short eating window helps many people lose weight (it does, by cutting intake and being simple to stick
to). It is wrong that the *timing itself* is doing metabolic magic — the one controlled trial (TREAT) shows
the eating *window* alone adds nothing to weight, and the calorie-matched trial (eTRF), which held weight
constant by design, found its only benefit — on blood pressure and insulin sensitivity — from eating
*early*, which is the opposite of the breakfast-skipping most people actually practise.

## The one test everything runs through: effect beyond the deficit

TRE reliably produces weight loss in uncontrolled reports. But so does joining any weight-loss study. The
honest question is never *does TRE work?* — it is **whether TRE beats a control eating the same number
of calories.** If the fasting arm only wins because it ate less, the lever is the calorie deficit, not the
clock. This is the same trap that runs through the rest of nutrition: the clever protocol usually turns out
to be a calorie-delivery vehicle in disguise ([[Free Sugars Intake]], [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]). Two randomized trials, with opposite designs, let us separate the two — and they
do not contradict each other once you see they test different things ([[Time-Restricted Eating]]).

## Sub-question 1 — Weight: the window alone buys nothing

### Lead: in the one adequately-powered, real-world trial, a 16:8 window with no other change produced no weight advantage over normal eating

TREAT randomized 116 overweight adults to a self-selected
12pm-8pm window (skip breakfast, eat late) versus three structured meals, with **no calorie or
macronutrient target** — the pure *just restrict the window* version of TRE, tested free-living
[@lowe2020]. Both arms lost a little weight; the
difference between them was null: **−0.26 kg between groups (95% CI −1.30 to 0.78; P=.63)**
[@lowe2020]. The authors' bottom line:
«Time-restricted eating, in the absence of other interventions, is not more effective in weight loss than
eating throughout the day» [@lowe2020].

Every metabolic marker was flat too — no between-group difference in fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR,
HbA1c, triglycerides, or cholesterol [@lowe2020]. The
control-arm lesson is the one to keep: the single-arm TRE studies that reported \~3% weight loss had no
comparison group, and «participation in a weight loss study alone (even in the control group) is
sufficient to lead to short-term weight loss»
[@lowe2020]. That is exactly the deficit-in-disguise
effect the one test is built to catch.

### Verdict: no independent effect on weight

TRE's real-world value, where it has one, is that a short
window tends to *cut intake* and is a single simple rule to follow — the effect is the deficit and the
adherence, relabeled.

## Sub-question 2 — Glycaemia and blood pressure: the one place timing may act on its own

### Lead: shifting the same food earlier in the day improved blood pressure and insulin sensitivity with zero weight change — a real timing signal, but tiny and preliminary

eTRF is the mirror-image trial: a
supervised, controlled-feeding crossover in 8 men with prediabetes, comparing a 6-hour *early* window
(dinner before 3pm) against a 12-hour window, with **calories matched meal-by-meal and body weight held
constant by design** — «feeding participants enough food to maintain their weight»
[@sutton2018]. Weight was stable in both arms
(difference 0.5 kg, p=0.12), so any effect cannot be routed through weight loss — the whole point of the
trial [@sutton2018].

What moved:

- **Blood pressure fell sharply.** Systolic and diastolic dropped by **11 and 10 mm Hg** respectively
  (both p=0.03) — the authors call it «on par with the effectiveness of anti-hypertensive medications such
  as angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors»
  [@sutton2018]. Arterial stiffness, LDL and HDL
  were unchanged.
- **Insulin sensitivity improved** even though glucose did not. Mean insulin fell **26 mU/L** and a
  measure of insulin resistance dropped **36 U/mg (p=0.005)**, while fasting and post-load glucose were
  unchanged [@sutton2018]. Oxidative stress fell
  \~14% (partly because it *worsened* in the control arm), and evening appetite dropped.

The proposed mechanism is circadian: the body is better set up to handle food in the morning, so eating
aligned to the body clock helps, and **late** eating tends to be worse
[@sutton2018]. If real, this is a genuine timing
effect distinct from *eat less* — the one hypothesis worth keeping separate.

### But hold the enthusiasm

This rests on **eight prediabetic men, five weeks, surrogate markers with no
hard outcome and no weight change**. Three caveats bite:

- (1) the trial did not match fasting duration
  before testing — participants had fasted 18 hours in the eTRF arm versus 12 in the control — which biases
  *against* the insulin finding (so that one is probably real) but may *manufacture* the one adverse result,
  a 57 mg/dL rise in fasting triglycerides likely reflecting lipolysis from the longer fast
  [@sutton2018].
- (2) Everything measured is a
  surrogate — a marker that stands in for an outcome in the evidence, not the outcome itself; a favourable
  marker is a legitimate *target* only if its transmission to something patient-important is itself
  evidenced, which it is not here ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).
- (3) Men-only, prediabetic, and glucose and BP
  measured only in the morning.

### Verdict: a real but small weight-independent signal from eating *early* — on surrogates only, from one tiny trial

This is the honest exception to *timing does nothing.* It is not a licence to sell early TRE
as proven.

## The specific harm the window can add: lean-mass loss

### Lead: in TREAT, the modest weight the fasting arm lost was disproportionately muscle, not fat

Of the
1.70 kg the in-person TRE group lost, «approximately 65% of weight lost» was lean mass
[@lowe2020] — against a normal 20-30% during weight
loss. Appendicular (limb) lean mass showed a significant between-group deficit (−0.47 kg, P=.009), while
fat mass did not differ between arms at all [@lowe2020].

The likely reason: an ad-libitum short window cuts total intake and, with it, **protein** — which is
eaten mostly at meals — and the fix is to raise meal frequency or protein inside the window
[@lowe2020]. The authors flag this as «a caution for
patient populations at risk for sarcopenia» [@lowe2020]. So a late/short window can trade the fat-loss you wanted for muscle you did not
-> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]].

## Even if it did cause weight loss, weight loss by lifestyle is not a proven heart lever

A second layer of honesty: suppose TRE *did* reliably drop weight. Would that prevent heart attacks?

The largest, longest randomized trial of lifestyle weight loss (Look AHEAD, 5,145 adults with type 2 diabetes)
was stopped early for futility — the cardiovascular composite came in at **HR 0.95 (95% CI 0.83-1.09)**,
null, despite greater weight loss and better risk factors in the intervention arm
[@look2013].

A 54-trial meta-analysis whose pool
*includes* Look AHEAD (so it is consistent with, not independent corroboration of, the CV-null) reaches
the same cardiovascular-events null (RR 0.93) and adds — across its other populations — a modest cut in
**all-cause mortality (RR 0.82)** that is *not* routed through the heart
[@ma2017].

The point for meal timing: a protocol marketed
as a weight lever may deliver neither the weight loss (TREAT) nor, even if it did, a reliable
cardiovascular-event reduction via the lifestyle route ([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]).
Weight loss stays strongly indicated for what it *does* move — glycaemic control, diabetes remission, liver
fat, function — just not as a guaranteed heart-attack lever, and certainly not as a reason to credit meal
timing.

## Sub-questions 3-5 — appetite, sleep, breakfast: mostly named gaps

These are stated plainly as **gaps**: the wiki does not hold the trials to resolve them.

- **Appetite (insufficient evidence).** The signals are mixed and adherence-dependent — some report less
  hunger, some more. eTRF did lower *evening* appetite [@sutton2018], but the satiety hormone PYY fell (a discordance the authors themselves flag), and
  there is no held trial that settles the direction. **Gap: a dedicated appetite RCT.**
- **Sleep (unheld).** The plausible story — late, large meals near bedtime disrupt sleep, so shifting
  eating earlier may help — is mechanistically reasonable but **not evidenced in the held sources**. Note
  that TREAT's late window *reduced daytime movement* (about 2,500 fewer steps/day), a behavioural
  compensation worth watching [@lowe2020], but that is
  activity, not sleep. **Gap: a meal-timing sleep RCT.**
- **Is skipping breakfast a free lunch? (largely unheld, with a caution.)** There is no held
  breakfast-skipping RCT, so the *breakfast is the most important meal* claim cannot be adjudicated on held
  evidence — treat it as an open question, not a settled one. What the held evidence *does* say cuts
  against skipping breakfast as the smart move: the one weight-independent benefit came from eating
  **early** (eTRF), and the trial that skipped breakfast to eat *late* (TREAT) is the one that showed no
  weight advantage and the muscle-loss signal. Any benefit of skipping a meal depends on **not
  compensating** at later meals — the same pattern by which the body offsets a naive intervention it is
  handed ([[Exercise Energy Compensation]]: burn more, eat more or move less, and the deficit partly
  refills). **Gap: a breakfast-skipping RCT that measures compensation at later meals.**

## The adherence angle — kept separate from any metabolic claim

None of the above denies that TRE helps real people. It often does — because *don't eat after X* is a
single, simple rule, and a shorter window tends to cut intake without calorie counting. **A protocol you
will actually keep beats a *better* one you abandon** — adherence is part of the effect, and under
diminishing returns a smaller sustained dose beats a larger abandoned one ([[Time-Restricted Eating]],
[[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]). That is a real,
legitimate value — but it is an *adherence* and *calorie-deficit* value, not evidence that the timing does
something metabolic. Keep the two claims apart: *this made it easier for me to eat less* is true and
useful; *the fasting window fixed my metabolism* is not supported.

## What this means for a decision

- **Do not adopt TRE expecting the timing itself to move weight or metabolism.** If a short window helps
  you eat less and stick to it, that is a fine reason to use it — as a calorie-deficit tool, judged on
  whether you keep it.
- **If you do a window, put it EARLY and protect protein.** The only weight-independent benefit signal is
  from eating earlier; the only clear harm signal is muscle loss from an ad-libitum short window. Both
  point the same way — eat earlier in the day, and keep protein and meal frequency up inside the window.
  An 8-hour-or-longer early window is the realistic target: eTRF's own participants found the 6-hour window
  *harder* than the 18-hour fast, so the most efficacious protocol may be the least feasible one
  [@sutton2018].
- **Meal timing ranks LOW as a health lever.** It is heavily discussed relative to a small,
  surrogate-only, weight-independent effect — and in a mature field, attention runs inversely to effect
  size ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]). For anyone still carrying a big rock — smoking,
  heavy alcohol, marked inactivity, a large calorie surplus — meal timing is not where the gains are.

## Mandatory caveats

- **Open loop.** This appraisal grades coherence and fidelity to its sources, never validity — no step
  here checks a recommendation against a realized outcome. A clean audit is not a validated recommendation.
- **Appraise, do not prescribe.** This is a general, population-level reading of the evidence, not medical
  advice; selecting, dosing, and screening for contraindications are prescriber acts requiring information
  this document does not hold.
- **Applied per person.** Adherence and schedule fit are individual — a *worse* protocol you keep beats a
  *better* one you drop, and the weighting of that trade-off is yours.
- **Guidance-null.** The cut *agrees* with the popular framing that a short window helps many people lose
  weight, and *diverges* from the *TRE is a metabolic hack* framing: the calorie-matched evidence says the
  timing itself adds nothing to weight, and the one weight-independent benefit is from eating early — the
  opposite of breakfast-skipping.
- **Evidence is thin and preliminary.** The whole appraisal rests on two small RCTs (n=116 free-living;
  n=8 controlled) plus the weight-loss-outcome frame. No hard outcomes, no long-term data, no trajectory
  measurement anywhere. `confidence: low`.
