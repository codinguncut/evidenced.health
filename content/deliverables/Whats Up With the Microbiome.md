The gut microbiome is invoked for almost everything: immunity, mood, weight, autoimmune disease,
*leaky gut*, *candida*. The honest picture is narrower and more useful than the hype. The microbiome
is genuinely modifiable and genuinely matters in a few specific, well-defined places — and in most of
the places it is marketed for, the evidence stops at a bacterial census that never reaches an outcome
anyone can feel.

## The bottom line

- **The dominant *dietary* lever on your microbiome is fermentable fibre — and that is
  already the fibre recommendation.** (Diet is one key modulator among several the evidence names,
  medication being another; the ranking of fibre as the top *dietary* lever is the wiki's synthesis.) You do not need a supplement aisle to act on it. Eat \~25-30 g/day
  of fibre from a plant-diverse diet ([[Dietary Fibre and Health]]).
- ***It altered the microbiome* is a surrogate, not a health benefit.** Almost every microbiome study
  reports that an intervention changed which bacteria are present. That is a change in a readout, not
  a change in your health, unless someone follows it through to an outcome and shows the chain
  transmits ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).
- **Probiotics work strain-by-strain and indication-by-indication — not as a general *gut health*
  tonic.** There are real, specific wins (below); *take a probiotic for immunity* is not one of them.
- **Prebiotics are mostly fibre by another name**, so their outcome case largely reduces to the fibre
  evidence.
- **Faecal microbiota transplant (FMT — transferring a healthy donor's stool bacteria) is real
  medicine, but so far for one thing:** recurrent *C. difficile* infection. Everything else is
  experimental.
- ***Leaky gut syndrome* and *chronic candida overgrowth*** as general, diet-treatable causes of
  systemic disease fail the basic test — is there a measurable claim on a real outcome? — and must be
  kept separate from the real medical conditions that share their names.
- **The microbiome is discussed far out of proportion to its established effect sizes.** In this field,
  attention is an anti-signal: rank the topic low, and put your effort into the levers (fibre, diet
  quality) that were already ranked without the word *microbiome* attached
  ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]).

Confidence is **medium**: the probiotics-by-indication and FMT-for-*C. difficile* claims rest on a
gold-standard guideline and routine clinical practice, but the broad *microbiome-and-health* story is
mostly observational, and the field's central confound — which way the causal arrow points — is
usually unresolved.

## The one move that decides most of it: composition-shift is a surrogate

This is the load-bearing idea. A microbiome study almost always reports a *composition* change: which
species, how diverse. Valdes 2018 (a BMJ evidence review) states the limit plainly: «In humans,
observational studies can show cross-sectional associations between microbes and health traits but are
limited by the inability to measure causal relations. The strongest level of evidence is obtained from
interventional clinical studies — in particular, randomised controlled trials.»
[@valdes2018]

So low microbial diversity is a *marker* of dysbiosis, not a demonstrated cause of disease, and the
direction of the arrow — does the dysbiosis cause the illness, or does the illness (or the diet behind
it) cause the dysbiosis? — is usually unknown. Treat a composition-shift the way you would treat any
biomarker: it earns belief only when someone follows it through to an outcome a person actually cares
about.

**The template done right is the artificial-sweetener case** ([[Non-Sugar Sweeteners]]). Suez 2022 did
not stop at *sweeteners changed the microbiome*. In a randomised controlled trial of 120 healthy
adults, «each administered NNS distinctly altered stool and oral microbiome ... whereas saccharin and
sucralose significantly impaired glycemic responses» — and then it proved causation by transplanting
the humans' bacteria into germ-free mice, which reproduced the donors' glucose responses.
[@suez2022] That is the full chain:
composition shift, to a measured glycemic outcome, to a causal transplant step. It is also a warning
about how narrow even a good result is — the endpoint is a two-week glycemic surrogate, not diabetes;
the effect is *personalized* (responders and non-responders); and causal-on-the-marker is not
causal-on-the-disease. Hold every microbiome claim to that bar, and most fall well short of it.

## What actually moves the microbiome: diet, and mostly fibre

The dominant *dietary* lever is **fermentable fibre** (diet being one key modulator among several,
alongside medication). Gut bacteria ferment
fibre into short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate), and the diversity marker tracks
fibre intake: long-term weight gain in humans correlates with low microbiota diversity, «and this
association is exacerbated by low dietary fibre intake» (Valdes).
[@valdes2018]

This is why the microbiome story largely *reduces to the fibre story* the evidence already supports.
The impressive fibre numbers — highest-versus-lowest intake tracking roughly **15-30% lower** risk of
all-cause mortality (RR 0.85), coronary heart disease (0.76), type 2 diabetes (0.84) and colorectal
cancer (0.84) — are observational, on self-reported intake, so they carry the healthy-user confound
and dietary measurement error ([[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]).
[@reynolds2019]

The RCT-grade effects (fibre lowers LDL, bodyweight, blood pressure) are real, causal, and small —
Brown's meta-analysis of 67 trials found soluble fibre makes «only a small contribution» to lowering
cholesterol.
[@brown1999]

The practical upshot: reach \~25-30 g/day of fibre from food, expect a genuine supporting lever, and do
not expect a miracle — and note that *feed your microbiome* and *eat enough fibre* are the same
instruction.

## Probiotics: strain- and indication-specific, not a general tonic

The central discipline of the WGO 2023 global guideline is that «the effects of probiotics are
strain- specific and dose-specific».
[@wgo2023] So *take a probiotic for
gut health* is not a claim the evidence supports; the evidence attaches to specific *strain x
indication x dose* combinations. Where it is real:

| Indication | Evidence status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Necrotizing enterocolitis (preterm neonates) | benefit — reduced NEC and reduced death, **NNT 20**, moderate certainty | «not all probiotic preparations tested are effective» — a strain-quality claim, not a class claim |
| Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea | benefit (specific strains) | a real prevention win |
| *C. difficile*-associated diarrhoea (prevention) | benefit (specific strains) | prevention, distinct from FMT *treatment* below |
| Acute infectious diarrhoea | benefit (specific strains) | modest, strain-specific |
| Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) | symptom benefit for *certain* strains | strain-specific |
| Pouchitis | benefit (specific preparations) | — |
| *H. pylori* eradication (adjunct) | reduces therapy side-effects | «quality of the evidence was weak» |

The striking one is the preterm-neonate result: «The number needed to treat to prevent one death from
all causes by treatment with probiotics is 20» — a genuine patient-important outcome — but paired
immediately with the warning that not all preparations work, so it is a claim about specific products,
not about *probiotics*.
[@wgo2023]

Two cautions carry across the whole table. First, pooling trials across different strains without a
shared-mechanism rationale can manufacture or wash out an effect — the *strain* is the exposure, not
the word *probiotic* ([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]). Second, swallowed probiotics are
typically **transient** colonisers: they pass through rather than taking up residence, so any benefit
generally requires continued dosing. Claims for general health, immunity, or mood sit outside the
evidenced indications — that is *insufficient evidence*, not a finding of benefit, and not a
disproof either.

## Prebiotics: mostly fibre by another name

A prebiotic is a substrate selectively used by your gut microbes to confer a health benefit
[@wgo2023] — which is to say, mostly
fermentable fibre. So the prebiotic evidence largely *is* the fibre evidence: the composition and
short-chain-fatty-acid effects are real, the outcome case is strongest exactly where it reduces to the
fibre recommendation, and thinnest for a standalone *prebiotic supplement for general health*. If you
are already eating enough fibre, a separate prebiotic product is buying a marginal, mostly
unevidenced increment.

## FMT: the field's strongest win, and only for one thing so far

Faecal microbiota transplant is a clinical proof that the microbiome is *causal* in humans — you can
transfer a phenotype by transferring bacteria. Valdes: «This procedure is effective in cases of severe
drug refractory *Clostridium difficile* infection and is now routinely used for this purpose around the
world. For other pathologies, faecal transplants are not yet clinical practice but have been explored.»
[@valdes2018]

So FMT-for-recurrent-*C. difficile* is the anchor of what real microbiome evidence looks like: a
defined intervention with a large, replicated effect on a hard outcome. The speculative uses — obesity,
mood, metabolic disease — are explicitly *not yet*. FMT is medicine for one indication, not a wellness
intervention.

## Antibiotics: a real perturbation, but the outcome question is a gap

Antibiotics measurably disturb the microbiome — that much is not in doubt. The decision-relevant
question is different: does the perturbation cause lasting *patient-important* harm, or does the
community mostly recover? The wiki holds no source that resolves this directly, so the honest answer is
**insufficient evidence** on the durable-harm claim ([[Gut Microbiome and Health]]). Two things are
nonetheless settled and worth stating: *don't take antibiotics you don't need* is already sound advice
for antibiotic-resistance reasons entirely separate from the microbiome; and where antibiotics *are*
indicated, the resistance and infection stakes dominate any speculative microbiome cost. The gap here
is the outcome evidence on long-term microbiome disruption — a named hole, not a reason to avoid
necessary treatment.

## *Leaky gut* and *candida*: demarcating real physiology from the syndrome

Two of the most-marketed microbiome ideas need the same functional test — *is there a measurable claim
on a real outcome?* — applied crisply, and kept separate from the real conditions that share their
names ([[Gut Microbiome and Health]]).

### Intestinal permeability is real; *leaky gut syndrome* as a general disease is not established

The gut lining's permeability is a genuine, measurable physiological property, and it is altered in
coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and some infections. The broader claim — that a
general *leaky gut* is a *diet-treatable cause* of systemic and autoimmune disease — is a different,
much larger assertion, and it runs straight into the field's central confound: increased permeability
may be a *consequence* of disease rather than its cause. Treat the physiology as real and the
systemic-syndrome framing as unproven; do not let a marketable label borrow the credibility of the
measurable phenomenon. The primary review that would let the wiki sort these cleanly (Camilleri on
intestinal permeability) is **not yet held** — a named gap.

### Medical candidiasis is real; *chronic candida overgrowth* as a diet-treatable systemic condition is not

*Candida* causes real disease — oral/vaginal thrush, and invasive candidiasis in
immunocompromised people — and those are clinical conditions with real treatments. The popular
*chronic candida overgrowth* narrative — a systemic yeast burden in otherwise healthy people, fixed
by an *anti-candida diet* — makes no falsifiable, quantified claim on a measured human outcome, and
belongs on the *Esoterik* side of the demarcation line ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). The distinction is
not dismissiveness toward the real conditions; it is refusing to let the real conditions launder the
unfalsifiable one.

## Attention is an anti-signal — rank the topic low

The microbiome may be the single most over-discussed topic in nutrition relative to its established,
outcome-level effect sizes. That is itself a signal: in a mature field, the large effects tend to be
settled and boring while the contested small ones generate the content. Rank interventions by
demonstrated outcome effect, never by how much is written about them ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]). Do that here and the ordering is unglamorous: the real levers are diet quality and
fibre (already ranked, without needing the microbiome frame); specific probiotic strains earn their
place only in their specific indications; FMT is medicine for one disease; and the rest is either *not
yet* or unfalsifiable.

## What the evidence cannot yet answer (the gaps)

- **The direction-of-causation problem is pervasive and mostly unresolved.** Does dysbiosis cause
  disease, or do disease and diet cause dysbiosis? This is the microbiome analogue of reverse
  causation, and it undercuts most observational *your microbiome is why you're sick* claims
  ([[Gut Microbiome and Health]]).
- **The landmark primary sources are unheld.** The FMT-versus-*C. difficile* trial (van Nood 2013),
  a Cochrane FMT review, a strain-level probiotics systematic review, and the intestinal-permeability
  review (Camilleri) are named acquisition targets — the strain-and-indication wins and the leaky-gut
  demarcation currently rest on a gold-standard guideline and an evidence review, not on the primary
  trials.
- **Antibiotics-and-long-term-harm** has no held outcome source (above).
- **Birth mode, early-life colonisation, and fermented foods** as microbiome levers are discussed but
  not held here on patient-important outcomes — *not yet*, not *no*.

## How this compares to the popular framing (the guidance null)

The consumer *gut health* market says: your microbiome runs your health, so buy a probiotic, a
prebiotic, a fermented drink, and follow a special diet to fix it. This appraisal (drawn together on
[[Gut Microbiome and Health]]) **diverges** from that framing on the interventions and **converges**
with the little formal guidance that exists on the
mechanism. Where specific bodies have spoken — the WGO on probiotics and prebiotics — the message is
the disciplined one: benefits are strain- and indication-specific, not general. The largest real lever
(fibre and diet diversity) is exactly the boring one the market underplays, and the branded products
are mostly selling a composition-shift that has not been shown to reach an outcome. Agreement with the
convention where the convention is right (fibre matters; specific strains help specific conditions),
divergence where it oversells (the general tonic).

## Caveats

- **Open loop.** This appraisal grades the coherence and source-fidelity of the evidence, never
  validity. Nothing here has been tested against a realized outcome; a clean appraisal is not a
  validated recommendation.
- **Appraise, not prescribe.** This is not a probiotic-brand guide, not a strain-and-dose
  prescription, and not clinical management of *C. difficile*, invasive candidiasis, or IBD — those
  are medical, and out of scope.
- **General, applied per person.** This is a population-level appraisal; the weighting of outcomes and
  the fit to any individual's situation happen case by case.
- **One axis.** The wiki holds a health axis only. It says nothing about the cost, marketing, or
  ecology of the *gut health* industry beyond whether its claims move a health outcome.
