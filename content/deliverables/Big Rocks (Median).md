**This is a portrait of a *type* of person, not a plan for any one reader — and not advice for
everyone.** It applies general evidence to one common situation; people who are leaner or further along
than this need the "it depends" flags below, not this exact list.

## Who this is for

A Western adult in their late 40s who is middling on the usual health measures and drifting the wrong
way: carrying extra fat around the middle, blood pressure and blood sugar creeping up (not yet diabetic),
triglycerides up, a bit of the low-grade inflammation and early fatty liver that travel with all this.
**Drifting, not diseased** — which is exactly why the large, safe levers matter more than fine-tuning.

## Read this first — two ideas that reorder everything

- **Movement and body fat are the headline; the carbs-versus-fat-versus-protein debate is the small
  print.** The biggest *measured* levers are how much you move and how much fat you carry. Which
  macronutrient you cut barely registers by comparison — swapping sugar for other carbs calorie-for-calorie
  does nothing to weight, low-carb beats balanced-carb by about **1 kg** (too little to matter), and cutting
  saturated fat moves heart-attack risk a little and death not at all.
- **The levers are sorted by how *safely* they generalise**, because advice aimed at this drifting
  majority must not backfire on the leaner minority. Each is graded **(a)** helps this person, harms
  no one; **(b)** helps *if* metabolically impaired, needless or mildly counterproductive if not; **(c)**
  small or genuinely contested.

## (a) The big rocks — pull these; they help this person and harm no one

**1. Move — aerobic activity is the largest well-measured lever.**

- Regular activity is tied to a **66% lower death rate** (hazard ratio 0.34; high confidence — this is
  as solid as this field gets) ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]).
- Most of that benefit arrives by about **24 minutes a day** of brisk activity; past that the curve
  flattens. You do not need to be an athlete.
- Sitting less is a *separate* lever: risk climbs above roughly **6-8 hours a day** of total sitting
  (about 3-4 hours of TV).

**2. Add strength training — mainly to keep muscle and function, and for a metabolic bonus.**

- It earns its spot on *proven* grounds: trials show it preserves muscle while you lose fat
  ([[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]) — you want to shed the belly fat, not the
  muscle — and it builds strength. And it harms no one.
- **A metabolic bonus that matters here:** muscle is the body's main sink for blood sugar, so building it
  improves glucose tolerance — a real plus for this sugar-drifting person, and why strength training tracks
  with less diabetes ([[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]).
- Its own link to living longer (about 15% lower death rate alone; ~40% paired with aerobic) is real but
  *weakly evidenced* — a bonus, not the reason to do it. A couple of sessions a week captures the benefit;
  no need to chase volume.

**3. Lose the fat around the middle (mostly by eating less) — the dominant metabolic lever.**

It works on the blood pressure, the blood sugar, the fatty liver, and the general drift all at once. The
honest version:

- It did **not** cut heart attacks and strokes in the big trial (and a 54-trial review agrees) — so do
  not sell it as heart-attack insurance ([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]).
- It **does** lower overall death rates (about **6 fewer deaths per 1,000**), by a non-heart route — so
  it is still worth doing for the liver, blood sugar, blood pressure, function, and that real survival edge.
- **Targets worth naming:** losing **5%** of body weight clears fat from the liver; **7-10%** cools the
  liver inflammation behind long-term damage ([[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]).
- **How matters less than sticking with it.** The one diet with a clear thumbs-up is defined by calories,
  not macros — a very-low-calorie total meal-replacement (~800-1,200 kcal/day) for up to 12 weeks
  ([[Diets for Weight Loss - What NICE Recommends]]).

**4. Eat enough protein (about 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day).** This protects muscle while you are
in a calorie deficit and supports the strength work. The benefit *plateaus* near 1.6 g/kg — more is not
better ([[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]).

**5. Sleep about 7-8 hours.** Avoid chronically short nights. More sleep is not a lever, and the higher
risk seen in long sleepers is a *sign* of underlying illness, not a reason to sleep less
([[Sleep Duration and Mortality]]).

## (b) Depends on your metabolism — not for everyone

**Cutting carbohydrates — only if blood sugar is already drifting.**

- In the insulin-resistant or pre-diabetic, it lowers **HbA1c** (a 3-month blood-sugar average) by about
  **0.47%** and can flip early type-2 diabetes into remission (you would need to treat about **3 people**
  for one to benefit, at 6 months) ([[Carbohydrate Restriction and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]]).
- **Two catches:** the effect fades by 12 months, and it nudges LDL cholesterol up (~0.14 mmol/L). For
  the metabolically *healthier* version of this person it is needless, and that LDL rise is a small
  net-negative — the clearest "helps some, could mildly harm others" call.
- For weight alone, low-carb beats balanced-carb by only ~1 kg — not worth arguing over
  ([[Low-Carbohydrate vs Balanced-Carbohydrate Diets]]).

## (c) Small, over-discussed, or genuinely contested — do not lead with these

- **Saturated fat → unsaturated:** a small effect on heart attacks/strokes (about **15 fewer per 1,000**
  at high risk, less here) and **none on death** ([[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]]).
  - The mechanism is real, though: cutting saturated fat lowers **apoB** — a count of the cholesterol
    particles that actively cause artery disease, and they add up over a lifetime
    ([[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]). So a *sustained* small cut is worth more than a short trial
    shows. For this metabolically-impaired person, watch **non-HDL cholesterol** (a cheap apoB stand-in),
    not LDL alone.
- **Free sugars:** the lever is *calories*, not sugar as such — a calorie-for-calorie swap does nothing
  to weight ([[Free Sugars Intake]]).
- **Alcohol:** moderate drinking is **not** protective — the famous "a glass is good for you" curve is a
  statistical artefact — so there is nothing to chase upward, and for this fatty-liver-prone person, less
  is better ([[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]).
- **Salt:** a small blood-pressure lever, worth *relatively* more here (blood pressure already creeping):
  about **4-6 mmHg** in people like this versus ~1.4 in the normal-pressure ([[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]). Drugs that lower blood pressure do prevent heart attacks even in healthy people
  ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]) — but at borderline numbers the lifestyle route
  (weight, salt, activity) is the move, not a pill.
- **Red / processed meat:** genuinely *contested* — one expert body says cut it, another says current
  intake is defensible, on the *same* evidence. The fight is about the standard of proof and whose values
  set the line, not whether meat causes cancer. Shown as an open question, not a verdict
  ([[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]]).

## Why the diet-composition debate is second-order for this person

This person is higher-risk than a lean adult but **still not high-risk**, so the levers mostly act on
metabolism, liver, and function rather than on preventing heart attacks. The heart-attack payoff of any
single cardiometabolic tweak is modest at this risk level — blood-pressure lowering is the partial
exception ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]). The reason is
simple: benefit scales with your starting risk, so a big *percentage* cut on a modest risk is a small
*real-world* gain ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).

## What we cannot yet say (stated plainly, and routed for later)

- **Pulses and plant-protein quality:** the evidence base here is nearly empty — the top gap to fill for
  a nutrition guide.
- **The "could mildly harm the healthier minority" calls are reasoned, not measured** — they rest on
  mechanism and on where the evidence differs by metabolic state, not on trials that quantified the harm.
- **No population statistics** — this person is a deliberate composite, not a survey figure.
- **No proven targets for waist size or resting heart rate** — treat them as progress markers that move
  the right way with the levers above, not as pass/fail cutoffs.

## The honest limits

- **This grades the evidence, not the outcome.** It scores what the studies show and how faithfully we
  read them — never whether acting on it actually made someone healthier.
- **It appraises, it does not prescribe.** Cutting carbohydrates in particular needs medication
  adjustments only a clinician can make.
- **A type, not a person.** Which outcomes matter to *you*, and what you can sustain, are yours to weigh —
  the leaner and the more-impaired need the "it depends" flags, not this exact order.
