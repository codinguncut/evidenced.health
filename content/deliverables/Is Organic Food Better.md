**A population-level read on the *health* question only — not a shopping list, and not a verdict on the
environment or animal welfare.** Up front, the honest caveat: the wiki does not yet hold the primary
sources on organic-vs-conventional (the nutrient meta-analyses, the residue and outcome studies), so this
is the **frame** — how to think about it, and what the answer will and won't be — with the effect sizes
flagged as *pending*. The frame turns out to do most of the work.

## The one idea that reorganises the whole question

**"Organic" is a label about how food is *produced*, not a property of the food itself — and production
tells you nothing about health on its own.** "Natural is healthier" is exactly as unwarranted as its
mirror, "processed is harmful": both are stories about origin, and health claims need outcome evidence,
not origin ([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]). So the useful move is not to ask "is organic
better?" but to break the label into the specific things it actually changes, and check each against
evidence.

## Break "organic" into what it actually changes

The certification is a *bundle* — no synthetic pesticides, no synthetic fertiliser, no routine
antibiotics, welfare rules, and *often but not always* outdoor/pasture access. Those change different
things, with different evidence and different sizes:

- **Pesticide residues — genuinely lower on organic.** This is the most solid difference. But the
  decision question is whether it reaches a *health* outcome: residues on conventional produce typically
  sit well below regulatory limits, so a *measurable* difference in residue is not the same as a
  *health* difference. Detecting an exposure isn't evidence of harm from it ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).
  *(Effect on outcomes: not held — pending.)*
- **Nutrient content — small, real, and mixed.** The broad literature finds organic crops modestly higher
  in some antioxidants and lower in the toxic metal cadmium; conventional is sometimes higher in others.
  The magnitude is the whole point, and it is small against the size of your *total* diet. *(Numbers: not
  held — pending.)*
- **Actual health outcomes — the weakest link, and it will stay weak.** You cannot blind or randomise a
  lifetime of organic eating, so there are essentially no trials, and the observational studies are
  **heavily confounded**: organic buyers are, on average, wealthier, leaner, more active, and less likely
  to smoke. Any "organic eaters are healthier" finding is mostly a portrait of who buys organic, not of
  the food (the classic healthy-user problem; and diet is notoriously hard to measure —
  [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]). *(Outcome attribution: not cleanly possible — expect
  "can't say," not a benefit.)*

## The cattle case — why the label is often the wrong variable

Your worked example makes the point cleanly. Compare an **organic** cow fed organic corn in a barn with a
**conventional** cow raised on pasture where some synthetic fertiliser was used on the grass. On the
nutrients that actually differ in milk and meat — omega-3 fats, CLA, fat-soluble vitamins — the driver is
**grass versus grain**, i.e. *what the animal ate and how it lived*, not the organic certificate. The
fertiliser on the grass barely transmits to the animal product. So the **pasture** cow likely wins on
those nutrients *regardless of organic status* — and "organic" was simply the wrong thing to optimise for.
The lesson generalises: **specify the exposure that actually matters (feed, husbandry, freshness), not the
word on the label** ([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]).

## Where this lands

- On **health**, at population level, the honest expectation is a **small, low-certainty** benefit at
  best — lower pesticide exposure of unproven health consequence, and minor nutrient differences swamped
  by everything else in your diet. Ranked against the big levers (activity, body fat, not smoking), it is
  a footnote.
- The **more useful questions hide underneath the label** — pasture vs grain-fed for animal products,
  freshness and variety for produce — and those you can act on without buying the certificate.
- **Health is not the only reason to buy organic, and the others are legitimate.** Reducing pesticide use
  in farming, soil and water impact, and animal welfare are real considerations — this appraisal simply
  doesn't weigh them (it has no environmental or welfare data), and they are yours to weigh. It neither
  smuggles them in as a health benefit nor argues against them.

## What we cannot yet say (routed for later — the deliverable's main product)

The whole evidence base needs acquiring; each is a named gap, not a guessed number:

- **The crop-nutrient comparison** (antioxidants up, cadmium and residues down) — to put real sizes on
  "small."
- **The safety/nutrition review** — to state plainly how little the outcome evidence supports.
- **The animal-product fatty-acid comparison** — to quantify the cattle case and confirm pasture, not the
  label, is the lever.
- **An organic-eaters health cohort** — to *demonstrate* the healthy-user confound, not to claim an
  effect from it.

## The honest limits

- **This grades the evidence, not the outcome** — and here it mostly grades what the evidence *cannot*
  establish. Coherence, not validity.
- **It appraises, it does not prescribe** — no shopping or budgeting rule.
- **Health axis only.** Environmental and welfare reasons to choose organic are real and out of scope
  here; they are the buyer's to weigh.
- **Frame now, numbers later** — the directional statements above are the general read of the field,
  pending the wiki actually holding the sources to attach effect sizes and certainty.
