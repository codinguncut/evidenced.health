Randomised trials and cohort studies bear on one decision question: does about one drink a day,
against none, raise blood pressure? They measure different quantities. Roerecke's trial meta-analysis
finds no significant BP change in its lowest band (two drinks a day or fewer; weeks to months, by
weight mostly one 4-week crossover in women) and reads it as an apparent threshold. Di Federico's
cohort meta-analysis finds BP *change* over 4-12 years higher at 12 g/day than with none (SBP +1.25,
0.49 to 2.01), a difference its authors call «rather small» at one serving a day yet read as making
«any alcohol» a risk factor for high SBP, with no threshold shown over 0 to \~55 g/day. Matched on quantity they do
not clash. One is a weeks-scale level response, the other a years-scale divergence in BP trajectory by
baseline intake, and both can hold at once (not-joined check ii). A numeric clash appears only if the
cohort difference is read as a level effect that stopping would reverse within weeks, which neither
source shows. Even then it appears only on overall DBP; in women only (the trials have no men-only <=2
stratum), the intervals touch. Filed as a
**distinction** with a conditional clash and a type-G gap: held evidence cannot say whether the cohort
gradient is causal, or whether a real low-dose effect needs years to show. Synthesis mode:
configurative (the two designs share no common metric to pool).

## View A: the randomised low-dose band shows no BP effect (Roerecke 2017)

Roerecke pooled 36 alcohol-reduction trials and stratified them by baseline intake. In the band of
two drinks a day or fewer, 5 trials gave SBP -0.18 (-1.02 to 0.66) and DBP +0.61 (-0.04 to 1.26) mmHg;
the alcohol arm averaged 15 g/day, and at this level alcohol «was mostly compared with abstinence or
near abstinence». [@roerecke2017alcohol]

> «A reduction in alcohol consumption reduced blood pressure in a dose-dependent manner with an apparent threshold eﬀ ect at two drinks per day. People drinking two drinks or fewer per day did not have a signiﬁ cant reduction in blood pressure when they reduced their alcohol consumption to near abstinence, suggesting that this amount of alcohol intake does not increase blood pressure.»

[@roerecke2017alcohol]

The next sentence keeps the dose-response above the band: «However, the more people drink beyond this
level, the higher the subsequent reduction in blood pressure.» The paper's conclusion softens the
threshold to «a possible threshold eﬀ ect». [@roerecke2017alcohol]

**What the five trials were.** Three gave abstainers or near-abstainers wine against (near)
abstinence, water or non-alcoholic beer: Cordain (10 weeks, overweight women), Gepner 2015 (104 weeks,
n=224, type 2 diabetes) and Shai (12 weeks, type 2 diabetes). Droste gave carotid-plaque outpatients
red wine against abstinence (20 weeks). Mori 2015 gave regular drinkers red wine against dealcoholised
wine (4 weeks). Three are parallel-group, two crossover. By weight the band is mostly Mori 2015
(about 8 g/day), which carries 52% of the SBP and 69% of the DBP pooled weight; Cordain adds 39% of the
SBP weight but only 10% of DBP, level with Droste (9%) and Gepner (11%). The two type 2 diabetes trials
carry 3% (SBP) and 12% (DBP).
[@roerecke2017alcohol]

## View B: the cohort SBP curve shows no threshold over 0 to \~55 g/day (Di Federico 2023)

Di Federico pooled 7 cohorts (19,548 adults; 3 North American, 4 Japanese or Korean; median follow-up
5.3 y, range 4-12), relating *baseline* intake to BP *change* over follow-up in a 1-stage spline model.
SBP was 1.25 (0.49 to 2.01) and DBP 1.14 (0.60 to 1.68) mmHg higher at 12 g/day than with «no alcohol
consumption», rising to SBP 4.90 (3.71 to 6.08) at 48 g/day. The plotted curves run 0 to \~55 g/day
(figure read); no formal non-linearity test or certainty rating is reported
`[searched: linearity / P for / GRADE / certainty across chunks 01-02 (main text; supplement read at prep but not chunked), srcgrep 0 / 0 / ref-list only / «uncertainty» only]`.
[@difederico2023alcbp]

The authors first grant that the change at one serving a day was «rather small», then draw the
no-threshold conclusion:

> «The main finding of our meta-analysis was that usual alcohol consumption at baseline was positively associated with change in SBP over time in both men and women, with a substantially linear pattern, although the amount of change associated with a limited amount of alcohol intake (such as one serving/day) was rather small. Our findings suggest that in longitudinal analysis there is no threshold below which no association exists between alcohol consumption and a higher SBP. Consequently, consumption of any alcohol should be considered a risk factor for high SBP, with important preventive and therapeutic implications.»

[@difederico2023alcbp]

In the abstract they limit the linear, no-threshold reading to SBP: «Our results suggest the
association between alcohol consumption and SBP is direct and linear with no evidence of a threshold
for the association, while for DBP the association is modified by sex and geographic location.» The
Discussion's closing paragraph states it for BP without the split: «Overall, our review indicates that
the relationship between alcohol intake and BP is almost linear with no evidence of a threshold for the
association.»
[@difederico2023alcbp]

The paper sets itself against the trial band. Its introduction, citing Roerecke, says «there is
uncertainty regarding the relationship between alcohol consumption and BP at or below an intake of 2
standard alcoholic drinks per day. Whether this results from a lack of statistical power in studies of
low levels of alcohol intake or reflects biological differences at lower compared with higher intakes
of alcohol is unclear.» [@difederico2023alcbp]
In women (2 cohorts) and North America (3), the 12 g/day SBP intervals include 0.
[@difederico2023alcbp]

## Not-joined checks: a distinction, with a conditional clash

- **(i) Same observable, differing only in words?** No. The trial band predicts no BP change from
  \~15 g/day over weeks; the cohort curve predicts about 1 mmHg more BP change over years at 12 g/day.
- **(ii) Different scope, consistent once matched?** Yes. The two differ in horizon (4-104 weeks vs
  4-12 years), estimand (BP level after a change in intake vs difference in BP change by baseline
  intake), population (mostly women and people with diabetes vs mostly men) and design. Both findings
  can hold at once: no detectable short-run level response at \~1 drink/day, and a slow divergence in
  BP over years. A numeric clash appears only if the cohort's difference in change is read as a level
  effect that stopping reverses within weeks. Neither source shows that (Di Federico infers only «the
  potential for bigger reductions in BP with prolonged reduction or abstinence from alcohol», a
  reversal over a prolonged horizon, not within weeks), and the change-score design subtracts a steady
  level effect out. Women only (Roerecke has no men-only <=2 trials), even that reading leaves the DBP
  intervals touching. The decision does not split either: under both readings the harm-side default holds and
  the BP payment runs from zero to small (Decision relevance below).
- **(iii) Part of a multi-position author?** No, but both positions are hedged. Roerecke's whole
  Discussion and conclusion were read: an «apparent», then «possible», threshold, and «Because only
  three trials reported results for women,28,50,53 we have less conﬁ dence in the pooled eﬀ ect
  estimates». Di Federico's whole Introduction and Discussion were read: one serving/day «rather
  small», and it names horizon as its advantage over the trial meta-analyses: «The trials considered
  were however generally of short duration (4--8 weeks). In contrast, follow-up in the cohort studies
  included in our meta-analysis ranged from 4 to 12 years, a major strength compared the meta-analyses
  based on experimental findings.» (The 4-8 weeks follows its account of Xin 2001; Roerecke's <=2 band
  ran 4-104 weeks.)

## Synthesis: neither the threshold nor the no-threshold is a shape the data can fix

The reflex reading is that the randomised evidence wins. It does not settle this question, because
neither side measures the shape directly. Roerecke's threshold is a comparison *between* trials: bands
are built from different trials (the only trial straddling the <=2 boundary is Mori 2015, whose low- and
high-volume arms sit in the <=2 and 3-drink bands; its within-trial contrast, SBP -0.40 at \~8 g/day vs
-2.00 at \~26 g/day and DBP +0.30 (-0.48 to 1.08) vs -1.20 (-1.98 to -0.42), leans toward a threshold
on DBP point estimates, but both intervals admit a straight line), and baseline intake explains most of
the between-study variance («Alcohol intake at baseline explained 75·4% of the between-study variance
in systolic blood pressure»), while the lowest band also cut the least alcohol (-15 g/day vs -30, -40
and -32 in the higher bands). Di Federico's no-threshold is a spline pinned at a referent whose make-up
(never vs former drinkers) is not reported in the main text, with no formal non-linearity test
`[searched: former / never / linearity across Di Federico chunks 01-02, srcgrep 0 / 0 / 0]`.
[@roerecke2017alcohol]
[inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol; @difederico2023alcbp]

Four readings reconcile the cohort gradient with the trial null, one with no real low-dose effect (a)
and three with one (b-d), and the held evidence does not pick among them.

**(a) The cohort gradient is confounded or referent-biased.** Every point on the curve, 48 g/day
included, is a contrast against «no alcohol consumption»; a 3-knot spline pinned at zero cannot
isolate a step there, so a contaminated referent could tilt the whole curve. The authors write that
«the nonexperimental design of the studies did not allow us to exclude the possibility of residual
confounding, although a relevant bias due to key unmeasured confounders did not appear to be likely»,
and credit healthy-adult longitudinal data with «reducing the risk of reverse causation»; nothing in the paper
tests unmeasured confounding (its sensitivity analyses only drop studies not adjusted for smoking or BMI).

**(b) The effect needs years.** Cohort follow-up was 4-12 years, trials weeks to months. The support
is thin on both sides: Di Federico finds only a «slightly positive association of duration of
follow-up», steeper for SBP than DBP, and Roerecke reports trial length «associated with the eﬀ ect
size» while its dose-response held «independent of the length of the trial». Within the trial band the
&gt;=10-week stratum does not trend toward a fall: DBP +1.38 (0.00 to 2.77), SBP -0.36 (-3.06 to 2.35);
only the one 2-year trial's SBP point estimate leans that way (Gepner 2015, -4.67, -10.89 to 1.55), and
whether that is a 2-year reading is not shown, since Roerecke preferred shorter time periods. In the
three trials with repeated measurements, one of them (Droste) in this band, Roerecke found «almost no
diﬀ erence from the ﬁ rst measurement to the last, which suggests that the eﬀ ect of a reduction in
alcohol consumption on blood pressure is sustained when lower alcohol consumption is sustained».

**(c) The trial band does not transport.** By trial count it mostly starts abstainers drinking, two
trials in type 2 diabetes. By weight it is mostly a 4-week crossover in 24 healthy premenopausal women
who already drank, given about 8 g/day against dealcoholised wine. So the pooled number is
short-horizon and mostly female (its weight-averaged contrast, about 11-13 g/day, matches the cohorts'
12 g/day, so dose is not the gap), while the cohorts are mostly men followed 4-12 years. A light
drinker cutting down for longer than 4 weeks is in neither.


**(d) Power.** On SBP the trial interval admits a fall of \~1 mmHg, so a small real effect could hide
in 5 trials, a possibility Di Federico's introduction raises alongside biological differences at low
intakes. On the overall pooled DBP the trials exclude a fall of that size; women only, the intervals
touch at \~0.7 mmHg, so power is not ruled out there.

[@difederico2023alcbp]
[@roerecke2017alcohol]
[inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol; @difederico2023alcbp]

The no-threshold trial result Di Federico calls «generally consistent» with its own, Xin 2001, adds
little: by Di Federico's account it «relied on a meta-regression linear model», which cannot show a
threshold, and it shares an author (Whelton). Xin is not held.
[@difederico2023alcbp]

## What would join the issue (type-G)

The two bodies would meet on one quantity only in a design that measures BP as habitual light
drinkers change intake, over the cohorts' horizon. Either of two would do:

- a randomised trial in **habitual** drinkers of two drinks a day or fewer who cut down, powered for
  \~1 mmHg SBP and run for months to years;
- a cohort analysis of within-person *changes* in intake against *changes* in BP.

Di Federico cites two trajectory cohorts of the second kind: Qiu 2022 (China Health and Nutrition
Survey) — would show whether BP change
tracks intake change at low doses; and Jung 2021 (Korean MRCohort, one of the 7 pooled cohorts) — the same, in non-hypertensive
adults. Neither is held. If such data showed BP falling when light drinkers cut down, the cohort
reading would gain a causal leg; if not, the trial null would extend past 4 weeks.
[@difederico2023alcbp]


## Decision relevance

For a light drinker (about one drink a day) the expected BP gain from stopping is **between zero and
about 1.25 mmHg SBP** (cohort interval 0.5 to 2): zero on the trial reading, \~1.25 mmHg on the cohort
reading if it is causal and reverses on cutting. On this wiki's arithmetic, carrying BPLTTC's
drug-trial hazard ratio (\~0.91 per 5 mmHg of pharmacological lowering,
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]) over to an alcohol-associated BP difference,
which neither source models, that is roughly a 2% relative reduction in major CV events (about 1-4%
across the cohort interval), modelled, not measured. The harm-side default holds either way, every
reduction pays, but through BP the payment is small at best. Which reading holds changes the *reason* to
cut light drinking more than the decision: the cancer curve rises from zero with no protective arm
([[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]), and the mortality J-curve's protective arm is largely
artifact ([[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]). Above two drinks a day there is no
disagreement: both bodies show BP rising with intake.
[inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol; @difederico2023alcbp]
