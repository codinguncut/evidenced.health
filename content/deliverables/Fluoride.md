## At fluoridation levels the decay benefit is small and, for most people, the serious harms are unresolved

Fluoride goes into drinking water and toothpaste to prevent tooth decay. At higher doses it is also
linked to lower IQ in children and to bone fractures. **This appraisal answers each outcome on its own
evidence and at its own dose**, because for most people the benefit and the serious harm signals sit
at different exposures ([[Fluoride and Health]]).

**At the 0.7 mg/L used in US water fluoridation, the decay benefit for children is small and
uncertain.** Fluoridated tap water may still prevent a little decay in baby teeth, but the estimate
includes no benefit at all, and studies begun after 1975, in the era of fluoride toothpaste, find
smaller effects than the older ones
[@iheozor2024fluoridation]. **Mild mottling of
the enamel, dental fluorosis, is common at the same dose.** It is cosmetic. The review rates its
evidence low certainty, and its authors also call the link widely accepted
([[Fluoride and Caries Prevention]]).

**Lower child IQ is associated with fluoride at moderate confidence where drinking water exceeds about
1.5 mg/L**, the WHO guideline value. At fluoridation levels the link is unresolved for most people: not
shown, and not shown to be absent. **Below 1.5 mg/L the signal is fragile and conditional:** some
estimates point toward lower IQ, others are null, and whether the best studies keep the link depends on
the grading tool ([[Fluoride and Child IQ]]). The US National Toxicology Program, which
reached the moderate-confidence finding, adds that it may also apply to some people on fluoridated water whose
fluoride from other sources is high
[@ntp2024fluoride]. For fractures at all sites, a
pooled dose-response curve shows no signal at fluoridation levels ([[Fluoride and Bone Health]]). The
IQ and fracture findings come from observational studies: they are associations, not demonstrated
causes.

**Three groups on fluoridated water may sit closer to the harm findings.** Babies fed formula made up
with fluoridated water can take in more fluoride than the water level suggests, NTP's named example
[@ntp2024fluoride]. The IQ studies
nearest fluoridation levels followed exposure in pregnancy and infancy, so pregnant women are a named
case too; at 0.7 mg/L that is a reason for concern, not an established effect
([[Fluoride and Child IQ]]). Women over 50 show a rise in fragility fractures starting
inside the range US fluoridation has used, in an additional analysis of a limited number of studies [@mazzoli2025fluoride]. This appraisal rates that signal
low certainty, partly because one research group produced it
[inferred from @mazzoli2025fluoride].

**What decides your case is the fluoride level of your water plus your other fluoride intake**, not
the word *fluoride*. **Overall confidence: low.** All of the decay studies are non-randomised, the IQ
finding reaches moderate confidence only above 1.5 mg/L, and the bone findings rest on one
dose-response analysis ([[Fluoride and Health]]).

## Fluoridated water still prevents some tooth decay, but far less than it did before fluoride toothpaste

**The best current estimate of the decay benefit is small, and it comes from studies of children
only**, in the 2024 Cochrane review of community water fluoridation (CWF, the deliberate addition of fluoride to a public supply, US target
0.7 mg/L). All 157 studies in the review are non-randomised, because the authors judge randomised trials
logistically unfeasible here, so every estimate starts from a low certainty floor
[@iheozor2024fluoridation].

### In the toothpaste era, the benefit is about a quarter of a tooth

The review splits its studies at about 1975, when fluoride toothpaste came into wide use
[@iheozor2024fluoridation]. Decay is counted as
dmf (decayed, missing or filled baby teeth) or DMFT for permanent teeth. **For children whose water was
fluoridated after 1975, CWF may lead to a slightly larger fall in dmf: mean difference 0.24 (95% CI −0.03
to 0.52; 2 studies, 2,908 children; low certainty).** The authors put this at about a quarter of a tooth
per child, and note that the interval includes both benefit and no benefit. Permanent teeth show a
similar point estimate with less certainty: DMFT mean difference 0.27 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.66; 4 studies,
2,856 children; very low certainty).

Studies that started fluoridation before 1975 found a much larger effect: dmf mean difference 2.10 (95%
CI 1.71 to 2.49; 5 studies, 5,709 children). The review rates it very low certainty and downgrades it
for indirectness, because it could not tell whether those results apply to current times
[@iheozor2024fluoridation]. Its point estimate is
roughly nine times the contemporary one.

### The benefit shrank as other fluoride sources spread

The review's explanation is that toothpaste, fluoride varnish and dental sealants spread from the late
1970s, so «the benefit of water fluoridation may be diluted»
[@iheozor2024fluoridation]. **The fair comparison
for CWF today is a population that already brushes with fluoride toothpaste, not one with no fluoride at
all**, and against that comparison the extra gain is small. No held source sizes the toothpaste benefit
itself ([[Fluoride and Caries Prevention]]).

The authors keep the older studies in play for one setting: «they may still be relevant to countries in
which tooth decay is very high and people don't have easy access to fluoride toothpaste and other
prevention strategies» [@iheozor2024fluoridation].
**The same relative effect prevents more decay in children at higher baseline risk**, so
high-caries-risk children with poor access to toothpaste likely gain the most; no held study sizes it

([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).

### What the review cannot say

The review found no eligible studies of decay in adults, so **whether adults benefit is unknown, not
shown to be zero.** Stopping fluoridation is in the same state: one study after 1975 looked at it, and
the evidence was too uncertain to tell
[@iheozor2024fluoridation]. The review also
estimates no caries dose-response above 0.7 mg/L, so whether more fluoride would add more benefit cannot
be answered from it ([[Fluoride and Health]], [[The Insufficient-Evidence Statement]]).

## Mild dental fluorosis is common at the fluoridation dose

Fluoridated water carries one harm at the 0.7 mg/L dose itself: dental fluorosis, a change in tooth
enamel laid down while a child's teeth form, before they erupt. Severe fluorosis discolours or pits the
teeth; EPA's non-enforceable 2.0 mg/L standard exists to prevent that
[@ntp2024fluoride].

**At 0.7 ppm (the same as 0.7 mg/L), about 12% of people have fluorosis of aesthetic concern** (95% CI
8% to 17%; 40 studies, 59,630 participants), **and about 40% have fluorosis of any level** (95% CI 35%
to 44%; 90 studies, 180,530 participants). These are modelled prevalences at that dose. The review's
summary table lists no comparison group, so **neither figure is an excess over unfluoridated water**.
The dose-response is the cleaner signal: **the odds of aesthetic-concern fluorosis rise 2.90-fold per
1 ppm** (95% CI 2.05 to 4.10)
[@iheozor2024fluoridation], a single linear
slope fitted across 40 studies whose «reported fluoride exposure ranged from 0 to 4.9 ppm with a mean
(SD) of 0.80 (0.90) ppm» [@iheozor2024fluoridation].
It says nothing beyond 4.9 ppm.

The review holds two readings side by side. Its earlier version rated the evidence low certainty, and
the 2024 update did not re-search it, explaining: «Because the association of fluoridated water with
dental fluorosis is widely accepted, we did not update the evidence on this occasion.»
[@iheozor2024fluoridation] So the GRADE rating
is low, while the authors treat the link as accepted and the dose-response interval excludes 1.
Fluorosis is a cosmetic outcome; it sits below child IQ and fracture in weight
([[Fluoride and Health]]).

Skeletal fluorosis, a painful joint condition distinct from fracture, is held only as the purpose of
EPA's enforceable 4.0 mg/L limit [@ntp2024fluoride];
no held source gives a dose-response for it.

## Lower child IQ is linked to fluoride above 1.5 mg/L; below that the evidence is unsettled

### The US review finds a moderate-confidence link at higher exposure

**The US National Toxicology Program (NTP) concluded, with moderate confidence, that higher fluoride
exposure is associated with lower IQ in children**, and tied that conclusion to an exposure level:
higher exposures, such as drinking water above the WHO guideline of 1.5 mg/L, «are consistently
associated with lower IQ in children»
[@ntp2024fluoride]. Using OHAT, its own appraisal
method, NTP held 72 IQ studies, of which 19 were rated low risk of bias (low-RoB)
[@ntp2024fluoride]. Those 19 covered 15 study
populations in 5 countries and more than 7,000 children, and 18 of them found an inverse association
[@ntp2024fluoride].

NTP's companion meta-analysis puts a size on it. Across 13 studies with individual-level data (4,475
children), **each 1 mg/L rise in urinary fluoride, a biomarker of total intake, went with 1.63 fewer IQ
points (95% CI −2.33 to −0.93)**. In the 11 low-RoB studies among them the drop was 1.14 points (95% CI
−1.68 to −0.61). The authors note that a 5-point fall in a population's average IQ would nearly double
the number of people classed as intellectually disabled
[@taylor2025fluoride]. That slope spans the whole pooled urinary
range; it is not an estimate for water at 1.5 or 0.7 mg/L, and the 5-point figure describes a
population shift, not a predicted effect at fluoridation levels.

**NTP and the meta-analysis count as one line of evidence, not two:** NTP's own authors ran the
companion analysis [@ntp2024fluoride]
[@taylor2025fluoride]. A third meta-analysis, by Veneri and
colleagues in Modena, comes from a separate team, but its 33 studies sit almost entirely inside NTP's
set, so agreement on direction is partly built in
[inferred from @ntp2024fluoride; @taylor2025fluoride; @veneri2023fluoride].

### 1.5 mg/L marks where the evidence is dense, not a safe threshold

**NTP chose the WHO 1.5 mg/L guideline as its referent for *higher* exposure** because «no alternative
safety guidelines for total fluoride exposure exist»
[@ntp2024fluoride]. The number shows where most of the
confident studies sampled their populations; no study located a point below which risk stops
. Below it, NTP finds associations
«remain unclear» [@ntp2024fluoride], and it does not
address fluoride added at 0.7 mg/L as the only exposure
[@ntp2024fluoride]. **Here *unclear* means
insufficient evidence, not a shown absence of harm** ([[The Insufficient-Evidence Statement]]).

### Below 1.5 mg/L some estimates point toward lower IQ and others are null

With exposure measured as water fluoride, Taylor's dose-response across all studies is inverse below 4
and below 2 mg/L but «null at less than 1.5 mg/L». In the low-RoB studies, the sub-1.5 water estimate
stays inverse in direction but is not statistically significant. Measured in urine, the association
stays inverse and significant below 1.5 mg/L, in all studies and in the low-RoB set. These sub-1.5
estimates are changes in a standardized mean difference, so they give a direction, not a size in IQ
points.

Taylor puts the water null down to thin data and small exposure contrasts, and notes that water
levels likely underestimate total exposure, which urine captures better. Urine has its own noise: spot
samples are more affected by timing and dilution than 24-hour samples, though dilution-adjusted spot
samples correlate with 24-hour ones [@taylor2025fluoride]. The
persisting urinary signal rests on that noisier measure ([[Fluoride and Child IQ]]).

Veneri's curve, in IQ points, gives «A weaker and substantially linear decrease of −2.15 (95% CI −4.48;
0.18) IQ points with increasing urinary fluoride emerged above 0.28 mg/L (approximately reflecting a
water fluoride content of 0.7 mg/L).»
[@veneri2023fluoride] **The decline starts near the
fluoridation dose, but its interval includes no effect.** Measurement error would also push a real
low-dose gradient toward zero: many studies assign an area-level water value to each child, and water
misses toothpaste, tea and formula
.

### Whether the best studies keep the link depends on the grading tool

**Under NTP's OHAT grading, the low-RoB studies keep the inverse association.** Veneri's team graded a
largely overlapping set with ROBINS-E (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Exposure)
[@veneri2023fluoride], which in these studies rates
confounding more strictly
[inferred from @ntp2024fluoride; @veneri2023fluoride].

Veneri's abstract reports both directions: «The inverse association between fluoride exposure and IQ
was particularly strong in the studies at high risk of bias, while no adverse effect emerged in the
only study judged at low risk of bias. Overall, most studies suggested an adverse effect of fluoride
exposure on children’s IQ, starting at low levels of exposure.»
[@veneri2023fluoride] The association weakens as
study quality rises, and «most studies» covers the whole set, not the low-dose estimates alone
[inferred from @veneri2023fluoride].

The low-RoB study is cross-sectional and gave +1.11 IQ points (95% CI −0.67 to 2.89); the three cohort
studies pooled gave −0.74 (95% CI −2.90 to 1.42)
[@veneri2023fluoride]. **Neither is a confident
null:** both intervals include a meaningful IQ loss, and the low-RoB result rests on one study
[inferred from @veneri2023fluoride].
Veneri adds that «a major role of residual confounding could not be ruled out»
[@veneri2023fluoride].

The tools also split on single studies: Veneri rates the Bashash analysis of Mexico's ELEMENT cohort high
risk of bias [@veneri2023fluoride], while NTP counts
it among cohorts that «reflect a low risk for bias»
[@ntp2024fluoride]. No held source settles which tool
suits this literature better
[inferred from @ntp2024fluoride; @veneri2023fluoride].

### Near fluoridation levels the cohort results are mixed

The studies nearest 0.7 mg/L are a few North American pregnancy and infancy cohorts, with exposure
measured mostly in the mother's urine. In Canada's MIREC cohort, maternal urinary fluoride went with
lower full-scale IQ in boys (−4.49 points per 1 mg/L, 95% CI −8.38 to −0.60) but not in girls or in
both sexes combined (−1.95, 95% CI −5.19 to 1.28). In the same cohort, maternal fluoride intake
(−3.66 per 1 mg, 95% CI −7.16 to −0.15) and water fluoride (−5.29 per 1 mg/L, 95% CI −10.39 to −0.19)
went with lower IQ with no sex interaction
[@ntp2024fluoride].

In the same study population, formula-fed infants showed lower full-scale IQ (−4.40 per 0.5 mg/L
water fluoride, 95% CI −8.34 to −0.46); breastfed infants did not, though their performance IQ was
lower (−6.19, 95% CI −10.45 to −1.94). Where studies analysed the sexes separately, NTP reads the inverse association as «generally reported for both sexes»
[@ntp2024fluoride].

One prospective cohort points the other way. In New Zealand's Dunedin cohort
[@veneri2023fluoride], children exposed at age 5 to
fluoridated water, fluoride toothpaste or tablets showed «no clear differences in IQ» at 38 years
[@ntp2024fluoride]. To the meta-analysis authors'
knowledge, no epidemiological study of fluoride and children's IQ has been conducted in the United
States [@taylor2025fluoride]. Study-by-study estimates are on
[[Fluoride and Child IQ]].

### A water number of 0.7 mg/L is not a total intake

People also take in fluoride from dental products and drinks such as black tea
[@ntp2024fluoride]. NTP states that «The moderate
confidence conclusions may also be relevant to people living in optimally fluoridated areas of the
United States depending on the extent of their additional exposures to fluoride from sources other than
drinking water», and names bottle-fed infants whose powdered formula is made up with fluoridated water as
a case where water figures underestimate intake
[@ntp2024fluoride]. So **most people on 0.7 mg/L
water sit in the unclear band, while a child with high other intake, most clearly a formula-fed infant,
may sit in the band NTP's finding covers**
[inferred from @ntp2024fluoride].

### Adults and other childhood outcomes

For adult cognition NTP held two low-RoB cross-sectional studies and, citing «a lack of evidence of an
effect», gave low confidence to any association
[@ntp2024fluoride]. For infant neurobehavioural
scores and ADHD, the low-RoB studies «provide some evidence of an association», at low confidence
because the outcomes vary and few studies compare directly
[@ntp2024fluoride].

## Fracture risk shows no overall signal at fluoridation levels, except in women over 50

**A single dose-response meta-analysis carries the bone arm:** 37 studies published between 1945 and
2024 [@mazzoli2025fluoride], with drinking-water fluoride
spanning roughly 0 to 4 mg/L. No pooled estimate exists above 4 mg/L, so the top of the curve is the
edge of the data, not a feature of it. Full detail is on [[Fluoride and Bone Health]].

### The overall curve is flat to about 1.4 mg/L

Only four studies feed the all-site fracture curve, too few for separate curves by sex. **Up to about
1.4 mg/L, fracture risk does not change:** RR 1.00 at 1.2 mg/L (95% CI 0.80-1.24). Above that, risk
climbs gradually, to RR 1.06 (0.80-1.41) at 2.0 mg/L, 1.19 (0.87-1.64) at 3.0 mg/L and 1.35 (0.94-1.95)
at 4.0 mg/L [@mazzoli2025fluoride]. The abstract rounds the bend
to about 1.5 mg/L, and the authors conclude there is «increased risk of fractures with fluoride exposure
&gt; 1.5 mg/L in drinking water» [@mazzoli2025fluoride]. This
appraisal reads the curve more cautiously: **every one of these intervals crosses 1, so the curve shows
a rising trend, not a demonstrated harm**
[inferred from @mazzoli2025fluoride].

### Women over 50 show a signal inside the old US fluoridation range

The authors ran an additional analysis of fragility fractures in women over 50, using that age as a
stand-in for menopause [@mazzoli2025fluoride]; the paper reports
it under its sensitivity-analysis results. In this group, risk starts rising at about 0.5 mg/L: RR 0.98
(0.83-1.14) at 0.25 mg/L, 1.00 (0.80-1.24) at 0.45 mg/L and **1.26 (1.10-1.46) at 1.0 mg/L**
[@mazzoli2025fluoride]. Among the fracture estimates the paper
reports in its text, this is **the only raised risk whose interval excludes 1**
.

**1.0 mg/L sits inside the 0.8-1.2 mg/L range that most US fluoridated systems used for many years**
before the 0.7 mg/L recommendation
[@ntp2024fluoride]. The authors conclude there is
«an enhanced susceptibility in postmenopausal females»
[@mazzoli2025fluoride]. They also say the over-50 findings rest
on a limited number of studies, and that the analysis stratified by age rather than confirmed menopause
status [@mazzoli2025fluoride]. The paper gives relative risks
only; no held source turns 1.26 into an absolute fracture risk for women of this age. Other levers that
shift at menopause are on [[Menopause and the Shifting Levers]].

### The other sex-specific curves do not change the picture

Across all ages, the female fragility-fracture curve is U-shaped and non-significant: RR 0.84
(0.67-1.07) at 0.34 mg/L, rising to 1.13 (0.93-1.37) at 1.1 mg/L. The authors say such U-shapes «might
be an artifact due to the more limited number of studies» behind the low-exposure estimates, but add
that a benefit around 0.5-0.6 mg/L, at least for some fracture subsets, «is consistent with our
findings» [@mazzoli2025fluoride]. **This appraisal does not
read the low-dose dip as a protective dose:** a protective arm has to pass a stronger check than a
non-significant dip before anyone should believe it
([[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]], [[The Underivable Optimum]]).

The male curve points the other way, from RR 0.83 (0.72-0.97) at 0.57 mg/L to 0.87 (0.48-1.58) at
1.8 mg/L [@mazzoli2025fluoride]. The interval at 0.57 mg/L
excludes 1, but the abstract reads the sex-specific results as «an effect in females but little or
nothing in males» [@mazzoli2025fluoride], and by 1.8 mg/L the
interval crosses 1. This appraisal does not carry it as a benefit for men
.

### The evidence is weak, and one research group ran the pooled analysis

**This appraisal rates the bone evidence low certainty; the meta-analysis applies no formal certainty
grade of its own** [inferred from @mazzoli2025fluoride].
Sixteen of the 37 studies are ecological, comparing areas rather than people, and the main reasons
studies were rated at high risk of bias were unmeasured confounding (11 studies) and error in measuring
exposure (8). The authors could drop the high-risk studies for only a few endpoints, and not for the
main all-fracture curve; their restricted estimates cover both-sex fragility fractures, hip fractures
and bone density, not the women-over-50 analysis
[@mazzoli2025fluoride]. The restricted figures are on
[[Fluoride and Bone Health]].

The same Modena group also produced the Veneri IQ review, and **no independent team has reproduced the
dose-response curve**. One study by
another team points the same way for older women: Mazzoli reports a Swedish cohort of postmenopausal
women, not poolable because it measured fluoride in urine and diet, in which all-fracture risk was
higher in the top urinary tertile even where water fluoride was ≤ 1 mg/L
[@mazzoli2025fluoride]. That is concordant backing, not a
replication of the curve.

### Higher bone density does not mean fewer fractures here

In the pooled observational curves, hip bone mineral density (BMD) rises with water fluoride in both
sexes. After the authors excluded studies at high risk of bias, both sexes together showed no relation
(«For hip BMD no relation with fluoride exposure emerged»), while women alone, in studies at low to
moderate risk of bias, held flat to about 0.9 mg/L and then rose. Fracture risk does not fall either
way. The authors explain the gap: BMD «only captures bone quantity (density), not bone quality»
[@mazzoli2025fluoride]. **Do not read a fluoride-related BMD
rise as a bone benefit**; judge it on fractures ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). Exercise is the contrast case:
there, a BMD gain does come with fewer fractures ([[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]).

## Your water's fluoride level and your total intake decide which findings apply

**Matched by dose, the caries benefit and the serious harm signals mostly describe different people**,
not one benefit-versus-harm clash; the exceptions on ordinary fluoridated water are named below
 ([[Fluoride and Health]]).

### The four reference levels each answer a different question

US and international limits mark four points on the fluoride scale, each set for its own purpose
[@ntp2024fluoride]. None of them is based on
cognitive outcomes: no drinking-water fluoride recommendation or restriction rests on neurodevelopment
[@taylor2025fluoride].

| Water fluoride | Who sets it | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 0.7 mg/L | US Public Health Service | added dose for community water fluoridation (historically 0.8-1.2 mg/L) |
| 1.5 mg/L | WHO guideline | protection against dental and skeletal fluorosis; NTP's referent for "higher" exposure |
| 2.0 mg/L | EPA secondary standard (not enforceable) | protection of children against severe dental fluorosis |
| 4.0 mg/L | EPA maximum contaminant level (enforceable) | protection against skeletal fluorosis |

### At 0.7 mg/L most people face a decision under uncertainty

For most people on fluoridated tap water, **the choice is not between two known quantities**: a small,
uncertain caries gain in children on one side, common mild fluorosis on the other. The serious harms,
lower IQ and fracture, are **insufficient evidence at this dose for most people, not shown to be
absent**. How to weigh a small decay gain against a visible
but mild enamel change is the reader's call.
([[Fluoride and Health]], [[The Insufficient-Evidence Statement]])

### Above 1.5 mg/L the IQ association carries the case for mitigation

Above 1.5 mg/L, **the moderate-confidence association with lower child IQ is the reason to act**, and
it bears most on pregnancy, infancy and childhood, the windows the studies measured. Switching to another
water source or removing fluoride from the supply is this appraisal's inferred response; no held source
tests whether either lowers the risk. The case rests on IQ alone, since no held source estimates the
caries benefit above 0.7 mg/L.
[inferred from @ntp2024fluoride]

### Five groups sit apart from the majority

**People on private wells or naturally high-fluoride supplies** are the clearest case. About 1.9 million
US residents (0.59%) are served by community water systems with naturally occurring fluoride at or above
1.5 mg/L [@ntp2024fluoride]. Counting private wells
as well, Taylor puts the figure at more than 2.9 million
[@taylor2025fluoride]. For them the above-1.5 findings apply by
water concentration alone.

**Pregnant women** are a named life-stage case, because the IQ studies nearest fluoridation levels
measured exposure in pregnancy, mostly in the mother's urine
[@ntp2024fluoride]. On a supply above 1.5 mg/L, a
pregnant woman is in the group the moderate-confidence finding covers. At 0.7 mg/L the prenatal cohorts
give a reason for concern, not an established effect, and NTP's caution about high other intake applies
 ([[Fluoride and Child IQ]], [[Fluoride and Health]]).

**Formula-fed infants** are NTP's named example of total exposure running above the water number. NTP
relays a CDC recommendation to mix infant formula with low-fluoride bottled water, made «for the purpose
of reducing risk of dental fluorosis»
[@ntp2024fluoride]. **Using it to lower the
exposure NTP flags for IQ is this appraisal's inference**, not a held recommendation
([[Fluoride and Health]]).

**Women over 50** carry the low-certainty fragility-fracture signal from about 0.5 mg/L set out in the
fracture section ([[Fluoride and Bone Health]]). **Children at high risk of decay** are where
fluoridation's benefit is likely largest, by baseline risk rather than by a held estimate
([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).

### The practical step is to learn your water's number

**The first useful act is to find out the fluoride level of your supply**; a private well needs its own
test. No held source appraises home filters for removing fluoride ([[Fluoride and Health]]).
Chlorination byproducts raise a separate bladder-cancer question
([[Disinfection Byproducts and Bladder Cancer]]).

### Other considerations are named here, not weighed

The Cochrane review calls fluoridation good value for money because delivery costs per person
are low, a judgment it says depends on context, and it found one life-cycle analysis of its
environmental footprint [@iheozor2024fluoridation].
On equity, only one study reported disparities data, and the review rates the evidence insufficient to
say whether fluoridation changes socioeconomic disparities in decay
[@iheozor2024fluoridation]. Whether a substance
should be added to everyone's water without individual consent is a question on which this appraisal
holds no evidence ([[Fluoride and Caries Prevention]]).

## The evidence leaves several questions open

**Each empty cell in the dose table marks a question the held evidence cannot yet answer**, not a
finding of no effect. The first three below would change the picture most.

- **Fluoride toothpaste has no effect size here**, so it cannot be compared with fluoridated water
 ([[Fluoride and Caries Prevention]]).
- **No US child IQ study is known to the meta-analysis authors**
  [@taylor2025fluoride], and no independent cohort has measured IQ at
  fluoridation-level exposure. Which bias tool, OHAT or ROBINS-E, suits this literature is unresolved
 ([[Does the Fluoride IQ Harm Survive a Low Risk-of-Bias Restriction]]).
- **The fracture finding for women over 50 has no absolute risk** and no independent dose-response
  study ([[Fluoride and Bone Health]]).
- **Thyroid effects**, including whether iodine status changes the IQ link, are not appraised here, and
  no held source tests how well home filters remove fluoride ([[Fluoride and Health]]).

## What to do turns on your water and who is drinking it

Most readers fall into one or more of these cases.

- **If your supply is above 1.5 mg/L** (some private wells and natural sources), the IQ association
  applies, most directly to pregnancy, infants and children. An alternative source or
  defluoridation is the inferred response. For children aged 8 or younger and bottle-fed infants on
  water above 2 mg/L, CDC advises an alternative source, to prevent fluorosis
  [@taylor2025fluoride].
- **If you are pregnant,** a supply above 1.5 mg/L puts you in the case above; at 0.7 mg/L the evidence
  gives a reason for concern, not an established effect, and NTP's caution about high other intake
  applies ([[Fluoride and Child IQ]]).
- **If you mix infant formula,** the CDC advises low-fluoride bottled water, to prevent dental fluorosis
  [@ntp2024fluoride]. That it also lowers the exposure
  NTP flags for IQ is inference ([[Fluoride and Child IQ]]).
- **On standard fluoridated water,** the decay benefit for children is small and uncertain, the serious
  harms are unresolved rather than ruled out, and **mild enamel mottling is the trade-off you can see**.
  Weighing these is your call ([[Fluoride and Health]]).
- **If you are a woman over 50,** a low-certainty fracture signal appears at fluoridation levels, so your
  water source is worth weighing ([[Fluoride and Bone Health]]).
- **Brush with fluoride toothpaste.** This is standard practice, held as a direction with no effect size:
  the Cochrane authors list it beside «other caries-preventive strategies of proven eﬀectiveness»
  [@iheozor2024fluoridation]
  ([[Fluoride and Caries Prevention]]).
- **Decay risk also depends on sugar:** the sugars-caries link persists despite fluoride
  [@moynihan2014] ([[Free Sugars Intake]]).

## This appraisal grades the evidence, not your outcome

- It appraises what the evidence shows; it does not prescribe for any individual
.
- It grades coherence and fidelity to its sources, not real-world outcomes, so a clean appraisal is not
  a validated recommendation.
- It weighs health only; the autonomy of mass supplementation, cost and equity are named, not weighed
.
- How the findings apply depends on the person: their water, intake, life stage and priorities
.
