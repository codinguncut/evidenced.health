## A few shared exposures matter most, and in older age the firmest evidence is about function

**The exposures that change the risk of several diseases of ageing at once are few:** smoking, physical activity, body fat, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and
alcohol. Heart disease and stroke, type 2 diabetes, several cancers and dementia share most of them, in
the same direction. **So one set of changes lowers the risk of several diseases together**: not smoking,
moving more, keeping body fat, blood pressure and LDL down, and drinking little or nothing, with balance
and muscle-strengthening work added in older age
([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]). Those shared levers are
appraised in full in [[Big Rocks (Median)]] and [[Big Rocks (Elderly)]]. The evidence comes disease by
disease, and no study adds it up into one combined effect.

**Most other candidates add little.** Some rest on insufficient evidence, and some help one disease
while raising the risk of another. Parkinson's disease breaks the shared pattern most clearly
([[Parkinsons Disease Modifiable Risk Factors]]). Dairy, hormone therapy after menopause and statins
each lower one risk while raising another; this guide lists those outcome by outcome without netting
them ([[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]], [[Statins and New-Onset Diabetes]]).

**For older adults, the firmest evidence concerns function more than length of life.** Randomized
trials show, at high certainty, that exercise built on balance and functional training prevents falls in
older people living in the community, cutting the rate of falls by about a quarter (rate ratio 0.76)
[@sherrington2019]. Exercise also lowers fracture risk,
on weaker evidence, and the trials do not show which part of the programme does it
([[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]). The link between activity and a longer life, by contrast, comes
from observational cohorts ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]). **None of the studies appraised
here measures whether a lever shortens the years spent ill or disabled before death**
([[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]).

**In the oldest age bands, the drug trials run thin rather than turn negative**: insufficient
evidence, not evidence of harm (age section below).

| Outcome | Lever | Evidence state | Certainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart attack and stroke | lowering blood pressure or LDL with drugs | benefit (randomized) | high |
| Falls, older adults living in the community | balance and functional exercise | benefit (randomized) | high |
| Type 2 diabetes, people with prediabetes | lifestyle programme | benefit (randomized) | high |
| Death, heart disease, cancer | smoking | harm | high |
| Death, cancer, Parkinson's | physical activity | benefit association; Parkinson's in men only | observational |
| Fractures | exercise | benefit | low to moderate |
| Dementia | the shared exposures | association | see [[Dementia Prevention]] |
| Years lived ill or disabled before death | any lever | insufficient evidence (not measured) | none |

**Overall confidence: medium.** It is high for the large shared exposures and for the blood-pressure
and LDL drug trials. It is lower for Parkinson's disease, the diet-and-cancer links and the eye, where
the evidence is observational and the reviews are few or of low methodological quality
([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]).

This guide is written for the general population. Dementia has its own appraisal in
[[Dementia Prevention]] and appears here as one column. Chronic kidney disease is outside this guide
([[Chronic Kidney Disease and Modifiable Exposures]]), as are treating an established disease and
choosing or dosing a drug.

## The same few exposures move most diseases of ageing

Line up the modifiable risk factors for heart disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer, dementia, Parkinson's
and falls, and **the same handful of exposures keeps reappearing**: smoking, physical activity, body
fat, blood pressure, LDL, alcohol and, more weakly, the overall diet pattern. No single disease body
says this; each studies its own disease. The overlap appears only when the lists sit side by side
-> [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

trials, (O) observational studies, (MR) Mendelian randomization, a genetic natural experiment.

### Why the rows run in this order

The order is a judgment about effect size, certainty and breadth taken together. It is not a
calculation: the cells measure different outcomes with different designs, so **no row can be added up
into one cross-disease effect**, and no held source computes one.

**Smoking comes first: the largest effect with the widest reach.** Current smokers die at about three
times the rate of never-smokers (all-cause HR 3.0, 99% CI 2.7-3.3, in women; 2.8, 2.4-3.1, in men)
[@jha2013] -> [[Smoking and Mortality]].

**Physical activity comes second: the broadest lever, and no drug replicates it.** In device-measured
cohorts, all-cause mortality fell most, HR 0.34 (0.27-0.43), at about 300 counts per minute of total
activity, with no further drop above that
[@who2020]. That counts movement at
any intensity [@ekelundacc2019]. These are
cohorts, where illness that lowers activity can inflate the contrast, so read the flat top as where
the data level off, not as a proven dose ceiling
-> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

Two of activity's cells are weaker than the row suggests. **The dementia link is contested**: a pooled cohort analysis found no association once activity was measured
10 or more years before diagnosis [@kivimaki2019inactivity],
while studies with at least 20 years of follow-up still found RR 0.79 (0.71-0.87)
[@isomarkku2022]. The linked page reads this as
insufficient evidence for a direct effect, not proof of no effect
-> [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]]. The Parkinson's benefit
appeared in men only (Parkinson's section below).

**Body fat comes third: broad associations, thinner trials of losing it.** Weight-loss trials cut
all-cause mortality (RR 0.82, 0.71-0.95) but did not measurably cut cardiovascular events
[@ma2017]; Look AHEAD found HR 0.95
(0.83-1.09) [@look2013]
-> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]], [[Body Fatness and Cancer Risk]]. For diabetes the
trial evidence is strong: in the DPP a lifestyle programme cut incidence by 58% (48-66) against 31%
(17-43) for metformin, and preventing one case over three years took 6.9 people on the programme and
13.9 on metformin [@knowler2002]
-> [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].

**Blood pressure and LDL carry the firmest randomized proof, and drugs supply most of it.** Each
5 mmHg of systolic lowering cut major cardiovascular events by about 9% in people without heart disease
[@bplttc2021], and each
1 mmol/L of LDL lowered by a statin cut them by 22% and all-cause mortality by 10% (RR 0.90, 0.87-0.93)
[@ctt2010]. Because mature drugs reach
these markers, **diet and exercise aimed only at blood pressure or LDL add less on cardiovascular
outcomes** than their raw effect suggests. The discount covers only those outcomes; no held evidence
shows one drug doing what activity does from falls to cancer, so activity's rank holds
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

**Alcohol has no protective arm once the comparisons are corrected.** Adjusted for how abstainers were
defined and for study quality, low-volume drinkers showed no survival advantage: RR 0.97 (0.88-1.07)
[@stockwell2016]
-> [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]], [[Alcohol and Cancer Risk]].

**Diet pattern comes last: its hard-outcome evidence is narrow.** PREDIMED cut a cardiovascular
composite, mostly through fewer strokes, but **all-cause mortality did not change: HR 0.98 (0.77-1.24)**
[@estruch2018]
-> [[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]], [[Diet Physical Activity and Cancer Prevention]].

### What the dementia figures can and cannot say

The Lancet Commission credits its 14 factors with 45.3% of dementia cases, but that is a population
figure. The Commission states that «this risk modification affects the population but does not
guarantee any individual will avoid dementia»
[@livingston2024]. The factors overlap, and the Commission
adjusts each factor's share for that overlap before combining them
[@livingston2024]. The same caution applies to any
attributable burden: it ranks causes of disease in a population, not levers for a person
-> [[Dementia Prevention]].

**Bundling the shared levers into one dementia programme has not prevented dementia in trials.**
FINGER's primary outcome was a cognitive-test score, which improved slightly over two years
[@ngandu2015]. Pooled data from the MAPT
and preDIVA trials showed no effect on incident dementia (HR 0.98, 0.80-1.21), and no subgroup
responded, whether pre-specified or found by a data-driven search
[@coley2025]. Blood-pressure
treatment on its own did lower incidence (table above)
-> [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]], [[Dementia Prevention]].

## Parkinson's, cancer and the eye add a few levers of their own

**Most disease-specific levers are weaker than the shared ones, and the strongest apply only to a
narrow group.** A few more lower one disease's risk while raising another's; those close this section
 -> [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

### Parkinson's disease has few credible levers

**The Parkinson's evidence is thin, and most of what looks like a lever may not be causal.** An
umbrella review of 46 systematic reviews rated none high or moderate quality on AMSTAR-2; 39 were
critically low. Activity went with lower risk in men (0.68) but not clearly in women (0.91, CI
crossing 1); the authors still advise activity for women for its general benefits. Body fat,
hypertension and LDL showed no significant association, while diabetes raised the risk, RR 1.31
(1.10-1.57; 4 cohort studies)
[@chen2021pd]. **Set against the
shared table, only activity and statin use point the same way as for the other diseases**: activity in
men only, and statins on observational data open to confounding (RR 0.82; opposite-sign table below)
.

The protective associations are coffee (RR 0.67, 0.58-0.76), caffeine (0.55, 0.43-0.71) and tea (0.64,
0.50-0.82); the coffee and caffeine estimates pool case-control with cohort studies. Dairy food carries
the one consistent dietary risk signal (RR 1.40, 1.20-1.63). Pesticide exposure (OR 1.76, 1.56-2.04)
rests on case-control and mixed designs, from which the review drew no reliable conclusion
[@chen2021pd].

**A prodrome lasting years can manufacture protective associations**: early, undiagnosed disease can cut
activity, coffee intake and smoking, so even cohorts cannot rule out reverse causation. On smoking's
inverse association the review leaves causality open and, because smoking causes many other diseases,
concludes it «does not impact public health strategies for reducing smoking among populations»
[@chen2021pd]. No held source sizes
any Parkinson's factor in absolute terms; activity is the one association worth acting on, and it is
already a shared lever -> [[Parkinsons Disease Modifiable Risk Factors]],
[[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

### Cancer adds processed meat, wholegrains and one harmful supplement

The largest diet-adjacent cancer levers are the shared ones: body fat, alcohol and activity
 -> [[Diet Physical Activity and Cancer Prevention]]. **Below them, processed meat is the
best-graded single food**: colorectal cancer RR 1.16 (1.08-1.26) per 50 g a day, graded convincing; red
meat's RR 1.12 (1.00-1.25) per 100 g a day touches the null and is graded probable
[@wcrf2018]. NutriRECS estimates about 7
fewer cancer deaths per 1000 people over a lifetime for three fewer servings of unprocessed red meat a
week, at low certainty, and suggests that adults continue their current intake (a weak recommendation)
[@nutrirecs2019]. The two bodies read
largely overlapping cohorts in opposite directions
-> [[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]].

WCRF grades wholegrains and dietary fibre as probable protection against colorectal cancer. **One
supplement causes harm: WCRF judges high-dose beta-carotene a cause of lung cancer in current and former
smokers** [@wcrf2018ter]. Two trials in people who
smoked or were exposed to asbestos at work each found more lung cancer: RR 1.18 (1.03-1.36) with
beta-carotene, and adjusted RR 1.28 (1.04-1.57) with beta-carotene plus vitamin A
[@uspstf2022supp]
-> [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

### The eye shares part of the core

The shared exposures also reach age-related eye disease, on observational evidence only. Smoking goes
with macular degeneration (AMD) at OR 1.86 (1.33-2.6), and high blood pressure at OR 1.24 (1.09-1.4)
[@babaker2025amd]. Activity is
associated with less cataract (RR 0.90, 0.86-0.94), though an E-value of 1.46 means a moderate
unmeasured confounder could explain it; for AMD the estimate (0.92, 0.84-1.01) is insufficient evidence,
not a null [@aune2026vision]
-> [[Cataract and Modifiable Exposures]]. In people who already have AMD, the AREDS supplement slowed
progression (OR 0.72, 0.58-0.90; moderate certainty), and its beta-carotene version went with more
lung cancer in former smokers (low certainty)
[@evans2023amdsupp]. The review
covers progression only, not preventing AMD
-> [[Age-Related Macular Degeneration and Modifiable Exposures]].

### Some exposures lower one disease's risk and raise another's

**Four exposures move different diseases in opposite directions.** The table does not net them: the
outcomes differ in weight and certainty, and the weighting belongs to the reader.

**HRT is not a drug for preventing disease.** WHI, after an average 5.2 years, concluded that «this
regimen should not be initiated or continued for primary prevention of CHD»
[@rossouw2002]. Formulation and timing
are appraised in [[Does Timing Modify HRT Cardiovascular and Mortality Effect]].

The statin's diabetes excess is about 0.12% a year at standard intensity, concentrated in people whose
glycaemia already sits near the diagnostic threshold, and the CTT investigators judge it greatly
outweighed by the drug's benefit on major vascular events
[@reith2024diabetes]. Dairy's colorectal grade did not
produce a WCRF recommendation: the panel held back because of a suggestive prostate-cancer signal
[@wcrf2018].

## In older age, function is where the firmest evidence sits

**Length of life and function are separate outcomes, and the trials measure them unevenly.** Falls,
fractures and mobility have randomized evidence; the mortality legs of the same levers rest mostly on
cohorts [[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]. A guideline network
meta-analysis judged that, because of its much higher disutility, a fracture of any type is probably
more important than a fall or a change in functional status (MODERATE certainty)
[@pillay2024falls]
-> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].

### Balance and functional exercise prevents falls in community-dwelling older adults

**Falls prevention in community-dwelling adults carries HIGH-certainty trial evidence.** A Cochrane
review pooled 108 RCTs with 23,407 community-dwelling participants, mean age 76. Exercise cut the rate
of falls by 23% (rate ratio 0.77, 0.71-0.83; 59 RCTs; HIGH certainty): at 850 falls per 1000 people per
year, 195 fewer falls (144-246 fewer). **Balance and functional exercise carries the effect** (0.76,
0.70-0.81; 39 RCTs; HIGH). Resistance training alone (1.14, 0.67-1.97) and walking (1.14, 0.66-1.97)
are very-low-certainty estimates crossing 1: insufficient evidence, not a shown null
[@sherrington2019].

**The relative effect held across ages; the absolute gain grows with fall risk.** It did not differ
between people aged 75 and over and younger participants, or by selection for high fall
risk. The authors wrote:
«There is likely to be a greater absolute impact in people identified at increased risk of falling, but
there is benefit also for those who are at more general risk in the community.»
[@sherrington2019] **The dose default comes from
comparing trials**: an earlier meta-regression found larger effects at 3 or more hours a week (ratio of
rate ratios 0.77, 0.65-0.91), which the authors flag as potentially confounded while still recommending
it. In care homes, exercise alone gave 0.90 (0.72-1.12), insufficient evidence
[@sherrington2017falls]. Read 3 hours as a
labelled practice default, not a tested dose.

### Exercise cuts major osteoporotic fractures, without saying which channel does it

**Exercise programmes reduced major osteoporotic fractures by about a quarter**: incidence ratio 0.75
(95% CI 0.59-0.94) across 11 trials in cohorts aged over 50 on average. The trials mixed fall-prevention
and bone-loading programmes, so the review cannot say whether fewer falls, stronger bone or both did it
[@hoffmann2022]. The same group produced
the matching bone-density meta-analysis, so the two are not independent confirmations
[inferred from @hoffmann2022] [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]].
The Cochrane falls review points the same way: fall-related fractures fell from 64 to 47 per 1000 (RR
0.73, 0.56-0.95; LOW certainty) [@sherrington2019].

**A bone-density gain is a surrogate, not a fracture result.** In postmenopausal women, exercise raised
bone density modestly (SMD 0.29, 0.16-0.42, at the lumbar spine; 0.41, 0.30-0.52, at the total hip)
[@mohebbi2023].
Fluoride shows why. Overall fracture risk began rising above about 1.5 mg/L in drinking water
[@mazzoli2025fluoride], while hip bone density rose with fluoride
in both sexes; the authors say the hypothesis that a density gain coexists with higher fracture risk
should not be dismissed [@mazzoli2025fluoride] -> [[Fluoride and Bone Health]],
[[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]].

### Vitamin D with calcium has shown no fracture benefit in community trials

**In community-dwelling adults the trials show no fracture benefit and one harm.** A US evidence review
of adults aged 50 and over excluded people known to be deficient, in institutions, or with osteoporosis
or a prior fracture. Its total-fracture result rests on one trial in postmenopausal women (n=36,282):
risk difference -0.35% (-1.02 to 0.31), and the review graded the evidence for no fracture benefit as
low strength. Kidney stones rose (RR 1.18, 1.04-1.35; absolute +0.33%, 0.06-0.60), on evidence graded
moderate [@kahwati2018]. Calcium raised
myocardial infarction in one meta-analysis (RR 1.24, 1.07-1.45)
[@bolland2011], a contested signal
-> [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]].

**A broader pool shows a modest hip-fracture benefit.** Vitamin D with calcium cut hip fracture by 16%
(RR 0.84, 0.72-0.97; 6 RCTs, including the community trial above); vitamin D alone did not (1.14,
0.98-1.32). The authors flag uncertain intervals and high risk of bias, and report gains «somewhat
greater in RCTs among older participants living in institutions»
[@yao2019]. That compares trials, not people, and no
held trial tests a deficient group [inferred from @yao2019]. For people in
institutions or with osteoporosis, a prior fracture or a known deficiency, the decision is a
prescriber's.

### In frailty, function moves; falls and survival are unshown

**For prefrail or frail adults, physical activity improves function.** A meta-analysis of 26 studies
(8022 adults aged 65 and over) found gains in mobility (SMD 0.60, 0.37-0.83), activities of daily
living (0.50, 0.15-0.84) and quality of life (0.60, 0.13-1.07). Frailty became less common (RR 0.58,
0.36-0.93), all at moderate certainty, though only 4 of the 23 meta-analysed trials reported frailty.
Falls were RR 0.80 (0.51-1.26; very low certainty), insufficient evidence. The programmes lasted 29
weeks on average [@racey2021frailtypa].

**So the community falls result should not be carried into frailty.** Start lower and supervised; the
linked page holds a labelled low-volume floor for resistance work (two sets of three lower-limb
exercises, twice a week), not a tested dose [[Frailty]]. Mortality was not measured.

### Grip, muscle mass and fitness are markers; training is the lever

**Low grip strength and muscle mass predict death, but no trial shows raising them lowers it.** In UK
Biobank, each 5 kg lower grip went with higher all-cause mortality: HR 1.20 (1.17-1.23) in women, 1.16
(1.15-1.17) in men [@celismorales2018]. In 9 cohorts
aged 65 and over, people who died had lower muscle mass at baseline (SMD -0.18, -0.23 to -0.12)
[@santana2021]. These markers, like fitness, place
a person in a risk stratum; they are not targets [[Grip Strength and Mortality]],
[[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]].

**The levers are the activities.** Muscle-strengthening activity goes with lower all-cause mortality
(RR 0.85, 0.79-0.93), at very low certainty, downgraded mainly for indirectness because most studies
were conducted in the USA [@momma2022].
In 15 trials of healthy older adults, power training (lifting fast) beat conventional strength training
on function tests (SMD 0.43, 0.23-0.62; GRADE high). No included study used physical activity in daily
life as an outcome, and falls were not among the review's outcomes
[@elhadouchi2022]
-> [[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]].

**Protein supports the training; it does not replace it.** PROT-AGE advises at least 1.0-1.2 g/kg a
day, 1.2 g/kg for active older adults [@bauer2013];
1.2-1.5 g/kg with acute or chronic disease, up to 2.0 g/kg in severe illness, injury or marked
malnutrition, except in severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30, not on dialysis)
[@bauer2013]. It is a consensus built largely on
surrogate endpoints [inferred from @bauer2013]
[[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

**Raising a hormone is not a fracture lever.** In TRAVERSE (men 45-80 with low testosterone and preexisting
cardiovascular disease or a high risk of it), clinical fractures were numerically higher on testosterone: 3.50% vs 2.46%, HR
1.43 (1.04-1.97), unadjusted for multiple comparisons
[@snyder2024] [[Testosterone Adiposity and Muscle]].

### Knee osteoarthritis is where weight loss does improve function

**For overweight adults aged 55 and over with painful knees, diet plus exercise beats exercise alone.**
In the 18-month IDEA trial (n=454), the combined arm lost 10.6 kg (11.4%), had less pain (1.02 points on
a 0-20 scale, 0.33-1.71; clinical meaning unclear) and better function (WOMAC difference 4.29,
2.07-6.50). Post hoc, 38% of the combined arm reported little or no pain, against 20% on diet and 22% on
exercise [@messier2013idea]. This is
a lever for one joint, not for frailty
[inferred from @messier2013idea]
[[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]].

### Nobody has measured the shape of decline

**The trials count events and short-term scores, not how the last decades unfold.** A 2026 review of 15
RCTs (4656 participants) on intrinsic capacity or quality of life could not pool them; exercise was
linked to better scores in 11 of 13 studies, with generally small effects
[@zheng2026healthspan]. Exercise
had no clear effect on quality of life in the falls trials (SMD -0.03, -0.10 to 0.04)
[@sherrington2019]. No held source measures years
lived without disability, though many people care more about how they decline than how long they live
-> [[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]].

## The order of levers shifts with age

**No held source ranks levers by age.** The tables below line up age-specific results from separate
sources; reading a pattern across them is this guide's own step. The cells mix designs and
units, so compare directions within a row, not sizes across rows
-> [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

### Lifestyle levers by age

| Lever | Younger bands | Older bands | Design and population | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quitting smoking (years of life gained vs continuing) | quit at 25-34: \~10; at 35-44: \~9 | quit at 45-54: \~6; at 55-64: \~4 | US national cohort (NHIS); data stop at 55-64 | [@jha2013] |
| Body fat: all-cause mortality per 5 BMI units above 25 | 35-49 y: HR 1.52 (1.47-1.56) | 50-69 y: 1.37 (1.35-1.39); 70-89 y: 1.21 (1.17-1.25) | pooled prospective cohorts, never-smokers without chronic disease | [@globalbmi2016] |
| Lifestyle programme: diabetes incidence vs placebo | 25-44: 48% lower (27-63); 45-59: 59% (44-70) | >=60: 71% lower (51-83) | RCT (DPP), adults with raised fasting and post-load glucose, BMI >=24 | [@knowler2002] |
| Daily steps: where mortality risk stops falling | under 60: \~8000-10 000 | 60 and over: \~6000-8000 | 15 cohorts, n=47 471, observational | [@paluch2022] |

**Quitting smoking pays at every age the data cover**, though the gain shrinks from about ten years to
about four. Quitting before 40 removes about 90% of the excess risk, yet quitters by about 39 still
carry a hazard ratio of 1.2, so earlier is better; there is no quit-age band past 64
[@jha2013] -> [[Smoking and Mortality]].

**The body-fat gradient flattens with age but stays above 1** at 70-89: a weaker association, not a
protective one. The Nordic Nutrition Recommendations decline to set an optimal BMI for
older adults because the data are inadequate
[@nnr2023]. An older adult who loses weight
should pair it with resistance training and protein, because energy restriction costs more muscle as
reserve falls -> [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]], [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

**The diabetes programme worked at least as well in older participants.** Placebo incidence was about
11 per 100 person-years in every band; lifestyle brought it to 6.2 at 25-44 and 3.1 at 60 and over
[@knowler2002], roughly 5 and 8 fewer cases per
100 person-years [inferred from @knowler2002]. The
subgroup tests were not planned or powered, so this is a direction, not a proven age effect
-> [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].

**Older adults reach the step plateau sooner.** The age-by-steps interaction was significant within the
pooled cohorts (p=0.012), and the top quartile's median was 10 901 steps, so the data say nothing about
counts far above that [@paluch2022]
-> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

### Drugs and supplements by age

| Lever | Younger bands | Older bands | Design and population | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood pressure: major CV events per 5 mmHg lower SBP | <55: HR 0.82 (0.76-0.88) | 55-64, 65-74: 0.91 (0.88-0.95); 75-84: 0.91 (0.87-0.96); >=85: 0.99 (0.87-1.12) | IPD from 51 RCTs, 358 707 people, with and without prior CVD | [@rahimi2021bpage] |
| Statin: major vascular events per 1 mmol/L lower LDL, over 75 | not shown separately | with vascular disease: RR 0.85 (99% CI 0.73-0.98); without: 0.92 (99% CI 0.73-1.16) | 28 statin RCTs, 14 483 people over 75 | [@ctt2019] |
| Aspirin, starting for primary prevention | 40-59 at >=10% 10-year risk: individual decision, small net benefit (Grade C) | 60 and over: recommend against starting (Grade D) | guideline, moderate certainty | [@uspstf2022aspirin] |
| Metformin: diabetes incidence vs placebo | 25-44: 44% lower (21-60) | >=60: 11% (-33 to 41) | RCT (DPP), as above | [@knowler2002] |
| Vitamin D, empiric, in healthy adults | 50-74: nothing above the recommended allowance (600 IU/day to age 70, 800 IU/day from 71) | 75 and over: empiric supplementation suggested (conditional), daily lower doses preferred; trial doses averaged \~900 IU/day; mortality RR 0.96 (0.93-1.00) | guideline on pooled RCTs | [@demay2024vitamind] |

**Blood-pressure drugs keep their relative effect to 84; at 85 and over the evidence is insufficient,
not negative.** The authors advise continuing a well-tolerated drug; frail, multimorbid and
institutionalized people were under-represented, so starting one in them is an individual trade-off
[@rahimi2021bpage]
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**Statins for people over 75 without vascular disease are unconfirmed, not refuted.** The authors say
there were too few such participants to assess the group alone
[@ctt2019]. The USPSTF calls the evidence for
starting at 76 or older insufficient [@uspstf2022];
the 2026 US cholesterol guideline says starting may be reasonable after discussion, if life expectancy
is at least 2.5 years [@accaha2026]
-> [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]].

**For aspirin, age is the harm switch**: the relative bleeding risk is constant, but the absolute rate
rises with age, more steeply from 60 [@uspstf2022aspirin]
-> [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]. **Metformin moves the other way from
lifestyle**: near-null in the oldest DPP band, where lifestyle's advantage was greater
[@knowler2002].

**Vitamin D appears only at the oldest age.** For healthy adults aged 50-74 the Endocrine Society found
high-certainty evidence of little or no effect on fractures, CV events, cancer and death. At 75 and over
the estimate is about 6 fewer deaths per 1000, with an upper bound touching 1.00 and age bands the panel
calls somewhat arbitrary [@demay2024vitamind]
-> [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]]. Menopausal hormone therapy by age is in
[[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]].

### The young end: judge the lifetime, not the next ten years

**For younger adults a 10-year risk score understates the case for acting.** ESC notes that young
people with high risk factors usually score low over ten years while their lifetime risk is very high;
below 40 drugs are not usually considered, and a lifelong healthy lifestyle matters more
[@esc2021]. Its modelled gain from lowering LDL by
1 mmol/L falls from roughly 0.6-1.6 CVD-free years for a man aged 45-49 to 0.0-0.3 at 90 and over
[@esc2021]
-> [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]. Population burden shifts with age too (alcohol
leads at 25-49, systolic pressure from 50) [@gbd2020rf],
but a population share ranks nothing for one person -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

### The old end: muscle, life expectancy and risk set the order, not a birthday

**No single age flips the order.** ESC raises treatment thresholds from 70, because almost everyone
over 70 exceeds the usual thresholds and treatment buys less CVD-free time
[@esc2021]. Three things re-rank the levers for an
older person: muscle, life expectancy and baseline risk. A sarcopenic 58-year-old already
puts keeping muscle before losing weight; a robust 68-year-old with high visceral fat does not
-> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

**For a frail person, deliberate weight loss can do harm.** Unintentional weight loss is one of the
five Fried criteria that define frailty, alongside exhaustion, low activity, slow walking and poor grip
[@who2020]. Someone who already
meets those criteria has little muscle or reserve to spare, so for them the weight lever reverses: keep
muscle first, and treat deliberate weight loss as contraindicated. This applies to the frail, not to
every older adult; a robust older person with excess weight can still lose it, paired with training
and protein -> [[Frailty]], [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

**Read together, the tables suggest a pattern, not a finding:** the lifestyle levers keep paying into
old age, while one drug harm (aspirin bleeding) grows with age.

## What does not carry a large effect

**A further set of candidates does not reach the size of the levers above.** Knowing that a
much-discussed option adds little is useful: it tells a reader where to stop optimizing
([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]).

| Beta-carotene supplements | harm | lung cancer in people who smoked (cancer section above) | Avoid, above all if you have ever smoked |

## What the evidence cannot yet say

**These are absences, and none of them points in a direction.** Each is a question the held sources do
not answer, not a finding of no effect.

### What has not been measured

- **The shape of decline.** No held source measures disease-free life expectancy or compressed
  morbidity, and the 15 capacity trials could not be pooled
  ([[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]).
- **Muscle and connection as levers.** No trial shows that raising muscle mass, grip or fitness lowers
  mortality ([[Low Muscle Mass and Mortality]], [[Grip Strength and Mortality]]), and none shows that
  more social connection does ([[Social Connection and Mortality]]).
- **Strength or power training alone and falls.** No falls-incidence trial tests resistance or power
  training on its own ([[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]]).
- **Weight loss and a person's cancer risk.** The cancer grades rest on body fat in cohorts; no held
  source shows that losing weight lowers an individual's cancer risk ([[Body Fatness and Cancer Risk]]).
- **A combined effect across diseases.** No source pools one lever's effect over several diseases, and
  this appraisal cannot compute it ([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]).

### What the held sources do not cover

- **Activity and Parkinson's.** No magnitude meta-analysis is held, so the leg rests on an umbrella
  review, with no attributable fraction or absolute risk ([[Parkinsons Disease Modifiable Risk Factors]]).
- **A shared biology of ageing.** No held source tests a common mechanism behind the shared levers; the
  shared pattern is epidemiological, and any mechanism claim here is inference
  ([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]).
- **Cancer causes outside lifestyle.** WCRF counts occupational carcinogens, environmental pollution
  and certain long-term infections among the preventable causes of cancer
  [@wcrf2018ter]; they sit outside this guide
  ([[Diet Physical Activity and Cancer Prevention]]).
- **Osteoporosis as one question.** No single page here appraises it; the fracture evidence is spread
  across exercise, falls and supplement pages ([[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]],
  [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]]).
- **Bone drugs as a comparator.** No review of bisphosphonate or other antiresorptive efficacy is held
  ([[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]).

**This appraisal checks that each claim matches its sources and that the claims hang together.**
Nothing in it has been tested against what later happened to people who acted on it.

## What to do at each stage of life

The numbers behind each item sit in the sections above and on the linked pages.

**Anyone: do not smoke, or stop**, which pays at every age ([[Smoking and Mortality]]). **Move more
at any intensity and sit less**; in cohorts, mortality stopped falling at about 8,000-10,000 steps a
day under 60 and 6,000-8,000 at 60 and over ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]). **Keep body
fat, blood pressure and LDL down** ([[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]],
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], [[LDL Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]),
and **drink little or nothing** ([[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]).

**Earlier is better.** Quitting smoking at 25-34 gains about 10 years against about 4 at 55-64; in
younger adults, judge the levers on lifetime risk, not 10-year risk
([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

**Older adults: add balance and functional exercise, about 3 hours a week as a practice default, plus
resistance work** ([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]],
[[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]). Eat at least 1.0-1.2 g of protein per kg a day
(1.2 g/kg if active), unless you have severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30) and are not on dialysis;
when losing weight keep muscle with training and protein
([[Protein Intake for Older Adults]], [[Resistance Training for Sarcopenic Obesity]]). Routine vitamin
D with calcium has shown no fracture benefit in community trials; for people in institutions, or with
osteoporosis, a prior fracture or a known deficiency, the question is a prescriber's
([[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]]). Do not start aspirin at 60 or
over ([[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]).

**The frail: keep muscle first, and avoid deliberate weight loss** ([[Frailty]]); a robust older adult
with excess weight can still lose it, with training and protein. **The oldest:** trial evidence for
starting a drug runs thin; missing evidence is not shown harm, and the decision is
individual ([[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]). A well-tolerated
blood-pressure drug already in use is usually continued
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

**Past the large shared levers, stop optimizing.** Supplements, acrylamide, coffee and similar
refinements are small or unproven (see *What does not carry a large effect*).

The questions still open are listed under *What the evidence cannot yet say*.
