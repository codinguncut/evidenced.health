Almost everything that lowers your dementia risk is what already protects your heart: blood pressure,
blood sugar, weight, cholesterol, not smoking, staying active. Only one of those levers has a
randomized trial showing it cuts dementia *diagnoses* rather than just test scores — lowering blood
pressure in older people with hypertension, and even there the benefit is small. The dedicated
"brain-health bundle" (diet plus exercise plus brain-training plus vascular checks) moves a cognitive
test score at best, comes up null on the disease itself, and no high-risk subgroup has been found in
whom it works. Single foods, supplements and named diets like MIND show promising associations that
shrink or vanish once anyone runs the experiment. The honest headline is calibrated uncertainty, not a
list of confident brain-specific fixes -> [[Age-Related Diseases]], [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. **Do the
cardiometabolic basics early; skip the supplements.**

## Most of what lowers dementia risk is what protects your heart

The Lancet Commission's headline is «nearly half of all cases of dementia being associated with 14
potentially modifiable risk factors» — a modelled population attributable fraction
[@livingston2024]. That figure carries three loads it is
usually stripped of. It is a **population** attributable fraction, not a
personal guarantee — it assumes every factor is fully removed and is fully causal. Most of the 14 are
the **same cardiometabolic rocks** already pulled for the heart: hypertension, diabetes, obesity,
physical inactivity, smoking, high LDL. And they overlap, so their separate percentages **cannot be
added** — a large share of the "dementia" benefit is the *same* benefit counted once for the heart and
again for the brain.

The practical consequence: a person managing blood pressure, glucose, weight and activity for
cardiovascular reasons has **already pulled most of the dementia levers**. Sizing any brain-specific
addition means asking what it adds *at the margin*, on top of rocks a healthy adult is pulling anyway
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]], [[Age-Related Diseases]]. The levers work
**earlier and longer**: midlife hypertension and hearing loss carry more weight than the same factor
late in life [@livingston2024]. What you *measure* to track
them is what you already measure for the heart — blood pressure, HbA1c, waist, LDL — not a brain scan.

## The dedicated brain-health bundle moves a test score, not the disease

Four trials tested a packaged multidomain program. Read them by **endpoint** and a clean split appears:
the bundle nudges a cognitive *test battery* (a surrogate) but does not lower dementia *incidence* (the
disease) -> [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]], [[Surrogate Outcomes]].

### FINGER moved a surrogate, and only a little

FINGER randomized at-risk older Finns to a two-year diet-exercise-cognition-vascular bundle. The
between-group difference in the neuropsychological test battery was **+0.022 Z-score points per year
(95% CI 0.002-0.042, p=0.030)**
[@ngandu2015] — statistically real, small,
and on a **test score, not a diagnosis**. FINGER did not run long
enough to count dementia cases; its disease-endpoint verdict waits on the extended follow-up.

### MAPT and preDIVA: null on the surrogate, null on the disease

MAPT tested a similar bundle (with and without omega-3) over three years and found **no significant
benefit on its cognitive composite** overall
[@andrieu2017]. preDIVA ran the harder test —
a six-year nurse-led vascular-care program against
**incident dementia** — and came up null: **HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.71-1.19, p=0.54)**
[@mollvancharante2016]. So the one
bundle trial powered for the disease did not move it.

### No high-risk subgroup rescues the bundle

The obvious defense — *it works, just in the right people* — was tested directly. Coley pooled MAPT and
preDIVA and searched systematically for a responder subgroup: «We did not identify any subgroups of
older adults in whom multidomain interventions significantly reduced incident dementia»
[@coley2025]. This is a **route-(b)
claim that failed its own test**: a responder subgroup needs positive interaction evidence, and the
well-powered pooled analysis found none -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. It is a
worked case against over-personalizing on hope rather than evidence.

### More components is not more benefit

A network meta-analysis of single- and multidomain programs found that stacking components did **not**
beat the best single ones, and the whole comparison sits on **cognitive-surrogate endpoints, not
dementia incidence**
[@mendes2025multidomain]. The bundle's
appeal is theoretical; the evidence does not reward the stack.

## The single levers, ranked by what each actually moves

### Blood pressure — the one randomized win on the disease

Pooling five trials of blood-pressure-lowering in older adults, Peters found an **odds ratio of 0.87
(95% CI 0.75-0.99)** for incident dementia
[@peters2022bp]. This is the **only** modifiable
lever with randomized evidence on the disease itself, and it
is **route (a)** — the relative effect is roughly uniform, so absolute benefit rises with baseline
risk. In absolute terms the effect is small: on the order of **0.4 percentage points over \~4 years
(NNT \~250)**. Real, worth having, already indicated for the heart — not a brain-specific
add-on.

Peters tested for non-linearity and found none substantive
[@peters2022bp], so the observational hint that
very low pressure in old age is harmful is evidence against a J/U-shaped harm, not for one
-> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]. The fuller cardiovascular case, harms and absolute sizing
live at [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### Diabetes drugs — confounded by indication

Whether a particular glucose-lowering *drug class* protects the brain is dominated by **confounding by
indication**: sicker patients get different drugs, so head-to-head class rankings from observational
data are unreliable [@kuatedefo2023].
Treat glucose control as one of the shared cardiometabolic rocks; do not chase a specific agent for its
claimed brain benefit on current evidence.

### Physical activity — direct effect on dementia is unproven

Being active is a big rock for the heart, and inactivity clearly causes the vascular diseases that
raise dementia risk. Whether activity protects the brain *directly and independently* is genuinely
contested: Iso-Markku's meta-analysis finds the association survives long follow-up (**RR 0.80,
0.77-0.84**) [@isomarkku2022], while Kivimaki's
individual-participant analysis finds it **vanishes beyond ten years (HR 1.01, 0.89-1.14)**, consistent
with reverse causation — the dementia prodrome makes people less active years before diagnosis
[@kivimaki2019inactivity]. The clean evidence in both
nearly converges on the null -> [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]]. Be
active regardless — the vascular warrant is not in dispute; the brain-specific one is insufficient.

### Hearing loss — a large marker, a weak treatment lever

Hearing loss is one of the strongest risk *markers*: **HR 1.35 (95% CI 1.26-1.45)** for incident
dementia [@yu2024hearing]. But
an exposure association is not a treatment lever. The ACHIEVE randomized trial of hearing aids found
**no overall benefit** on three-year cognitive change; a pre-specified high-risk subgroup improved
(hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory)
[@lin2023achieve] -> [[Hearing Loss and Dementia]].
Correcting hearing has its own quality-of-life case; do not
oversell it as proven dementia prevention.

### Education, mentally demanding work, staying social

Higher cognitive engagement across life is associated with lower risk — mentally demanding work carried
**HR 0.77** in Kivimaki's cohort
[@kivimaki2021cognitive]
-> [[Cognitive Stimulation at Work and Dementia]]. These support the
**cognitive-reserve** idea but are observational and hard to separate from
lifelong confounders. Reasonable to favor, weak as a claimed intervention.

## Diet and single foods: the signal fades at the diagnosis

### The MIND diet — an observational benefit a trial did not confirm

Observational cohorts tie the MIND diet to better cognitive *function* (pooled **+0.042 per SD,
0.020-0.065**) [@huang2023mind]. The one randomized
test, Barnes, returned a **between-group difference of +0.035 SD (95% CI -0.022 to 0.092)** — a
positive point estimate that does not reach significance
[@barnes2023mind]. Both arms of the tension survive:
the trial's interval **rules out a large effect
but not a small one**, and its point estimate sits inside the observational range, so the RCT *bounds*
the claim rather than overturning it -> [[MIND Diet - Observational Benefit vs Randomized Null]]. And no
trial has tested MIND or Mediterranean on **incident dementia** at all. Eat this way for its cardiometabolic merits; do not bank
on it as a proven brain diet.

### The single-food ceiling

Line up the single-component arms — fruit and vegetables, flavonoids, soy, dairy — and the same shape
repeats: a modest signal on a soft or surrogate endpoint that **disappears at the hard Alzheimer's or
dementia diagnosis** -> [[Single Food Components and Cognitive Outcomes]]. Fruit and vegetables:
cognitive-impairment OR 0.76 but **AD null, 0.88 (0.76-1.01)**
[@zhou2022fruit]. Flavonoids: decline
OR 0.88 but **dementia null, 0.97 (0.79-1.19)**
[@peng2025flavonoid]. Soy's only randomized signal
is a short-term memory test score, with **no dementia-event evidence**
[@cui2020isoflavone] -> [[Soy Products]]. Dairy is
null throughout [@villoz2024dairy] -> [[Dairy]].

**No single food or supplement is an established hard-endpoint lever.** Do not chase foods or pills for
cognition; the value is in the whole pattern routing through the cardiometabolic rocks.

## Sleep and the weaker candidate markers

These carry a plausible directional signal but no established intervention on incident dementia — hold
them at the certainty the evidence supports, not above it.

- **Sleep** — disordered sleep is associated with cognitive decline and dementia, but long sleep
  duration behaves like a *marker* of the prodrome rather than a cause; no trial shows that changing
  sleep prevents dementia [@zhang2025sleep]
  -> [[Sleep]].
- **Ultra-processed food** — associated with higher dementia risk in cohorts, confounded and observational
  [@henney2023upf].
- **Air pollution** — a small association whose interval crosses the null in the current meta-analysis
  [@wilker2023].
- **Omega-3 / fish** — cohort signal, no confirmed dementia-prevention effect from supplementation
  [@wei2023omega3].

## What we still don't know

- **Does the bundle work on the disease over a longer horizon?** FINGER's extended follow-up on incident
  dementia is the pending test.
- **Is there any RCT of a diet pattern on incident dementia?** None exists; the MIND trial measured
  cognition, not the disease.
- **Does physical activity protect the brain independent of its vascular effect?** Insufficient evidence
  -> [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]].
- **Do hearing aids help the highest-risk?** ACHIEVE's positive subgroup is hypothesis-generating only.
- **The loop is open** (R1): no source here grades a lever against a realized dementia outcome under a
  lifetime of intervention. A clean appraisal is not a validated promise.

## What to do

**Pull the cardiometabolic rocks early and keep them pulled** — blood pressure, glucose, weight, LDL,
not smoking, staying active. Blood-pressure control is the one lever with randomized evidence on the
disease, and the rest carry their heart benefit for free. **Correct hearing loss** for its own sake and
because the risk marker is large, without expecting proven dementia prevention. **Eat a
Mediterranean/MIND-style pattern** for its cardiometabolic value, not as a validated brain intervention,
and **skip single-nutrient supplements** for cognition — none has hard-endpoint support. Do not buy a
packaged "brain-health program" expecting it to beat the basics; the bundle has never beaten the disease
and no subgroup rescues it. The realistic promise is a **modest shift in the odds**, largest for those
starting at high cardiovascular risk — worth doing, honestly small.
