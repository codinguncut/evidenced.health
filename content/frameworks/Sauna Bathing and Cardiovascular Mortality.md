**Peripheral-scope lifestyle lever (heat exposure), LOW-CERTAINTY — not a big rock.**
Sauna is admitted on the same evidence bar as food or activity, not by a category exemption; the
Layer-1 ranking is what keeps it peripheral. The reported relative associations are large (2015 fatal
CVD 0.50, all-cause 0.60 at 4-7/wk [@laukkanen2015sauna];
2018 fatal CVD 0.36, Model 3 [@laukkanen2018sauna]),
but how much of them is causal is unknown:
one cohort, plausible residual confounding, and a top category resting on few deaths. The low rank
comes from that certainty, not from a measured small size. Under the wiki's ranking rule, the attention
an exposure gets is never a pass; what decides here is that every mortality estimate the wiki holds rests on one
observational cohort, and its transfer to anyone outside that cohort is an open question, not an
assumption.

## The dominant limitation — one cohort, one research group, two analyses

Every sauna -> mortality estimate below comes from **one** cohort, the Finnish Kuopio Ischaemic Heart
Disease Risk Factor Study (KIHD), analysed by **one** research group:

- **2015 analysis — men only.** A prospective cohort of **2315 middle-aged men (age 42-60)** from
  Eastern Finland, baseline 1984-89, median follow-up 20.7 years (IQR 18.1-22.6); outcomes SCD, fatal
  CHD, fatal CVD, all-cause. [@laukkanen2015sauna]
- **2018 analysis — men and women, older.** The KIHD re-examination sample: «In the 11-year follow-up
  visit of the first cohort, women were invited to join this study.» **1688 participants (867 women,
  821 men), aged 53-74** (the 53.4-73.8 range describes the sampled cohort of 2358), baseline March 1998 - December 2001, median follow-up 15.0 years (IQR
  14.1-15.9), **181 fatal CVD deaths (118 men, 63 women)**. Fatal CVD is its only outcome — no
  all-cause, SCD or CHD-death results. [@laukkanen2018sauna]
- **The two analyses are not independent.** Same cohort, same group (T. and J.A. Laukkanen, Khan,
  Zaccardi author both), so agreement between them is **not replication** and never type-E backing.
  The 2018 paper does not state how many of its 821 men were also in the 2015 analysis; its wording
  places the 1998-2001 visit inside the original male cohort's follow-up, so the men are plausibly
  largely the same people re-examined 11 years on. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]
- A companion review (the Mayo 2018 review) shares authors with these papers, so it cannot
  supply type-E backing whatever it contains. Its content is not held, and it must not be cited as
  replication. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]

The wiki holds **no independent replication cohort, no non-Finnish population, no RCT, and no
systematic review over independent data**; women enter only through the 2018 KIHD sample, where the
frequency association was **not detected** and the duration estimates are imprecise (below). Whether an
independent cohort or a review exists is an open acquisition question: the searches run so far did not
settle it, so this is a statement about holdings, not about the literature. One named candidate is a
systematic review of regular dry sauna bathing by authors outside the KIHD group, cited by the 2018 paper
(ref 3); what it covers, and whether it adds any non-KIHD mortality data, is unverified. In 2018 the authors wrote of
their 2015 paper: «To our knowledge, this is the only available study [19] on the prospective
association between sauna habits and the risk of mortality outcomes» — a statement as of 2018, not a
current survey. [@laukkanen2018sauna] Transportability is
therefore almost entirely *unestablished* — the association is documented in middle-aged and older
Eastern Finns, with the frequency signal carried by men's deaths, by one research group. The peripheral-scope framing and Layer-1 ranking here are
the wiki's own appraisal, applied to the sources' reported estimates.

## The dose-response — an observational gradient in one cohort

### 2015 analysis — men 42-60, four outcomes

- **Absolute rates (crude):** fatal CVD per 1000 person-years, once / 2-3 / 4-7 per week: **10.1
  (7.9-12.9) / 7.6 (6.3-9.2) / 2.7 (1.3-5.4)**. Unadjusted. The 4-7 group was younger (mean 60 vs 64
  years), which widens the crude gap, and more often male (64.4% vs 38.9%), which narrows it; the crude
  ratio 4-7 vs once (about 0.27) sits close to the age-and-sex-adjusted Model 1 HR of 0.30.
  [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]
- **The abstract's headline 4-7/wk HR, 0.23 (0.08-0.65), is Model 4** — Model 3 plus incident CHD as a
  time-varying covariate, run only in participants free of CHD at baseline («The model was limited to
  the population at risk and did not include those who already had coronary heart disease»; 28.1% had a
  CHD history). It is not the full-sample estimate. It also adjusts for incident CHD, which the authors
  include «as it is a known factor in the path- way for development of CVD mortality» — a step on the
  causal path, so adjusting for it is a second reason not to read 0.23 as the association.
  [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]
- **The top category rests on 8 deaths, all in men.** The 73 women at 4-7/wk add person-time with no
  deaths, which pulls the pooled estimate down (overall Model 3 0.36 vs men 0.39), so the overall figure
  is not a men-only estimate; but every death in the top category is a man's. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]
- **Weekly duration** (total minutes per week: <=15, 16-45, >45 — a different quantity from 2015's

  0.30-0.80); 16-45 min/week 0.77 (0.55-1.07). Neither sex alone reaches significance in Model 3 (men >45:
  0.57, 0.31-1.05; women >45: 0.75, 0.31-1.85); sex interaction P = 0.314. Weekly duration largely
  tracks frequency (median 60 min/week in the 4-7 group vs 10 in the once group), and no model holds
  both, so it is not shown to be a separate lever. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]

### Shape — no knee located; curvature not detected for frequency, detected for weekly duration

**2015:** three categories with point estimates ordered inversely for frequency (all four outcomes,
P-trend reported) and for duration (except all-cause, where the trend P is .93 and the top multivariable
cell is unreadable, above). With three categories this is an ordering plus a
trend test, not a tested shape, and the middle frequency category does not separate from the referent
for SCD (0.78, 0.57-1.07). No knee is located; asserting one would need it pinned.
The highest-frequency group holds only n=201, and the studied ranges are frequency 1-7/wk, duration
2-90 min (mean 14.2), temperature 40-100°C (mean 78.9). [@laukkanen2015sauna]
The apparent *optimum* (4-7/wk, >19 min) is therefore the **top studied category edge, not a curve
feature**; do not launder a category edge into a target, as there is no data above the top bin.


**2018:** a restricted cubic spline (Model-2 covariates) «decreased linearly with increasing sauna
sessions from one to seven (P value for non-linearity = 0.932)»; the abstract states it «decreased
linearly with increasing sauna sessions per week with no threshold effect». For weekly duration the
fit was the other way: «statistical tests suggested a fit with a non-linear shape (P for non-linearity
= 0.005)», and the authors leave open whether a curvilinear or linear shape fits better.
[@laukkanen2018sauna]

**Reading:** for frequency the statement is *monotone over 1-7 sessions/week* and no more. A
non-linearity P of 0.932 is a failure to detect curvature, not evidence of a straight line; the top of
the curve is carried by 8 deaths, and the authors themselves say the event count «precluded detailed
assessment of (i) effect modification by relevant clinical characteristics on the associations and (ii)
dose-response relationships of the associations». The spline's 95% band touches or crosses 1.0 around
2-3 sessions/week and again near 7 (read from Fig 3), so even the frequency line is imprecise at both
ends. No data exist above 7/week. The top category remains a studied edge, not a target. Weekly
duration's top category (>45 min/week) is open-ended; Table 1 puts the overall median at 30 min/week
(IQR 15-40) and the 4-7/week group's at 60 (40-90), so data above roughly 90 min/week are sparse, and
the detected non-linearity has no located knee. The duration-quartile plot (Fig 4) is not transcribed
here, so any flattening it shows is not read. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]

## Women — studied, frequency association not detected: insufficient evidence

Estimates from [@laukkanen2018sauna];
the evidence-state verdict is this page's.

- **Frequency:** women at 2-3/wk vs once/week, Model 3 HR **1.00 (0.57-1.74)** (Model 1 0.95,
  0.57-1.57); at 4-7/wk, **0 deaths among 73 women** (vs 24 among 278 in the once-a-week group), not
  estimable. A zero cell points the same way as the association but is crude and unadjusted: at the
  once/week women's crude proportion (24/278) about 6 deaths would be expected among 73, before allowing
  for the younger age of the 4-7 group (Table 1, both sexes), so 0 is informative in direction but
  carries no hazard ratio. *Not detected* here therefore means a null-centred 2-3/wk estimate plus an
  unestimable 4-7/wk cell that leans favourable, not a null across the range.
  [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]
  The authors: «In the analyses by
  gender, there was no statistically significant evidence of associations in women, which could be
  attributed largely to the low event rates in the sauna exposure categories (Table 2). A test of
  interaction showed that the association between sauna bathing frequency and CVD mortality was not
  significantly modified by gender (P for interaction = 0.524).»
- **Weekly duration:** women 16-45 vs <=15 min/week, Model 1 0.53 (0.31-0.89), Model 3 0.61
  (0.35-1.07); >45 min/week Model 3 0.75 (0.31-1.85). Interaction P = 0.314.
- **How to read it:** 63 deaths among 867 women. An interaction test that fails to reject (P = 0.524)
  is **not evidence that the association is equal in women** — it is a low-power test; equally, a
  null-centred estimate with a wide interval is **not evidence of no association**. The evidence state for women is
  **insufficient**, not benefit and not no-effect. The title («Sauna bathing is associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality and improves risk prediction in men and women») and the abstract's wording («strongly,
  inversely, and independently associated with fatal CVD events in middle-aged to elderly males and
  females»; the main-text Conclusions say «may be related to a lower risk») rests on sex-adjusted pooled estimates. For frequency the top category contains only men's
  deaths; the women's 2-3/wk estimate is null-centred and their 4-7/wk cell has no deaths to estimate
  from. For weekly duration the
  women-specific estimates point lower but are imprecise: 16-45 min/week reaches significance only in
  Models 1 and 4 (Model 4 0.51, 0.28-0.91), and >45 min/week rests on 6 deaths (Model 3 0.75,
  0.31-1.85). [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]
- **Older band:** among men aged 53-74 the frequency estimates run the same direction as in men 42-60
  (4-7/wk Model 3 0.39, 0.18-0.84, 8 deaths), within the same cohort and plausibly largely the same men, so this
  is a later-life look at one group, not an independent older-age confirmation. Split at age 65.2 (both
  sexes, Model-2 covariates, 4-7 vs once/week), the younger half gives 0.15 (0.04-0.66) and the older
  half 0.46 (0.20-1.09), not significant; the test for a difference (P 0.287) fails to reject, which is
  not evidence that the association holds equally above 65. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]

## Is the gradient causal, or a healthy-adherer / reverse-causation artifact?

This is the prime suspect for any protective observational gradient: frequent sauna users plausibly
have more leisure, higher income, and better baseline health, and sick people sauna less (sick-quitter
/ reverse causation). [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]] sets the rule: a protective arm that has
survived only covariate adjustment and early-follow-up exclusion (the *weak* checks) is
**unadjudicated, not established**; the strong checks are a referent correction or a genetic /
quasi-experimental test. The rule is written for U/J shapes; this page applies it to a monotone
protective gradient, the same protective-arm question. The sauna gradient is reported to survive the
weak checks in both analyses (the early-exclusion numbers are in unheld supplements) and faced neither
strong one: the referent is once a week (possibly including the 43 non-users, below), not a never-user
group, and there is no genetic instrument. The 2018 authors add that «We could not account for the
longer-term duration and regularity of sauna use prior to the study entry because of the lack of data»,
so people who had cut down before baseline cannot be separated out of the referent. By that rule the
gradient stays unadjudicated. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]

**What the checks cover:**

- **2015 — adjusted for the prime confounders.** The multivariable model adjusts for age, BMI, systolic
  BP, LDL-C, smoking, alcohol, previous MI, type 2 diabetes, **cardiorespiratory fitness**, resting
  heart rate, **physical activity**, and **socioeconomic status**, and the gradient survives the
  adjustment. [@laukkanen2015sauna] Fitness
  and SES are the candidates this page treats as most likely to drive a healthy-adherer pattern; the
  source does not rank confounders.
- **2018 — a thinner model.** Model 3 adjusts for self-reported physical activity (duration per week)
  and an SES index, but **not cardiorespiratory fitness**. The authors call PA confounding «unlikely as
  our analysis accounted for the role of physical activity», leaning on the group's own joint
  fitness-and-sauna papers (not held). [@laukkanen2018sauna]
  So the 2015 fitness-adjustment argument does not carry over to the women or the older band.
- **2018 — baseline differences between frequency groups.** The 4-7 group was younger (60 vs 64),
  more often male (64.4% vs 38.9%) and smoked less (9.3% vs 15.4%; three-group heterogeneity P 0.091).
  Once vs 4-7 per week: SBP 137 vs 135, LDL-C 3.56 vs 3.59 mmol/L, CHD history 28.1% vs 27.8%, type 2
  diabetes 9.9% vs 6.3% (three-group P 0.233); the
  frequent users had *higher* BMI (27.4 vs 28.2), alcohol (median 8.75 vs 24.0 g/week) and energy intake,
  and more PA (7.31 vs 8.48 h/week), and they worked longer days (7.9 vs 8.7 h, three-group P < 0.001). They also differed on housing — family house 23.4% vs 70.7%,
  apartment 60.1% vs 19.0% — and on education, where frequent users had *fewer* academic degrees (10.3%
  vs 2.9%). Annual income
  was 16,970 / 15,337 / 18,377 EUR across once / 2-3 / 4-7 per week (heterogeneity P 0.001, not
  monotone). From «minor differences in annual salary levels» the authors argue «sauna ownership does
  not correlate with financial status in Finland»; housing conditions are one component of the adjusted
  SES index, and on that index «SES did not differ when comparing one vs. four to seven times per week
  frequency groups; SES level was the highest among those using sauna two to three times per week»
  (Table 1: 10.3 vs 10.3). [@laukkanen2018sauna]
  The differences point both ways. Toward a healthy-user pattern: the frequent users were younger,
  more active, smoked less (not significantly), had the highest income and mostly lived in family
  houses. Against one: fewer academic degrees (which may partly track rural vs urban living), longer
  working days, higher BMI and more alcohol. The combined SES index does not separate once from 4-7 per
  week. Covariate adjustment covers some of these; which way the residual runs is not known.
- **Sensitivity analyses addressed reverse causation (both), with the numbers in unheld supplements
  in both.** 2015: associations remained consistent after excluding men who died in the first 5 years
  («eTable 2 in the Supplement») and when restricting follow-up to 10 years («eTable 3 in the
  Supplement») [@laukkanen2015sauna]. 2018:
  «consistent» after excluding the first 5 years (Tables S1-S2) [@laukkanen2018sauna].
  Neither paper's sensitivity estimates are checkable here; both rest on the authors' narrative.


**Subgroups — not evidence about confounding in either paper.** 2015 states that results «were
consistent among subgroups, although associations seem to be somewhat stronger in nonsmokers and in men
with type 2 diabetes and poor cardiorespiratory fitness at baseline», with the subgroup figures in a
supplement not held [@laukkanen2015sauna].
Fitness is already in the 2015 model, so a stronger association among the least fit does not test
fitness confounding either way. Both papers ran within-study interaction tests on
prespecified characteristics that include diabetes. 2015: subgroup analyses «were con- ducted using
interaction tests to assess statistical evidence of any differences in hazard ratios across levels of
prespecified individual-level characteristics, such as age at survey, history of diabetes»
[@laukkanen2015sauna]. 2018: tests across
«pre-specified clinically relevant characteristics» including history of diabetes, with results printed
in Fig 5 [@laukkanen2018sauna]. Side by side:

| Parameter | 2015 (men 42-60) | 2018 (men + women 53-74) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the held text gives | a narrative summary: «associations seem to be somewhat stronger in ... men with type 2 diabetes», after stating results were consistent among subgroups; the test results are in a supplement not held | printed estimates and test: diabetes the one significant modifier of frequency (Fig 5, P for meta-regression 0.021) | NO — a narrative summary of unheld test results vs printed results |
| Contrast | not given in the held text | 4-7 vs once/week: no diabetes 0.26 (0.11-0.62), 146 deaths; diabetes 1.59 (0.33-7.58), 35 deaths in 138 people | NO |
| Outcome | SCD / fatal CHD / fatal CVD (statement covers all) | fatal CVD | partly |
| Population | men, 1984-89 baseline | men + women, 1998-2001 baseline, overlapping men | NO |

No cell reads *yes*, so this cannot be filed as a tension; it is held as a **gap**. In narrative terms the directions
differ (2015: «somewhat stronger» with diabetes; 2018: a point estimate above 1 with diabetes and below 1
without), but the 2015 test results are not held, so whether the two actually conflict cannot be checked
here — a gap, not a finding. The 2018 authors warn that «findings from the subgroup analyses should be
interpreted with caution given the multiple statistical tests of interaction and the low event rates in
these subgroups»; the 2015 tests ran across a similar list of characteristics, so the same caution
plausibly applies to them, though the 2015 authors do not say so. Neither paper's diabetes reading is
offered here as evidence about confounding. [inferred from @laukkanen2015sauna; @laukkanen2018sauna]

**What the checks do not remove:**

- **Residual confounding.** 2015: «potential for residual confounding remains as with all
  observational studies» [@laukkanen2015sauna].
  2018: «Though we accounted for many potential confounders to ensure the validity of our associations,
  there is a potential for residual confounding.» [@laukkanen2018sauna]
  A single cohort of one population cannot exclude an unmeasured disposition (a "healthy lifestyle"
  bundle) that drives both sauna use and survival. Both papers answer the reverse-causation worry with
  consistent subgroup results, and the 2018 paper answers confounding with the salary, SES and
  physical-activity arguments above; those are arguments, not identification.
- **Reverse causation is named, not excluded.** 2018: «It is possible that underlying diagnosed or
  undiagnosed diseases may have an effect on sauna bathing habits, suggesting reverse causality;
  however, our subgroup analyses according to various clinical characteristics were consistent and the
  associations remained robust in several sensitivity analyses, independent of many underlying clinical
  conditions and exclusion of the first 5 years of follow-up.» [@laukkanen2018sauna]
  The sensitivity tables are supplementary and not held; consistent subgroups and a 5-year exclusion
  narrow reverse causation but do not rule out a slow-developing illness that changed habits before
  baseline. The 2018 subgroups were also not uniformly consistent: the diabetes subgroup differed
  (meta-regression P 0.021, below), which sits awkwardly with the *consistent subgroups* defence even
  if it is a chance finding.
- **Exposure measured once, by self-report.** 2015: «We could not correct for regression dilution bias,
  which may have underestimated the associations found» [@laukkanen2015sauna].
  2018: «exposure assessments based on self-administered questionnaires are prone to misclassification
  and recall bias»; habits may have drifted over follow-up, which the authors expect to be «minimal as
  sauna habits are fairly stable in the Finnish population» [@laukkanen2018sauna].
  The same self-report / single-timepoint fragility that [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]
  makes central applies here: non-differential error in one exposure tends to pull estimates toward
  the null, but recall bias, or error correlated with covariates, can move them in either direction, so
  the data alone secure neither the point estimates nor, strictly, the direction.
- **No non-user comparator.** The authors note Finns are accustomed to a sauna bath «regularly at
  least once per week», and the referent is once/week, not none. 2015 excluded its non-users: «Twelve
  men who did not use a sauna were excluded» [@laukkanen2015sauna].
  2018 ran a subsidiary analysis with a 0-1/week group «including participants who did not use sauna at
  all (n = 43)» and reports that against 4-7/week «the associations were similar» (Table S3, not held)
  [@laukkanen2018sauna]. With 43 non-users
  folded into a group of roughly 450-500, that is not a never-user referent. Where those 43 sit in
  the main analysis is unclear: the three main frequency groups (455 + 1028 + 205) add up to the full
  1688, so they may be inside the once/week referent rather than excluded.

**Honest read:** in men the inverse association survives adjustment for fitness/SES (2015) and a
reported early-death exclusion in both papers (numbers in unheld supplements). These are the weak checks
only. Against that: one cohort, one research group, residual confounding, single-timepoint self-report,
a 2018 top category of 8 deaths with men's lower categories not separating from 1 in Model 3, and no fitness
adjustment in the sample that adds women. In women the estimates neither confirm nor refute the
association: the 2-3/wk estimate is null-centred and wide, and the 0/73 cell points the same way but
carries no estimate. That keeps it well short of a causal dose-response the design can support.


## Proposed mechanism — directional, not established

The 2015 authors propose sauna heat loads the cardiovascular system like light-to-moderate exercise:
heart rate may rise up to 100/min in moderate and up to 150/min in more intense sessions, and they describe «proposed
functional improvements» corresponding to exercise benefits in blood pressure and left-ventricular
function. Separately, they cite improved endothelial function «in patients with CHD risk factors and
heart failure», ejection fraction and exercise capacity in heart failure, and a study in which regular
sauna «lowers blood pressure, especially in patients with hypertension».
[@laukkanen2015sauna]
The supporting studies the 2015 paper cites are largely in patients, so their transfer to healthy people
is not shown by them; the 2018 paper's own mechanism discussion is not appraised here. This is a
*mechanism* (marked as such), not an outcome finding: the causal chain from an acute hemodynamic response to a
20-year mortality difference is not demonstrated, and whole-organism compensation could blunt a naive
mechanism. It gives the association directional plausibility, discounted; it does not upgrade it. See
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]] for the BP channel and
[[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]] for the exercise comparator the authors lean on.

## Sauna frequency as a risk-prediction marker — surrogate metrics

Source: [@laukkanen2018sauna]


Adding sauna frequency to a model of age, sex, SBP, diabetes, total cholesterol, HDL-C and smoking moved
the C-index from 0.7716 to 0.7807 (**+0.0091, 0.0022-0.0160**, P = 0.010); categorical NRI over 10-year
CVD-mortality categories (<1%, 1-<5%, >=5%) **4.14% (1.30-6.97%)**; IDI 0.0037 (0.0002-0.0072). These
measure how well a model sorts people, not what sauna does to anyone's risk, and they were estimated in
the same data that fitted the model, with no external validation reported. They are not an effect size
and do not rank the lever. Read against ESC's five criteria for a risk modifier in
[[Risk Modifiers - When Extra Information Changes a Risk Estimate]], it meets the first in-sample only,
reports nothing on public-health impact, and cannot be judged for publication bias with one cohort, so
it does not clear that bar. [inferred from @laukkanen2018sauna]

## What this changes at the margin

For a reasonably healthy person the big rocks (not smoking, weight/adiposity, activity, sleep,
alcohol) dominate; a sauna habit is a low-certainty add-on whose causal size is unknown, not a
substitute for any of them.
The 2015 authors call it «a recommendable health habit, although further studies are needed to confirm our
results in different population settings» [@laukkanen2015sauna]; the same paper calls sauna «a protective factor», causal
language its single-cohort design does not support. The 2018 authors generalize to «Northern European populations» and ask for studies «in
populations who are not accustomed to regular sauna bathing» [@laukkanen2018sauna].

By stratum:

- **Men, middle-aged to older, Finnish-style dry sauna:** the low-confidence inverse association
  applies. In the 2018 Model 3 only the men's 4-7/week category separates from 1 (0.39, 0.18-0.84, 8
  deaths); 2-3/week (0.69, 0.46-1.03) and >45 min/week (0.57, 0.31-1.05) do not, though both exclude 1
  in the age-adjusted Model 1 (0.61, 0.41-0.90; 0.50, 0.27-0.91). The 2018 age split,
  which pools both sexes, gives a non-significant estimate above 65.2 with no detected difference
  between the halves (P 0.287). [@laukkanen2018sauna]
  In the larger 2015 men-only analysis, 2-3/week did separate from once/week for fatal CHD, fatal CVD
  and all-cause death (table above), but not for SCD. [@laukkanen2015sauna]
  No target dose — 4-7/week is the studied edge.
- **Type 2 diabetes:** insufficient evidence, held as a hypothesis. In 2018, a within-study test on a
  pre-specified characteristic, the 4-7 vs once/week estimate (Fig 5, adjusted for the Model-2
  covariates) was 1.59 (0.33-7.58) with diabetes against
  0.26 (0.11-0.62) without (P for meta-regression 0.021; 35 deaths in 138 people with diabetes, of whom
  only 13 were in the 4-7/week group, Table 1); the 2018 authors warn about multiple tests and low event
  counts. [@laukkanen2018sauna]
  The 1.59 therefore rests on very few people at the top frequency; the *insufficient, held as a
  hypothesis* label is this page's.
  The 2015 paper's own prespecified diabetes test is summarized only in words, as «somewhat stronger»
  with diabetes, with its numbers in an unheld supplement, so the two cannot be checked against each
  other. [inferred from @laukkanen2015sauna]
- **Women:** insufficient evidence — studied once, frequency association not detected, duration
  estimates imprecise, too few deaths to tell.
  Absent a detected sex difference (interaction P 0.524), the sex-pooled estimate stays the default
  reference (though all 8 deaths in its top frequency category are men's), and the women's own data neither confirm nor refute it.
- **People prone to orthostatic hypotension:** both papers give the same caution. 2015: «individu- als
  who are prone to orthostatic hypotension should be cautious when sauna bathing because of a possible
  decrease in blood pressure» [@laukkanen2015sauna];
  2018, for the subset of older people so prone: «How- ever, in a specific group of older individuals who
  are prone to orthostatic hypotension, sauna baths should be taken cautiously due to possible sudden drop
  in blood pressures which may occur just after a hot and dry bath [22, 30, 56].»
  [@laukkanen2018sauna]. An asserted caution,
  not a measured harm rate.

**Harm: neither cohort measured it.** 2015: previous studies «suggested that sauna bathing might have
some harmful effects», which the authors set against their own result; they suggest «The possible
differences in results obtained in Finnish studies compared with studies from other countries» (refs 2,
12) «may be at least partly due to the different conditions and temperature of the sauna»; then «Dry sauna bathing seems to be safe», with
cited studies of cardiac patients, the orthostatic caution above, and the cited figure that «Only 1% to
2% of sudden deaths occurred within 24 hours of sauna bathing, with most of them being unintentional,
and alcohol intake together with sauna bathing has been a major contributing factor for these adverse
events» [@laukkanen2015sauna]. 2018: «Regular
Finnish sauna bathing is safe and may have several additional health benefits» (no citation on that
sentence), then a cited sentence that cardiac patients «can usually enjoy sauna bathing without any
significant adverse cardiovascular effects [5, 22, 55]», then its own 4-7/week CVD mortality of 2.7 per
1000 person-years read as «indicating low risk», then the orthostatic caution, followed by the
statement that hypotension during and after a sauna «can be easily prevented by appropriate fluid intake to avoid
dehydration» [@laukkanen2018sauna].
The safety readings rest on cited studies in cardiac patients, plus in 2018 an uncited sentence and the
cohort's CVD death rate, which does not isolate sauna-related events. The 1-2% figure has no comparator
(how many deaths would fall within 24 hours of a sauna by chance among weekly bathers), so it is neither
a harm rate nor a safety demonstration. The specific risks the papers name are orthostatic or
dehydration-related drops in blood pressure and alcohol. The harm state is *no harm measured here*, not
*safe*.

**Labelled practical default (standard practice, not trial-tested):** for someone with access to a
Finnish-style sauna, using it is a reasonable low-cost habit if they enjoy it, with no target frequency
or duration. What was studied, as a description and not a target: 1-7 sessions/week of a hot dry sauna
at a mean of about 76-79°C, with sessions averaging about 14 minutes in 2015 (median 13 minutes in 2018,
Table 1). Skip alcohol before and during, drink fluids, and take care standing up afterwards if prone to
dizziness on standing. For someone already saunaing it is not a lever to *drop*. It does not
earn a high rank on the intervention hierarchy, and confidence stays **low** by construction.

## Gaps (acquisition targets, not silences)

- **No replication cohort** outside KIHD — a second independent prospective cohort is the single most
  valuable missing source; until one is held, the wiki's evidence here is un-triangulated. No specific
  independent cohort is named yet; the one named review candidate is the systematic review by
  authors outside the KIHD group, above, whose outcomes are unverified.
- **Women: insufficient evidence** — one KIHD sample of 867 women, 63 deaths, none at 4-7/wk; the 2015
  authors asked for female cohorts and the 2018 sample is too small to answer. Needs a cohort with
  enough events in women, outside the KIHD group.
- **No all-cause mortality beyond men 42-60** — the 2018 analysis reports fatal CVD only.
- **No non-Finnish population** — cannot separate the sauna exposure from Finnish lifestyle/genetics.
- **No RCT** — no randomized heat-exposure trial on hard endpoints (feasibility aside, none is held).
- **Participant overlap between the two KIHD analyses is unreported**, so how much of the 2018 men's
  signal re-observes the 2015 men is unknown. For any man in both, a CVD death between the 1998-2001
  visit and the end of 2011 is counted in both analyses (2015 deaths ran «by the end of 2011»; 2018
  follow-up ran to 2015), so some of the same events, not only the same people, likely recur.
- **Transport to other sauna types is unestablished** — results «cannot be directly applied to steam rooms, hot tubs,
  and some other types of saunas» that run cooler and do not allow water on the rocks. The exposure is
  specifically the hot dry Finnish sauna (mean 79°C in 2015, 75.9°C in 2018). [@laukkanen2015sauna] [@laukkanen2018sauna]
