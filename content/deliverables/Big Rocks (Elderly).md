> **Who this guide is for.** This guide is for an obese adult around 70 who still lives independently:
> often with high blood pressure, prediabetes or diabetes, fatty liver, lower bone density and some loss
> of muscle, and often on several medicines. It is not written for frail people or care-home residents;
> several of its numbers do not transfer to them, and the guide says where. It appraises lifestyle levers
> and the standard drugs as comparators. The prescriber, not this guide, chooses, doses, stops or
> combines medicines.

## Rank by what this age loses: function, falls, fractures and independence

- At 70 three outcomes matter most: **staying independent, avoiding a hip fracture and keeping
  function** [[Rating Outcome Importance]].
- Balance-challenging exercise is the best-tested lever: **about a quarter fewer falls (rate ratio
  0.77, 0.71-0.83), high certainty** [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].
- Raised blood pressure is still worth lowering: in trials, lowering it cut major cardiovascular
  events up to 84, and slightly reduced dementia [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].
- Strength and power training comes next. Its trials show gains in function and lean mass, not fewer falls, and
  only observational studies link it to death
  [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].
- For bone, exercise programmes cut major fractures in controlled trials. They overlap the falls
  programmes, so the same training serves both [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]. Routine vitamin
  D or calcium is not a fracture lever for someone at home who is not deficient
  [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]].
- With prediabetes, a lifestyle programme cut diabetes in one large trial, at least as well at 60 and
  over [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]. At high cardiovascular risk, a Mediterranean
  diet cut cardiovascular events in one trial repaired with propensity scores (men 55-80, women 60-80)
  [[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]].
- Weight loss cut mortality in trials of mostly middle-aged adults
  [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]. In older adults with knee osteoarthritis, diet plus
  exercise improved function; diet alone did not beat exercise alone. No trial in this guide's evidence
  tested weight loss on falls or fractures at this age, so at 70 it is **worth doing only if muscle is
  defended** [[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]].
- A salt substitute cut stroke and death in rural China, where most salt is added at home. Elsewhere,
  only its blood-pressure fall has been shown to transfer [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

Lower down sit levers resting on weaker study designs, the drug choices, levers that turn harmful at
this age or in some conditions, and levers that add little:

- The protein floor rests on consensus and muscle markers [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]]; the steps
  plateau rests on cohorts [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].
- Without vascular disease, the evidence for starting a statin weakens after 75; at 71-75 the decision
  rests on absolute risk [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]].
- **Aspirin started for prevention is the clearest lever that turns from help to harm**: modelled net
  life-years turn negative for starting at 70-79 [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]].
  Two narrower cautions rest on advice rather than shown harm: limit protein in advanced kidney
  disease, and avoid brisk walking at high fall risk.
- Supplements add little [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]], and multidomain
  memory programmes did not show a reduction in dementia (wide interval)
  [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]]. These two mark the ceiling, and
  reaching it is itself a result.

**A fall matters less than what it leads to.** The Canadian Task Force review scored health states from
0 (full health) to 1 (death). A fall in the past year cost about 0.09 (-0.04 to 0.22, low certainty).
**A hip fracture cost 0.53 in the first three months (moderate certainty) and still 0.16 at twelve
months (high certainty).** Admission to long-term care scored a median of 1, equivalent to death. That
figure comes from one time-trade-off study of 194 people with a mean age of 83, low certainty
[@pillay2024falls].
**This guide therefore ranks fracture and lost independence above the fall itself.** Trials count
falls, fractures and deaths; almost none measure how long function holds before it drops. A trial that
reports survival can say nothing about what a person most wants [[Rating Outcome Importance]].

**Length of life still counts, but fewer years remain for a benefit to build up.** In the European
Society of Cardiology's lifetime model, 1 mmol/L lower LDL cholesterol gives a non-smoking man aged
45-49 roughly 0.6 to 1.6 extra years free of cardiovascular disease, and someone aged 90 or over
about 0.0 to 0.3 years. These are modelled figures, not trial results
[@esc2021] ->
[[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

**This guide's advice stops where frailty begins.** Vermeiren pooled 31 articles on 13 outcomes in
community-dwelling adults aged 65 and over (158,764 people across all outcomes). **Frail people died
sooner than robust ones (RR 2.01, 1.82-2.22)**, prefrail people sat in between (RR 1.47, 1.32-1.62),
and frailty also predicted institutionalisation and fractures
[@vermeiren2016frailty]. These
cohorts show prognosis, not cause. Frail people and care-home residents carry a higher baseline risk,
and most of the trials behind the estimates below did not test them. This paragraph states that limit
once; later sections point back to it [[Frailty]].

**This guide grades each lever on its own outcome and does not add them into one score.** The reader
chooses how much staying independent should count against living longer
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

## Train balance hard, about three hours a week, and keep it up

A Cochrane review pooled 108 randomised trials of exercise in people living in the community, mean
age 76. **Exercise reduces the rate of falls by about a quarter, with high certainty: rate ratio 0.77
(0.71-0.83).** At the review's illustrative control rate of 850 falls per 1000 people per year, that is
195 (144-246) fewer falls per 1000 per year. **Fewer people fall at all**: fallers RR 0.85 (0.81-0.89),
also high certainty [@sherrington2019].

Trial control groups fell more often than older people in general, and the relative effect did not
differ by age (75 and over vs under 75) or by baseline fall risk
[@sherrington2019]. **A lower-risk
70-year-old should therefore expect fewer falls averted, but not none** ->
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

### Balance is the active ingredient; strength is an add-on

Balance and functional exercise (gait, balance and task training) reduces falls: rate ratio 0.76
(0.70-0.81), high certainty. **Resistance training on its own has not been shown to reduce falls**:
1.14 (0.67-1.97), five small trials, very low certainty, an interval that fits benefit or harm
[@sherrington2019]. That is insufficient evidence, not a
null. Resistance training therefore goes on top of balance work, not in its place.
*High-challenge balance training* here means exercise that moves the body's centre of mass, narrows the
base of support and keeps hand support to a minimum. Examples are reaching, shifting weight from leg to
leg and stepping up
[@sherrington2017falls].

### How much: a labelled default, not a tested dose

**Labelled default, for community-dwelling people only: high-challenge balance training inside about 3
hours a week of prescribed exercise in total, kept up indefinitely.** In a meta-regression of 61
community trials, 3 or more hours a week gave a ratio of rate ratios of 0.77 (0.65-0.91), and
high-challenge balance content 0.85 (0.73-1.00). The 3 hours count all prescribed exercise, and the
authors add that benefits are lost when people stop
[@sherrington2017falls].

Both are comparisons between trials, which the authors call potentially confounded, and **the authors
made no comparison above 3 hours**. High-challenge balance under 3 hours still cut falls in the model
(0.79, 0.71-0.88) [@sherrington2017falls],
so 3 hours is a level to build toward, not a line below which effort is wasted.

**The trials found mostly minor harms, but watched for them poorly.** Adverse events were reported in
27 trials and monitored closely in both arms in only one. The two serious events, a pelvic stress
fracture and a hernia operation, came from a single trial
[@sherrington2019]. Delivery formats are on
[[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]] and
[[Aquatic Exercise vs Land-Based Exercise for Balance in Older Adults]].

### Fractures: a smaller and less certain signal

Exercise may reduce fall-related fractures: RR 0.73 (0.56-0.95) across 10 trials, low certainty. That
is 47 instead of 64 per 1000 over 4 to 42 months, **about 17 fewer fall-related fractures per 1000**
[@sherrington2019]. Exercise that loads bone to raise
its density works by a separate route, covered in the bone section.

### Walking alone, and screening everyone's eyes, are not falls prevention

Walking programmes have insufficient evidence of benefit for falls: rate ratio 1.14 (0.66-1.97), 2
trials, 441 participants, very low certainty, the point estimate on the harm side
[@sherrington2019]. **The 2017 authors advise that
walking not be anyone's only fall-prevention exercise, and that high-risk older people not be told to
walk briskly**; walking can sit on top of other falls exercise
[@sherrington2017falls]. The
steps advice later in this guide targets mortality; it does not substitute for falls exercise
.

**Universal vision screening and treatment, offered to everyone rather than to people with poor sight,
increased both fallers and fractures**, with moderate certainty
[@pillay2024falls].

### Outside the community, the effect has not been shown

Exercise alone gave a falls rate ratio of 0.90 (0.72-1.12) in care homes, 1.16 (0.88-1.52) after
hospital discharge and 0.74 (0.42-1.32) after stroke
[@sherrington2017falls]. In prefrail
or frail people, physical activity gave 0.80 (0.51-1.26) for falls across 7 studies, very low
certainty, though mobility improved
[@racey2021frailtypa]. **Each is
insufficient evidence: not shown to work, not shown not to work, and not shown safe** -> [[Frailty]].

## Lift for strength and power, and eat at least the protein floor that feeds it

A person needs strength and power to get up from a chair, climb stairs and keep living alone.
**Resistance training (RT) beats none on most function and muscle outcomes after 60, and a short,
low-volume programme is enough to start (low certainty).** Power training, done with fast movements,
adds a further gain on function tests, with high certainty. The protein numbers that go with it are
consensus floors set on muscle markers [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].

### Start with two short sessions a week; more sets did not add mobility or muscle size

Radaelli pooled 151 randomised trials of supervised RT in untrained adults aged 60 and over (median
age 68.8). Most volume bands beat no exercise on most outcomes. The lowest band, 5 to under 18 lower-limb
sets a week (median 12), may improve the timed up-and-go test (stand, walk, turn, sit): standardised
mean difference (SMD) -1.20 (-1.57 to -0.82), low certainty. **Except for strength, higher volume added
no benefit on the outcomes examined**, and fast walking speed was the one function test where higher
volume did better [@radaelli2024rtvol].

**Labelled default:** two sessions a week, two sets of each of three lower-limb exercises, under 20
minutes, at a typical trial load of about 80% of the one-repetition maximum (1RM), progressing from
there. The authors translated it from the low-band median; it is not a tested dose. For sarcopenia or
frailty the authors say the most effective dose «remains to be determined»
[@radaelli2024rtvol] -> [[Frailty]].

### Add power training: moving fast beats lifting heavy on function

El Hadouchi pooled 15 trials (583 people, mean age over 65) comparing power training with conventional
strength training. **Power training improves function tests (walks, chair rises, balance) more: SMD
0.43 (0.23 to 0.62), high certainty**, a small-to-moderate gain over a comparator that itself works.
**None of these trials counted falls**, so power training is a lever on function tests, not a proven
falls lever [@elhadouchi2022] ->
[[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]].

With sarcopenic obesity, RT alone (9 trials, mean age 68, about 12 weeks) improved body composition,
SMD 0.35 (0.12 to 0.57), moderate certainty, and may improve performance, SMD 0.36 (0.03 to 0.69), low
certainty. **Lower body fat carried the gain; muscle mass did not change significantly**
[@poloferrero2025] ->
[[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]].

### Eat at least 1.2 g protein per kg body weight a day while training

PROT-AGE, a consensus endorsed by four geriatric societies, recommends that healthy older adults eat at
least 1.0 to 1.2 g of protein per kg body weight a day, and advises 1.2 g for those exercising or
otherwise active, which includes anyone doing RT. Illness raises the floor to 1.2-1.5 g/kg; severe
illness or marked malnutrition may need up to 2.0 g/kg. Per meal, PROT-AGE puts the threshold at 25-30
g of protein [@bauer2013].

**These floors rest on nitrogen balance, lean mass and short-term muscle protein synthesis. No trial
shows that reaching them changes fractures, disability or survival.** Which kilogram to count for an
obese person is unresolved; the arithmetic is on [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]]
[inferred from @bauer2013]. **These are floors: PROT-AGE states no upper
limit for older adults with healthy kidneys.** For a light person the per-meal threshold, not the
per-kg floor, sets the daily total: at 50 kg, three meals of 25-30 g come to 75-90 g
a day, 1.5-1.8 g/kg. No trial in this guide's evidence tests what a light person needs.

**With established kidney disease, the floor turns into a cap.**
With eGFR below 30 and not on
dialysis, PROT-AGE limits protein to 0.8 g per kg of ideal body weight a day; with eGFR 30-60 it calls
more than 0.8 g/kg safe if GFR is monitored twice a year
[@bauer2013]. With normal kidneys, trials
averaging 1.81 g/kg, in adults aged 23 to 72, found no difference in GFR change (very low certainty).
That is no harm detected on a surrogate, not proof of safety
[@devries2018]. **Above about 2 g/kg
after 65 the evidence is insufficient** -> [[Protein Intake and Kidney Function]].

## Get out of the least-active band; the steps plateau arrives earlier at this age

A harmonised meta-analysis of 15 cohorts (n=47,471; 3013 deaths) found that **mortality falls with
daily steps and then levels off**. Against the lowest quarter, the hazard ratio was 0.60 (0.51-0.71) for
the second quarter and 0.47 (0.39-0.57) for the top; quarter medians spanned 3553 to 10,901 steps a day.
**Adults aged 60 and over reached the plateau at about 6000-8000 steps a day**, against about
8000-10,000 under 60 (age interaction p=0.012)
[@paluch2022].

The plateau is read off a spline with no confidence interval, so it is a region, not a cutpoint. These
are comparisons between people who walk different amounts, not the effect of adding steps, and **the
gain lies in leaving the bottom of the distribution** ->
[[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

Fitness predicts survival. In cohorts of healthy men and women, each 1 MET of higher fitness went with
13% lower mortality, RR 0.87 (0.84-0.90). That figure comes from a dated 2009 meta-analysis, which the
umbrella review below rates critically low on AMSTAR 2
[@kodama2009]. The umbrella review
grades the fitness-mortality link LOW certainty, mainly for indirectness, because the studies were
**mostly men (1,858,274 male against 180,202 female participants)**. It attributes nearly half the
variation in fitness to genetics
[@lang2024crf]. **Fitness is a
predictor that training moves, not a proven lever on death** ->
[[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]].

### Start gradually; with diabetes, get cleared first

**Exercise-related cardiac risk is very low but rises with age**, and the excess concentrates in
sedentary people with heart disease doing unaccustomed strenuous exercise. The AHA advises ostensibly
healthy, inactive starters to begin slowly, usually with walking
[@franklin2020acute].
Under the ACSM consensus, an inactive person without cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, diabetes
or symptoms can start light-to-moderate exercise directly; **a person with one of those gets clearance
first** [@riebe2015screen]. Diabetes is common
in this group -> [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]].

### A painful knee is a reason to move, at lower impact

Goh pooled 68 trials on knee or hip osteoarthritis pain. Trials whose participants averaged 60 or over
showed a smaller pain benefit from exercise (SMD 0.44, 0.34-0.55) than trials averaging under 60
(1.32, 0.79-1.86). **In trials of older participants the benefit was smaller but did not reverse.** Obesity showed no sign of
blunting it (BMI >=30: 0.56 vs 0.46, P 0.78), though a study-level subgroup cannot rule a difference out
[@goh2019]. For an obese beginner, a
lower-impact mode such as stationary cycling is the labelled default ->
[[Exercise Therapy for Osteoarthritis Pain and Function]], [[Stationary Cycling for Knee Osteoarthritis]].

## Lose weight only while defending muscle

**Intentional weight loss stays a lever around 70, but only when resistance training and the protein
floor come with it.** At this age, the trials in this guide's evidence tested weight loss on function
only in knee osteoarthritis (IDEA, below). None tested falls or fractures, and the mortality trials
were mostly middle-aged. This condition is a reason to pair the levers, not evidence that weight loss
harms a 70-year-old.

### Excess weight still predicts death at 70, less steeply

Among never-smokers without chronic disease, with the first 5 years excluded, each 5 kg/m2 above a BMI
of 25 went with a death rate **about a fifth higher at 70-89 (HR 1.21, 1.17-1.25)**, against half again
higher at 35-49 (HR 1.52). This is an observational association
[@globalbmi2016] -> [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]].
Across 54 RCTs in obese adults, **weight-reducing diets cut all-cause mortality (RR 0.82, 0.71-0.95; 34
trials reporting deaths): six fewer deaths per 1000 (2 to 10) over a median two years**.
Cardiovascular events did not fall significantly (RR 0.93, 0.83-1.04). Most trials recruited
middle-aged adults; 14 of the 54 had a mean or median age of 60 or more
[@ma2017] ->
[[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].

### The one trial at this age on function: diet plus exercise, in knee osteoarthritis

IDEA randomised 454 overweight or obese adults aged 55 and over (mean 66) with knee osteoarthritis for
18 months. **Diet plus exercise gave the best function**: WOMAC function 4.29 points better than
exercise alone (2.07-6.50) and 3.30 better than diet alone (1.09-5.51), and a 6-minute walk 21.3 m
farther than exercise alone. Diet alone did not differ significantly from exercise alone on function.
In a post hoc analysis, 38% of the diet-plus-exercise arm had little or no pain at 18 months, against
20-22% in the other arms. Grouped by weight actually lost, regardless of arm (a non-randomised
comparison), people losing 10% or more improved function and pain more than those losing 5-9.9% or
under 5% [@messier2013idea] ->
[[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]].

The same trial shows the muscle cost: **4.2-4.7 kg (8-9%) of whole-body lean mass lost in the diet
arms**, the larger loss in the diet-plus-exercise arm, whose programme included strength training.
Lean mass as a share of body weight still rose 2-3 points, because fat fell further
[@messier2013idea].

### Pair the diet with resistance training and the protein floor

**Dieters who added RT kept 0.82 kg more lean body mass (0.36-1.27)** in six RCTs of older adults
(mean age over 57), without changing fat or weight loss
[@sardeli2018] ->
[[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]]. **Dieters who ate at least 1.0 g protein per kg a
day kept 0.83 kg more lean mass (0.47-1.19)**, in the trials that classified protein by g/kg among 20
RCTs of adults aged 50 and over
[@kim2016] ->
[[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]]. Both measure lean mass, a surrogate; **no trial in this
guide's evidence shows that the extra kilogram keeps function**.

### Pace is untested at this age; amount and rate interact in predicting regain

On pace, the evidence at this age is **insufficient**. Turicchi's between-study meta-regression
covered 43 studies, limited to those where regain occurred (median age 44.8; study medians 34.5
to 70.6). Once amount lost was in the model, rate alone no longer predicted regain (p=0.42), while
amount did (p=0.001). **But the rate-by-amount interaction was positively associated with regain
(p=0.042)**, though after BMI adjustment it fell to a trend (p=0.09). The authors conclude that amount
and rate were significant, interacting factors in regain. **For a small loss the pace mattered little,
and the larger the planned loss, the more the pace predicted regain.** They therefore suggest a more
conservative method for a substantial loss. That reading comes from between-study data, not a trial of
pace. In the model without rate, each extra kg lost went with about 0.5 kg regained (0.25-0.74)
[@turicchi2019regain].

In Franz's review, a reduced-energy diet and/or weight-loss drugs took off a mean 5-8.5 kg by six
months, then levelled off. **Trials running to 48 months kept 3-6 kg off**, in younger adults
[@franz2007] ->
[[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]].

### The obesity drugs take off more weight; lean loss and regain come with diet too

Against lifestyle modification alone, at one year, **tirzepatide took off 14.9% of body weight
(-16.0 to -13.9) and subcutaneous semaglutide 9.8% (-10.6 to -9.1)**, moderate to high certainty. Lean
mass fell 8.3% (-12.9 to -3.7) with tirzepatide and 5.8% (-8.7 to -2.9) with semaglutide, moderate
certainty. Nong cites a review of 37 studies in which weight returned at about 0.4 kg a month after
stopping treatment. Semaglutide's mortality RR of 0.81 (0.72-0.93) comes from trials largely in people
at high cardiovascular risk. No drug could be tested by age
[@nong2026obesity].

**On diet and on drugs alike, people lose lean mass and regain weight; each was measured in its own
population.** IDEA's diet arms lost 8-9% of lean mass as a change within each arm. The drug figures are
differences over lifestyle, so each drug arm's total loss is that figure plus what the lifestyle arm
lost. The two sets cannot be ranked from these numbers: **the evidence in this guide shows neither that
the drugs cost more muscle than diet nor that they cost less**. Dieters regain too (Turicchi, Franz,
above). Neither mortality figure transfers unchanged to a lower-risk 70-year-old: diet's 0.82 comes
from mostly middle-aged trials, and semaglutide's 0.81 from people at high cardiovascular risk
 -> [[Comparing Obesity Drugs]], [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]].

For fatty liver, EASL ties **7-10% sustained loss to less liver inflammation and 10% or more to less
fibrosis**, largely on one histology trial with no age-specific data
[@easl2024] ->
[[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]. **Unintentional weight loss is a warning sign, not a lever.** For
intentional loss without RT or extra protein, the evidence on function at 70 is insufficient
 -> [[Frailty]].

## Treat blood pressure, glucose and lipids by absolute risk, not by age

**The benefit from each risk-factor lever depends on absolute risk, not on age alone.** Risk climbs
with age, so where the relative effect holds, the same treatment prevents more events at 70 than at 50
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. The prescriber decides drug choice, targets, doses
and stopping.

### Blood pressure: the absolute benefit holds up to 84, the relative effect shrinks a little

Rahimi split 358,707 people from 51 trials by age. Per 5 mmHg lower systolic pressure,
major cardiovascular events fell **0.91 (0.88-0.95) at 65-74 and 0.91 (0.87-0.96) at 75-84**, adjusted
p-interaction 0.050 across all bands [@rahimi2021bpage].
The authors find the relative reduction diminishing with age while the absolute reduction appeared
largest in the oldest groups. At 85 and over the estimate is 0.99 (0.87-1.12), from about 1000 events
over a median 2.8 years [@rahimi2021bpage]. **On its own,
that band gives insufficient evidence, not evidence of no benefit**.

The trials under-represented people who are frail, multimorbid, on many medicines or living in
institutions, and the authors call the reach to them uncertain
[@rahimi2021bpage]. **A frail person and a clinician weigh
starting treatment as an individual trade-off** [[Frailty]]. Absolute-risk bands are on
[[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]; SPRINT's harms are on
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### Salt substitute: hard outcomes in rural China, after a kidney and potassium check

SSaSS randomised 600 villages in rural China (20,995 people with prior stroke, or aged 60 or more with
high blood pressure; mean age 65.4; 72.6% after a stroke) to a salt substitute (\~25% potassium chloride)
or regular salt for 4.74 years. **Stroke fell to 0.86 (0.77-0.96) and death to 0.88 (0.82-0.95), about
5.3 fewer deaths per 1000 person-years** [@neal2021],
from an extreme baseline.

**How far the result transfers beyond rural China is open.** Without SSaSS, all-cause mortality lost
statistical significance, and no non-Chinese trial contributed mortality data
[@lai2026salt]. Lai judges the evidence of
very limited generalisability beyond populations following Chinese dietary patterns with high
discretionary salt use and home food preparation
[@lai2026salt]. **The hard-outcome figures
belong to that Chinese pattern; the blood-pressure fall transfers more readily**
-> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**Check first.** Lai advises ruling out kidney problems and hyperkalaemia risk before recommending a
substitute [@lai2026salt]. That means no
substitute with kidney disease, or where anyone eating the food takes a potassium-sparing drug or
potassium supplements; check first with diabetes or an ACE inhibitor or ARB
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]. Brand's Cochrane review adds low blood sodium as a concern,
particularly in older people and thiazide users, though as a concern rather than an observed harm
[@brand2022lsss]. Serious hyperkalaemia in
SSaSS was 1.04 (0.80-1.37), but the trial did not test potassium routinely, so **that result is
insufficient evidence, not shown safe**.

### Prediabetes: the lifestyle programme worked at least as well in the oldest group

The Diabetes Prevention Program's lifestyle arm aimed for at least 7% weight loss and 150 minutes of
activity a week. In 648 participants aged 60 or more it **cut diabetes incidence by 71% (51 to 83),
from 10.8 to 3.1 cases per 100 person-years**, against 48% (27 to 63) at 25-44. The age test reached
significance on the lifestyle-versus-metformin comparison; metformin cut incidence by 11% (-33 to 41)
at 60 and over [@knowler2002]. **At this
age the drug is no substitute for the lifestyle lever.**

Over 21 years the middle-aged cohort showed no fall in cardiovascular events in either arm. The
authors name three things that may have diluted the effects: group lifestyle advice to everyone,
statin and blood-pressure drugs taken outside the study, and a shift from study metformin toward
metformin taken outside it [@goldberg2022] ->
[[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].

### Mediterranean diet: one trial cut cardiovascular events at 55-80

PREDIMED, in Spain, assigned 7447 men aged 55 to 80 and women aged 60 to 80 at high cardiovascular
risk to a Mediterranean diet with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts, against advice to cut fat. No arm
restricted calories. **The diet arms cut heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death by 30%: HR 0.70
(0.55-0.89), an absolute 1.7 to 2.1 percentage points over about 5 years.** The fall in stroke drove
it (0.58, 0.42-0.82); death from any cause did not fall (0.98, 0.77-1.24). The first report was
withdrawn: 425 household members had been assigned without randomisation, and one site assigned by
clinic. The authors re-analysed with propensity scores and leave the question of transfer to
lower-risk people to further research
[@estruch2018]. **It is a randomised trial with a
statistical repair, not a clean randomised contrast** ->
[[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]].

### LDL: a statin's case weakens after 75 without known vascular disease

CTT reported its age subgroups with 99% intervals. With vascular disease the benefit held past 75: 0.85
(99% CI 0.73-0.98) per 1.0 mmol/L lower LDL. **Without it, the estimates were 0.84 (99% CI 0.70-1.01)
at 71-75 and 0.92 (99% CI 0.73-1.16) over 75**, and the relative reduction shrank with age (trend
p=0.05; 0.03 without the heart-failure and dialysis trials)
[@ctt2019]. USPSTF calls the evidence for starting at
76 or older insufficient [@uspstf2022].

**For a statin started after 75 without vascular disease, the evidence is insufficient, not a shown
absence of benefit**, and at 71-75 the decision rests on absolute risk
[[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]. The benefit builds over years: Reith cites
CTT's 9% fewer major vascular events in year 1 (99% CI 3-15) against 24% over years 1 to 5 or more
(95% CI 21-26) [@reith2022muscle]. Muscle aches are on
[[Statin Muscle Symptoms and the Nocebo Effect]].

### Aspirin: do not start it for prevention

**USPSTF recommends against starting low-dose aspirin for primary prevention at 60 or older (Grade D),
with moderate certainty of no net benefit**; bleeding risk rises with age
[@uspstf2022aspirin]. Across 13 trials aspirin cut
cardiovascular events (HR 0.89, 0.84-0.94), raised major bleeding (HR 1.43, 1.30-1.56), and was not
associated with fewer deaths (HR 0.94, 0.88-1.01; 95% credible intervals)
[@zheng2019aspirin]
[[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]. The prescriber decides whether to stop
aspirin already taken.

## Protect bone by loading it and preventing the fall, not with routine pills

**Exercise programmes cut major osteoporotic fractures (hip, spine, wrist, upper arm) by about a
quarter: rate ratio 0.75 (95% CI 0.59-0.94)**, across 11 controlled trials in adults over 50 on
average, most of them women-only [@hoffmann2022].
The trials mixed bone-loading and falls-prevention programmes, so **the figure does not show how much
of the benefit runs through stronger bone and how much through fewer falls**. One research group wrote
the review
-> [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]].

**For a person living at home who is not deficient, routine vitamin D or calcium is not a fracture
lever.** Vitamin D alone did not lower hip fracture: RR 1.14 (0.98-1.32). Vitamin D plus calcium did,
RR 0.84 (0.72-0.97), with reductions somewhat greater in older people in institutions
[@yao2019]. In the one large community trial (WHI),
in postmenopausal women, the combination gave hip fracture HR 0.88 (0.72-1.08), 0.14 percentage
points fewer over 7 years. One trial of vitamin D alone found fewer total fractures (risk
difference -2.26%, -4.53 to 0.00)
[@kahwati2018]. In VITAL, adults
chosen by age, not deficiency, did not show fewer total fractures: HR 0.98 (0.89-1.08)
[@leboff2022].

The combination raised kidney stones, RR 1.18 (1.04-1.35), moderate certainty
[@kahwati2018], and calcium pills
carry a contested signal of more heart attacks, RR 1.24 (1.07-1.45), independent of dietary calcium
intake [@bolland2011]. Food calcium is on
[[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]]. A measured deficiency is different:
test, then replete -> [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]].

**Hormone therapy protects bone, but starting it at about 70 adds harm.** In WHI (postmenopausal
women aged 50-79), oestrogen plus progestin cut hip fractures, HR 0.66
(nominal 95% CI 0.45-0.98; 0.33-1.33 after
adjustment for multiple testing)
[@rossouw2002]. Started after 65, it
added 23 dementia cases per 10,000 person-years, and NAMS calls use in older women not appropriate to
reduce heart disease or dementia
[@nams2022] ->
[[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]].

**With low bone density, swap repetitive loaded spinal flexion for extension.** A safety review advises
people with osteoporosis to avoid specific, repetitive forward-flexion exercises and focus on spinal
extension, while stressing that evidence of flexion causing vertebral fractures is limited
[@kunutsor2018adverse].
The rule covers programmed exercise such as sit-ups, not everyday bending ->
[[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]].

## Lower blood pressure, check hearing; expect little from multidomain programmes for memory

**Blood-pressure treatment slightly reduced dementia.** In five placebo-controlled trials (28,008
people, mean age 69.1), blood pressure fell by a mean of about 10/4 mmHg and dementia OR was 0.87
(0.75-0.99). Dementia developed in 2.9% of the active groups and 3.3% of the placebo groups, over a
median 4.3 years. Age did not modify the effect
[@peters2022bp]. That is about 4 fewer cases per
1000 treated -> [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]].

**Multidomain programmes at this age did not show a reduction in dementia, and the interval is wide.**
MAPT tested cognitive training with diet and activity education; preDIVA tested nurse-led vascular
care. Their pooled data on 5,205 people over 70 gave HR 0.98 (0.80-1.21), and no responder subgroup
was found [@coley2025] ->
[[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]]. The MIND-diet null is at
[[MIND Diet - Observational Benefit vs Randomized Null]].

**Hearing aids help hearing; the one trial was null on cognition overall.** ACHIEVE randomised 977
people aged 70-84 with untreated hearing loss; 3-year global cognition differed by 0.002 SD (-0.077 to
0.081) [@lin2023achieve]. A higher-risk subgroup
declined 48% less, but a review sharing the trial's lead investigator calls it «a pre-planned but secondary analysis»
[@yu2024hearing]. Treat
dementia protection as an unconfirmed bet -> [[Hearing Loss and Dementia]].

**The cohorts do not settle whether activity itself prevents dementia.** Active people show about a
fifth less dementia (RR 0.80, 0.77-0.84). In Iso-Markku's pool the association held at 20 or more
years of follow-up (RR 0.79, 0.71-0.87), in cohorts starting in midlife (mean baseline age 50.5)
[@isomarkku2022]. Kivimaki's
individual-participant analysis found the association gone after 10 years, including over 60
(HR 1.04, 0.90-1.19) [@kivimaki2019inactivity]. **That
adds up to insufficient evidence for a direct effect, not proof of no effect**; activity keeps its rank
for cardiovascular reasons ->
[[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]].

**Long sleep looks like a marker of ill-health; short sleep may be a cause.** Across all ages, short
sleep went with slightly higher mortality (RR 1.12, 1.06-1.18); long sleep went with RR 1.46
(1.30-1.64) in cohorts aged 60 or over. The authors leave cause open, but read indirect evidence as
suggesting that sustained sleep deprivation may harm health, while long sleep may be a powerful
additional marker of ill-health [@cappuccio2010]
-> [[Sleep Duration and Mortality]].

## Stop optimising once these levers are in place: the ceiling and the open gaps

**Vitamin D and fish oil are not a lever for a person who is not deficient.** VITAL, a 2x2 factorial
trial, gave vitamin D3 2000 IU a day and marine omega-3 to 25,871 US adults (men >=50, women >=55)
chosen by age. Both missed their primary cancer and cardiovascular endpoints
[@manson2019vitd].

Omega-3 cut total heart attacks, a secondary endpoint, HR 0.72 (0.59-0.90)
[@manson2019n3]. USPSTF grades multivitamins insufficient
evidence (grade I) and beta carotene a harm (grade D)
[@uspstf2022supp]. **From 75, the Endocrine
Society conditionally suggests low-dose vitamin D** for a possible mortality benefit, RR 0.96
(0.93-1.00), about 6 fewer deaths per 1000 (from 11 fewer to 0 more)
[@demay2024vitamind] ->
[[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

Levothyroxine for a mildly raised TSH did not improve quality of life, SMD -0.11 (-0.25 to 0.03)
[@feller2018thyroid] ->
[[Levothyroxine for Subclinical Hypothyroidism]]. Single foods and diet patterns beyond PREDIMED refine
the levers above rather than add a new one ->
[[Food Groups and Health Outcomes - A Dose-Response Matrix]],
[[Dietary Patterns and All-Cause Mortality]]. **The remaining levers are small, and saying so is the
result:** it tells the person they can stop optimising ->
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

### Smoking goes to the top if present; heavy drinking likely does too

**Smoking, where present, outranks every lever in this guide.** Current smokers die at about three
times the rate of never-smokers: HR 3.0 (99% CI 2.7-3.3) in women and 2.8 (2.4-3.1) in men. Quitting
at 55-64 still gained about 4 years [@jha2013].
The data stop at a quit age of 64, so they give insufficient evidence on the size of the gain at 70,
not evidence of no gain -> [[Smoking and Mortality]].

Heavy drinking is a dominant lever on the same logic, but no source in this guide gives an
age-specific magnitude. After
adjustment for abstainer biases, low-volume drinkers showed no lower mortality than abstainers: RR 0.97
(0.88-1.07) [@stockwell2016]. In Chinese men,
Mendelian randomisation found stroke risk rising with intake, with no protective lower arm
[@millwood2019]. **The J-shaped
curve gives no reason to start or keep drinking for health** ->
[[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]].

### What the evidence cannot yet answer

- Weight loss at \~70 with muscle defended: outside knee osteoarthritis, the trials in this guide's
  evidence measure lean mass, not function, and none measures falls or fractures
  -> [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]].
- Pace of weight loss at this age: one randomised trial enrolled adults aged 18-70 with a BMI of 30-45,
  excluding people with diabetes. A 12-week very-low-calorie diet got more people to the loss target
  than a 36-week diet that included one or two meal-replacement shakes a day (76 of 97 vs 53 of 103 who
  started the diet, by intention to treat). The arms also differed in dietitian visits (6 vs 18), so the
  trial compared two programmes, not pace alone. Among those who reached the target and finished, «At the end of phase 2, both
  gradual weight loss and rapid weight loss participants who completed the study (n=43 in gradual weight
  loss and n=61 in rapid weight loss) had regained most of their lost weight (gradual weight loss 71·2%
  regain, 95% CI 58·1–84·3 vs rapid weight loss 70·5%, 57·8–83·2).»
  [@purcell2014rate]. That comparison covers only people who
  reached the target, and whether it holds around 70 is an assumption
  -> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]].
- Older-age cardiovascular trials (HYVET, PROSPER, SCORE2-OP) and ATEMPT in frail people are not among
  this guide's sources -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].
- Deprescribing and polypharmacy: not covered by this guide's evidence, and outside its scope.
- Falls exercise above about 3 hours a week -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]; the
  RT dose for frail people -> [[Frailty]].
- Exercise and bone in older men; an osteoporosis drug as exercise's comparator
  -> [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]].
- A cut-off for sarcopenic obesity -> [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]]; which body weight to
  dose protein against -> [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

## What to do, in order

**If the person smokes, quitting comes before every line below** (HR about 3 for current smokers;
quitting at 55-64 gained about 4 years) [[Smoking and Mortality]]. Heavy drinking likely belongs there
too [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]. For everyone else, within each block below the
lines run in order of effect size x certainty, as the sections above state them. A line that starts
with *If* applies only to that group.

1. **Train balance with high-challenge exercise, building toward about 3 hours of exercise a week, and
   keep it up.** Falls rate ratio 0.77 (0.71-0.83), high certainty; the 3 hours is a labelled default
   for community-dwelling people. Walking should not be anyone's only falls exercise.
   [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]
2. **If blood pressure is raised, lower it; discuss treatment.** 5 mmHg lower systolic cut major
   cardiovascular events, 0.91 (0.88-0.95) at 65-74 and 0.91 (0.87-0.96) at 75-84; 85+ is insufficient
   evidence. Dementia OR 0.87 (0.75-0.99) after a fall of about 10/4 mmHg. With frailty, a clinician
   trade-off.
   [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]
3. **Do resistance training twice a week, start at low volume, progress, add power work.** Timed
   up-and-go SMD -1.20 (-1.57 to -0.82), low certainty; power beat strength, SMD 0.43 (0.23-0.62), high
   certainty. [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]],
   [[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]]
4. **Use exercise for bone; skip routine vitamin D or calcium as a fracture lever if not deficient.**
   Exercise programmes cut major osteoporotic fractures, rate ratio 0.75 (0.59-0.94), in adults
   averaging over 50, mostly in women-only trials, in a review by one research group; the programmes
   overlap line 1. Vitamin D alone gave hip fracture RR 1.14 (0.98-1.32); with calcium 0.84
   (0.72-0.97), somewhat greater in care-home trials at high risk of bias and not significant in the
   one large community trial. Kidney stones rose, RR 1.18 (1.04-1.35). From 75, the Endocrine Society
   conditionally suggests low-dose vitamin D for mortality, RR 0.96 (0.93-1.00).
   [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]],
   [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]],
   [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]]

Lines 5-8 apply only to the group each one names.

5. **If prediabetic, join a DPP-style lifestyle programme.** Diabetes incidence -71% (51-83) at 60 and
   over, in one trial. [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]
6. **If at high cardiovascular risk, consider a Mediterranean diet.** Heart attack, stroke or
   cardiovascular death HR 0.70 (0.55-0.89) in men 55-80 and women 60-80 in Spain, after a
   propensity-score repair; deaths did not fall. [[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]]
7. **If losing weight on purpose, pair it with RT and the protein floor.** Diets cut mortality, RR 0.82
   (0.71-0.95), in mostly middle-aged trials [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]; RT kept
   0.82 kg (0.36-1.27) and protein of at least 1.0 g/kg 0.83 kg (0.47-1.19) of lean mass. **Not for
   unintentional loss or a frail person.** [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]],
   [[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]]
8. **If the diet is Chinese-style, with most salt added at home, consider a salt substitute after a
   kidney and potassium check.** In rural China (72.6% after a stroke) stroke 0.86 (0.77-0.96), death
   0.88 (0.82-0.95); elsewhere the blood-pressure fall is what transfers. Not with kidney disease, or
   potassium-sparing drugs or potassium supplements in the household; check first with diabetes or an
   ACE inhibitor/ARB, and with a thiazide for low blood sodium. [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

Lines 9 and 10 rest on weaker designs: a consensus on muscle markers, and cohort data.

9. **Eat at least 1.0-1.2 g protein per kg a day, 1.2 if exercising, about 25-30 g a meal.** Consensus
   floors on surrogates; with eGFR below 30 and not on dialysis, PROT-AGE caps it at 0.8 g/kg ideal
   body weight. [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]]
10. **Walk toward 6000-8000 steps a day if below it.** At 60 and over mortality stopped falling there
   (observational; a spline with no interval; medians 3553-10,901 steps).
   [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

The last two lines cover drug choices and what not to expect.

11. **Weigh a statin on absolute risk; do not start aspirin.** Without vascular disease, statin 0.84
    (99% CI 0.70-1.01) at 71-75, insufficient evidence after 75; aspirin at 60 and over is Grade D.
    [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]],
    [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]
12. **Get hearing checked, but do not expect a multidomain programme to protect memory.** Cognition
    0.002 SD (-0.077 to 0.081) with hearing aids [[Hearing Loss and Dementia]]; multidomain dementia
    HR 0.98 (0.80-1.21), a wide interval [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]].
