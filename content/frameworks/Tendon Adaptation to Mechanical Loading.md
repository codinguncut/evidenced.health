**Peripheral scope** (exercise-programming), and — unlike the injury-prevention evidence it underlies —
the measured outcomes here are **surrogates, not patient-important endpoints**: this is a meta-analysis
of tendon *material and structural* change, not of injury or function. It matters because the fabric
holds tendon/connective-tissue conditioning as **fabric-zone for the untrained / obese-untrained /
near-inactive stratum** (where a wrong loading ramp causes an injury that *ends* the attempt — route-(c)
harm plus adherence), and this is the first held source on how tendon actually adapts. The headline:
«Tendons are highly responsive to increased mechanical loading and adapt through changes of their
mechanical, material, and morphological properties.»
[@bohm2015]

**Evidence-tier note (`confidence: low`).** Single gold source (Bohm 2015 SR-MA: 27 studies, 37
exercise interventions, N=264, Achilles or patellar tendon, healthy adults 18-50, interventions
&gt;=8 weeks). Held `low` on total web support: single-source; the primary trials are small (6-15
participants each) and **cannot blind** an exercise exposure (randomization, allocation concealment,
and assessor blinding were unreported in 34 of 37 interventions, so risk of bias was unassessable for
most); and the population is narrow (see transportability). The methodological-quality score averaged
71% +/- 9%. [@bohm2015]

## The effect — tendon adapts, material property more than size

Pooled standardized mean differences (random-effects, Hedges' g), all with a significant overall
effect (p<0.05): [@bohm2015]

| Tendon property | SMD (95% CI) | N interventions | I2 | Read |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stiffness** (mechanical) | **0.70 (0.51, 0.88)** | 37 | 30% (moderate) | moderate-to-large; the primary adaptation |
| **Young's modulus** (material) | **0.69 (0.36, 1.03)** | 17 | 57% (substantial) | moderate-to-large; comparable to stiffness |
| **Cross-sectional area** (morphological) | **0.24 (0.07, 0.42)** | 33 | 21% (moderate) | small-to-moderate; the weak arm |

- **Stiffness rises mainly via material change, not hypertrophy.** Young's modulus (0.69) tracks
  stiffness (0.70) far more closely than CSA (0.24) does: «intervention-induced changes in tendon
  stiffness seem to be more attributed to adaptations of the material rather than morphological
  properties.» [@bohm2015] The authors frame material
  change as the *early* mechanism and hypertrophy as a *long-term* one, so the small CSA effect may be
  an artifact of the short intervention durations (mean 12.9 +/- 4.5 weeks), not evidence that tendons
  do not thicken. [@bohm2015]
- **Surrogate flag.** Stiffness, Young's modulus and CSA are `[[Surrogate Outcomes]]` — mechanical
  properties of the tissue, not injury, pain, or function. Bohm measures none of the latter; the
  transmission from a stiffer tendon to a better patient outcome is argued below, not demonstrated here.

## The driver is loading MAGNITUDE, not contraction type

The single decision-relevant finding: adaptation depends on how hard the tendon is loaded, and is
nearly indifferent to *how* that load is applied. [@bohm2015]

- **High vs low intensity splits the result cleanly.** «The subgroup analysis on the loading intensity
  showed that pooling interventions using muscle contraction intensities higher than 70% of MVC or RM
  (N = 27) and those using lower intensities (N = 5) resulted in a weighted averaged effect size of
  tendon stiffness of 0.90 (CI 0.71, 1.08) and 0.04 (CI −0.46, 0.53), respectively.»
  [@bohm2015] The high group had **no heterogeneity**
  (I2=0%); the difference between groups was p<0.00001. The low-intensity CI spans zero — below the
  threshold, the pooled effect is **indistinguishable from nothing**.
- **Contraction type is irrelevant within the high-intensity group.** Isometric (SMD 0.95, CI 0.66-1.24),
  concentric-eccentric (0.82, 0.49-1.15), and purely eccentric (1.04, 0.37-1.72) did not differ (p>0.5).
  [@bohm2015] Conclusion: «high magnitude loading (i.e.,
  muscle contraction intensity) is most effective to elicit tendon adaptation and that longer
  intervention durations (>12 weeks) are beneficial compared to shorter ones.»
  [@bohm2015]
- **Plyometrics are the exception that fits the rule.** «The effect of muscle contraction type
  (isometric, concentric-eccentric, or isometric) seems insignificant; however, the review suggests
  that plyometric training may not be optimal to facilitate tendon adaptation.»
  [@bohm2015] Plyometric results ranged from +28% to -9%
  stiffness; the proposed mechanism is that the *very short* loading duration during a jump limits
  stimulus transduction (tendon viscosity) — so it is loading *dwell*, not the stretch-shortening cycle
  per se, that underperforms. [inferred from @bohm2015] (the dwell-time mechanism
  is the authors' suggestion, not a measured contrast).

### The *70% MVC/RM* is an analytic cutpoint, not a located knee

The 70% figure is the **boundary the authors drew to dichotomize the subgroups**, not an estimated
threshold on a dose-response curve `[[The Underivable Optimum]]`. The high group bundled 70-90% MVC and
70-80% RM; no curve was fitted, so the data locate no knee — they show only that a high-load cluster
adapts and a low-load cluster does not. Two load-bearing caveats:

- **The real exposure is tendon strain, which was almost never measured.** «However, only three studies
  (i.e., eight interventions) specified the corresponding tendon strain magnitude to the muscle
  contraction intensity [28,29,31].» [@bohm2015] Muscle
  contraction intensity (%MVC/RM) is a **proxy** for the mechanical quantity the tendon cell actually
  senses (strain); the 70% cutpoint inherits that proxy error.
- **Studied range.** Interventions ran 8-14 weeks, 2-4 days/week, in Achilles/patellar tendons. A
  *minimum effective dose* is a region inside that range, not a transferable number; nothing here
  bounds the dose for other tendons, longer horizons, or the deconditioned.

### Duration — responds by 8 weeks, more reliable past 12

«several of the included studies featuring a duration of 8 weeks found significant adaptations of
tendon stiffness [17,25,30], indicating that tendons already respond to increased mechanical loading
within 2 months.» [@bohm2015] Pooled by duration, >=12
weeks gave SMD 0.91 (CI 0.71-1.12, N=23) vs 0.81 for 8-12 weeks (N=4) — **not a significant difference**,
so the *longer is better* claim rests on individual time-course studies (Kubo: no significant tendon
stiffness increase at 2 months, significant at 3), not on the subgroup contrast.
[@bohm2015]

## The muscle-tendon adaptation-rate MISMATCH — the injury-risk mechanism

This is the beyond-summary move and the reason the page sits in the fabric zone for novices. Bohm finds
high load drives tendon adaptation **and** warns that tendon adapts *slower* than muscle:
«adequate regeneration times and slower adaptation rates of tendon compared to muscle [72-74] should be
considered in an exercise intervention to avoid episodes of high tendon strain and stress [75] that may
cause maladaptation and tendon damage.» [@bohm2015] The
overload pathway: «excessive mechanical loading (i.e., overloading) was considered as an important
factor in the etiology of tendinopathy [20,34,35], which is associated with pain, focal tendon
tenderness, and decreased strength and movement [32].» [@bohm2015]

- **The decision-relevant synthesis [inferred from @bohm2015]:** the same high
  magnitude that best conditions a tendon is also what, applied faster than the tissue can adapt,
  overloads it. Because muscle strengthens faster than tendon stiffens, a trainee whose *muscular*
  capacity outruns *tendon* capacity can generate forces the tendon is not yet conditioned for — the
  rate mismatch is the mechanistic basis for ramping load gradually, not jumping to high-impact volume
  on an unconditioned tendon. This connects directly to the fabric's exercise-programming taper (a
  wrong modality for an obese novice — high-impact mileage on unconditioned tendons — causes a harm
  that ENDS the attempt). The **epidemiological counterpart** now lives on
  [[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]] (Damsted 2018 SR): a weak, directionally-consistent
  signal that a *sudden* training-load increase associates with running injury — consistent with this
  rate-mismatch mechanism, though measuring a different quantity (volume ramp, not contraction
  intensity), and notably the popular "10% rule" has **no** supporting evidence (10% and 24% weekly
  increases were indistinguishable in the one study that tested it).
- **Injury PREVENTION is not measured here — it is inferred.** Bohm reports that «None of the... included
  interventions reported a significant dropout of participants due to clinical symptoms of overloading»
  [@bohm2015] — i.e. no injury *outcome* was an endpoint;
  the caveat is a mechanistic warning, not an injury finding. That a conditioned (stiffer, stronger)
  tendon resists overuse injury is the plausible transmission, and the one patient-important outcome
  for which *strength-type* training has causal-grade evidence lives on
  [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]] (Lauersen: strength training RR 0.315 for
  sport injury; overuse injury almost halved). Bohm supplies the **tissue mechanism** beneath that
  result; it does not itself demonstrate it. [inferred from @bohm2015]

## Transportability boundary — who was actually studied

The evidence base is **young, recreationally-active men** — not the stratum for which the fabric files
tendon conditioning as a big-rock-adjacent lever. [@bohm2015]

- 237 of 264 participants were male; \~164 of 264 were regularly physically active in recreational
  sport, not untrained; all were 18-50. Only one intervention was in untrained women; the
  obese / deconditioned / older novice is essentially absent.
- **Support-factor caveat (route (a) + the mechanism's conditions).** The adaptation magnitudes and the
  *70% MVC/RM* proxy come from tendons in young active men. Whether a deconditioned or obese novice's
  tendon adapts at the same rate, tolerates the same relative load, or carries the same overload
  threshold is **not established here** — and the mismatch caveat suggests the deconditioned may need a
  *gentler* ramp, not the same one. This is a named gap, not a finding. [inferred from @bohm2015]
- A lower-limb-tendon-specific synthesis is awaited to test these parameters in a broader frame: — would it confirm the magnitude-over-type
  finding and bound the deconditioned-stratum transfer.

## Decision relevance

- **To condition a tendon, the lever is LOAD MAGNITUDE, not exercise style.** High-intensity loading
  (the studied cluster: \~70-90% MVC / \~70-80% RM) adapts the tendon; low-intensity work does essentially
  nothing (SMD 0.04, CI spans zero). Isometric, concentric-eccentric, and eccentric all work when the
  load is high — pick on preference, joint tolerance, and adherence, not on a believed tissue advantage.
- **Give it time and a gradual ramp.** Tendon responds within 8 weeks but adapts *slower than muscle*;
  the actionable consequence is to progress load gradually and allow recovery, because the rate mismatch
  — muscle outrunning tendon — is the overload pathway. This matters most for the deconditioned novice,
  for whom a too-fast ramp (high-impact volume on an unconditioned tendon) is the injury that ends the
  attempt.
- **Do not read stiffness gains as an injury or function result.** The outcomes here are surrogates; the
  patient-important payoff (injury avoidance) is mechanistically plausible and has separate causal-grade
  support for strength training ([[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]]), but it is not
  what Bohm measured.
- **Plyometrics are a weaker tendon-conditioning tool** than sustained high-load work — likely the short
  ground-contact loading dwell — so if tendon adaptation (not power) is the goal, slower high-load
  contractions are the better bet.
- **Scope to young active adults until broader evidence lands.** The parameters are from young
  recreationally-active men; transfer to obese, older, or untrained tendons is unproven, and the
  mismatch caveat argues for *more* caution there, not less.
