**Peripheral scope** (exercise-programming), but the outcome is **patient-important**: a running-related
injury (RRI) ends the training attempt — route-(c) harm, and most consequential for the
deconditioned / obese novice whom the fabric's exercise-programming taper places in the **fabric zone**
(where a wrong load ramp causes the injury that *ends* the attempt, not a marginal performance loss).
The decision this page answers: *does ramping running volume too fast cause RRI, and is the "10% rule"
— add no more than 10% to weekly distance — evidence-based?* The honest verdict is uncomfortable for a
rule that reads as settled: **the evidence is insufficient to confirm the causal claim, and there is no
evidence at all for the 10% figure itself.**

Headline conclusion: «Very limited evidence exists supporting that a sudden change in training load is
associated with increased risk of running-related injury.»
[@damsted2018]

**Evidence-tier note (`confidence: low`).** Single systematic review (Damsted 2018), tier `high`,
**no meta-analysis** — the included studies were too heterogeneous to pool: «due to the heterogeneity
observed in the study designs, the runners' profiles, as well as the methods used for data collec-tion
and analysis of changes in training load, com-parison of the results of the four included studies in
the present systematic review must be performed with caution.»
[@damsted2018] Only four studies met eligibility, and
all involved «"novice" and "amateur" runners, which limits the applicability of the findings to other
populations of runners.» [@damsted2018] So the page
extracts the **structure** of the evidence, not a pooled magnitude.

## The evidence structure — four studies, three point the same way, weakly

The SR found four eligible studies; three reported an association between a load increase and RRI, the
directions consistent but each estimate individually weak or uncorrected:
[@damsted2018]

| Study finding (as reported in the SR) | Estimate | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Recent change in velocity/distance/frequency, injured vs non-injured | p=0.037 | significant, but a chi-square the **review authors ran themselves** on raw proportions |
| Weekly distance increase **>30%** vs **<10%** | HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96; 2.66) | directionally raised, **CI crosses 1** — not significant |
| Total distance the week before injury vs other weeks | mean diff 86% (95% CI 12%; 159%), p=0.026 | significant within-runner, but susceptible to reverse causation (the pre-injury week) |
| Weekly increase **10% vs 24%** | HR 0.8 (95% CI 0.6; 1.3) | **null — no difference between the two** |

- The direction is consistent but thin: «a tendency toward an increased injury risk following a sudden
  increase in training load was identified in three out of the four studies included...»
  [@damsted2018]
- For the Cantidio study the SR had to **supply the statistics**: «no result for statistical comparison
  was provided. Therefore, as all data were available to run a Chi-square test this was performed by the
  present authors...» [@damsted2018] — so one of the
  three supporting findings is a re-analysis, not an original result, which weakens the independent-study
  count further.

## The 10% rule is unsupported — and the one study built to test it falsifies its special status

The SR is unambiguous: «no evidence exists for the use of the so-called "10% rule", which is commonly
used by runners, coaches and clinicians as a guideline for a maximum increase in training load per
week.» [@damsted2018]

The data go further than *unproven*. The one estimate that directly tested the 10% boundary found
**10% and 24% weekly increases indistinguishable** — «no difference was found between a 10% and a 24%
average increase in weekly volume (HR=0.8, 95% CI: 0.6; 1.3)»
[@damsted2018] — and only the much larger >30% step
showed even a (non-significant) signal. The authors' own gloss: «An average weekly increase in training
load of 24% may not be sufficiently large to reflect the mechanism of running too much, too soon in
novice runners.» [@damsted2018] So the 10% cut has no
demonstrated privileged status: a novice can apparently more than double it (to 24%) without a measured
excess risk, and the hazardous zone — if one exists — sits well above 10%, nearer 30%, not at it.

Note this is **within one study** — the SR's highest-quality study (100% methodological-quality score)
produced *both* the 10%-vs-24% null *and* the >30% signal. That the same methods, same population and
same analysis found 10% and 24% equivalent yet >30% elevated makes the refutation of the 10% figure a
within-study contrast, not a fragile between-study artifact.
[@damsted2018]

**Underivable-optimum discipline (`[[The Underivable Optimum]]`).** The 10% figure is a round-number
heuristic, not a located knee. The SR states the knee is unknown: «a well-defined threshold for
hazardous or sudden changes in training load above which the risk of injury development is significantly
increased is still unknown.» [@damsted2018] No curve was
fitted; the only datapoints are the dichotomies above (10% vs 24% null; >30% vs <10% HR 1.59, CI 0.96-2.66).
A threshold quoted as *10%* is a bare number stripped of a CI and a studied range — exactly the false
precision the fabric forbids.

## The relative-vs-absolute trap — why a novice's *small* increase is large

A fixed percentage rule interacts badly with a low baseline. «a small increase in training load might
result in a large relative sudden increase (e.g. increasing from 2 to 3 km per week represents an
absolute difference of 1 km but a relative sudden increase of 50%).»
[@damsted2018] This is the
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]] logic applied to dose: for a true beginner running a
handful of kilometres, trivial absolute additions are large *relative* jumps, so a percentage-based rule
is most restrictive precisely where volume is lowest — and the studies that measure *percent increase*
inherit this, making the exposure metric itself ambiguous across runners at different baselines. It cuts
the other way for a high-mileage runner, for whom 10% is a large absolute load.

## Evidence-state verdict — INSUFFICIENT, not null, with a weak directional signal

Applying the four evidence states (benefit / harm / no meaningful effect / **insufficient**) to the SR's
findings [inferred from @damsted2018]:

- This is **insufficient evidence**, not a null. *Very limited evidence* with four heterogeneous,
  un-poolable studies, three directionally positive but individually weak, is the insufficient-evidence
  state — the expectancy test is *not* satisfied (a properly-powered prospective cohort with objective
  load measurement could show a gradient we have not yet seen). It is emphatically **not** a
  *no-effect* finding: the SR does not say ramping fast is safe, it says the evidence cannot yet
  adjudicate.
- The **10% rule specifically** is closer to refuted-as-a-number: the one direct test found 10% and
  24% equivalent, so the figure has no support. That is a decision-change — it licenses dropping slavish
  adherence to the 10% cut — without licensing the opposite (fast ramping is not thereby endorsed).
- **The conservative default survives on other grounds.** Three directionally-consistent studies plus a
  coherent tissue mechanism (below) make *progress load gradually* the low-regret choice even under
  insufficient evidence: over-caution on a hidden plateau merely forgoes a little fitness, while a
  too-fast ramp risks the injury that ends the attempt. The default is *gradual progression*, not
  *the 10% number*.

## Mechanism bridge to tendon conditioning — consistent, but NOT a matched-parameter composite

The weak epidemiological signal here is *consistent with* the tendon-adaptation mechanism on
[[Tendon Adaptation to Mechanical Loading]]: tendon adapts **slower than muscle**, so load ramped faster
than the tissue can condition is an overload pathway. But the two sources measure **different
quantities**, so this is a mechanistic bridge, not a type-F composite — the parameter table forbids
treating them as the same claim:

| Parameter | Damsted (this page) | Bohm (Tendon Adaptation) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure | running training-load **ramp** (weekly % increase in volume) | muscle contraction **intensity** (\~70% MVC/RM) per session | **NO** — rate of volume progression vs single-session load magnitude |
| Outcome | clinical **running-related injury** (whole-body, any RRI) | tendon mechanical **surrogates** (stiffness, modulus, CSA) | **NO** — patient-important injury vs tissue surrogate |
| Population | novice & amateur runners (mixed sex) | young recreationally-active men (237/264 male) | partial, not matched |
| Axis of the dose | *how fast* load rises over weeks | *how hard* a contraction loads the tendon | different axes (rate vs magnitude) |

So Bohm's rate-mismatch (muscle outruns tendon) gives a plausible **why** for Damsted's signal, and
Damsted supplies the **epidemiological counterpart** Bohm lacks (Bohm measured no injury), but neither
confirms the other's number. Kept as convergence, not pooled evidence.
[inferred from @damsted2018]

The separate causal-grade evidence that **strength training** reduces injury (RR 0.315, overuse almost
halved) lives on [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]] — a different lever (build
tolerance) from this page's question (manage the ramp), and a stronger evidence base.

## Transportability — who was studied, and who was not

- **Novices and amateurs only**, so the findings do not transfer cleanly to competitive or high-mileage
  runners (for whom the relative-absolute trap inverts and 10% is a large absolute load).
  [@damsted2018]
- The exposure metric is **self-reported training load** across studies using «methods used for data
  collec-tion and analysis of changes in training load» that differed study to study
  [@damsted2018] — i.e. the ramp was measured
  heterogeneously and often by recall, so [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]'s logic applies by
  analogy: measurement error flattens a real dose-response, so a failed or weak gradient is weak evidence
  of no gradient. [inferred from @damsted2018]
- A broader training-error SR and an updated cohort would test whether the signal firms with better
  measurement: (the earlier training-error
  review this SR updates) and — both would bear on
  whether the >30% signal is real or an artifact of the pre-injury week.

## Decision relevance

[inferred from @damsted2018] — the actionable reading of the SR's findings:

- **Do not treat the 10% rule as evidence-based.** It is a round-number heuristic with no support; the
  one study testing it found 10% and 24% weekly increases equivalent in injury risk. Dropping rigid 10%
  adherence is a licensed decision-change.
- **Still ramp gradually — but for mechanism and low-regret reasons, not the 10% number.** Three
  directionally-consistent studies plus the tendon rate-mismatch mechanism make gradual progression the
  conservative choice; the *number* is undefined (the hazardous threshold, if any, is unknown and
  appears to sit nearer 30% than 10%).
- **Watch the relative-absolute trap at low volume.** For a true beginner, percentage rules are most
  restrictive where mileage is lowest; think in absolute steps a deconditioned body tolerates, not a
  fixed percent.
- **This is insufficient evidence, not reassurance.** The SR cannot say fast ramping is safe — it says
  the evidence cannot yet decide. For the deconditioned/obese novice (route-(c) harm, high stakes), the
  asymmetry favours caution.
- **The stronger injury lever is building tolerance** ([[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]]: strength training), a better-evidenced route than load-ramp management alone.

## Sibling cell — the bone-stress-injury counterpart (distinction + convergence)

[inferred from @damsted2018] — the *bone* overuse injury (stress fracture) has
its own risk-factor SR-MA on [[Stress Fracture Risk Factors in Runners]] (Wright 2016). It is a
**distinction, not a tension**: this page's exposure is the *rate of change* of load -> *any* RRI, Wright's
is *absolute volume* + host factors -> *bone* stress fracture (parameter table on that page). But the two
**converge** on a structural claim neither makes alone: the popular training-load injury heuristics lack
an evidence base — here the *10% rule* (ramp rate), on Wright's page the widespread anecdote that higher
mileages cause stress fracture and that cutting volume prevents them (Wright's own verbatim wording lives
on that page). Two different load rules, both received wisdom without support — a type-A convergence, not
a permission to ramp freely (both are insufficient-evidence verdicts).
