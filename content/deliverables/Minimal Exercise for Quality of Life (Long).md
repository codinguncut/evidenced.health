> [!warning] Applies to a specific group
> This appraisal is written for **a sedentary, untrained middle-aged adult with obesity, in a developed
> country, whose goals are quality of life, physical function, and keeping function into old age**. If you
> are not in it, the recommendations here may not transfer — the population-level picture can differ.

The simplest program the evidence backs for this reader is **resistance training twice a week, about 150
minutes a week of moderate, low-impact aerobic exercise, and more everyday walking**
([[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]], [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]], [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]).

- **Most of the benefit comes from starting.** Each dose in the plan sits at the lowest level the studies
  tested and found to work for its outcome. That is a region, not a precisely located minimum
  ([[The Underivable Optimum]]).
- **Build up over about eight weeks, then keep going.** Tendons adapt more slowly than muscle, and the
  evidence offers no smaller dose for keeping the gains ([[Tendon Adaptation to Mechanical Loading]],
  [[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]]).
- **Fitness and strength are the gauges; function and quality of life are the goal.** Training probably
  improves both. Most of that evidence comes from older adults, frail people and people with knee
  osteoarthritis, and nobody has measured whether training keeps function intact into old age
  ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).
- **Higher-intensity intervals are optional.** They can raise fitness faster, but they feel worse while you
  do them, and the minimum does not need them ([[Affective Response to High-Intensity Interval Exercise]]).
- **The plan matches standard guidance.** On the mortality curve, the guidance figure is a sensible target
  where the benefit has begun to level off, not the point where benefit begins.

## Lifting twice a week and walking most days is the simplest program the evidence backs

The program has three parts: **two resistance-training sessions a week, about 150 minutes a week of
moderate, low-impact aerobic work, and more everyday movement on top**. Each number sits at the lowest dose
that trials tested and found to work for its own outcome, not below it. **No trial has tested this exact
package for quality of life in this group.**
Plain numbers in the table trace to the evidence; numbers and choices marked †, here and throughout, are
practical choices the evidence leaves open (spacing, exercise list, rep ranges, timing of progressions).

### The first six months, then the long run

1. **Weeks 1-8, build-up.** Start below the target, for example half the aerobic minutes†, with lighter
   loads, and add in small absolute steps. The popular 10% weekly rule has no evidence behind it
   [@damsted2018]. **Tendon responds to load within about
   8 weeks but adapts more slowly than muscle** [@bohm2015].
2. **Weeks \~8-24, full program.** Run the whole template. The fitness trial closest to this reader ran 24
   weeks [@ross2016]; trials pooled
   for adults with obesity averaged 20 weeks, most at three 45-minute sessions a week
   [@odonoghue2020].
3. **Month 6 onward, maintenance.** Keep going at the same dose: the evidence offers no smaller maintenance
   dose [[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]]. **A short break does not reset
   you; stopping for good does.**

Why not go lower? None of the dose curves shows a sharp minimum, so the template stops at the lowest dose
that was tested and worked.

## Most of the benefit comes from starting, and no curve shows a sharp minimum

**The biggest drop in the risk of death comes with the first small step out of near-total
inactivity.** In cohorts that measured movement with devices, people in the second-least-active
quarter had less than half the mortality hazard of the least active (HR 0.48)
[@ekelundacc2019]. After that, the
curves either flatten or keep falling. **None of them marks a dose where benefit switches on.** So in
this guide, *minimum* means the lowest dose that trials tested and found to work for a given outcome.
That is a region, not a located threshold. The curve's shape also changes with the outcome and with
what was measured, so no single curve answers the question for everything at once
 [[The Underivable Optimum]].

### Mortality falls steeply at the first step, then flattens

Ekelund pooled eight cohorts that wore hip accelerometers: 36,383 adults aged 40 or older, with 2,149
deaths. Against the least-active quarter, the hazard ratios for total activity were **0.48 (95% CI
0.43-0.54), 0.34 (0.26-0.45) and 0.27 (0.23-0.32)** in the second, third and most active quarters.
The authors found **the greatest risk reduction between the bottom quarter and the second**, at every
intensity [@ekelundacc2019].

That first step was small, and **it did not need to be exercise**. Ranked by light activity alone
(pottering, slow walking, light chores), the second quarter had a hazard ratio of 0.60 (0.54-0.68)
against the bottom quarter, a gap of roughly 60 minutes a day. Ranked by moderate-to-vigorous activity
alone, the second quarter's hazard ratio was 0.64 (0.55-0.74), a gap of only about 5 minutes a day
[@ekelundacc2019]. These data point
to **moving more at any intensity and sitting less**, not to aerobic workouts as such.

For moderate-to-vigorous activity alone, the mortality curve in the WHO evidence review stops
falling steeply after a modest daily amount
[@who2020]. Read the flat part as a
broad region of **roughly 20-40 minutes a day**, not a target: it is this guide's reading of where
the curve bends, with no confidence interval of its own
 [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]. Two limits bound it. Few people in these
cohorts did that much, so the confidence intervals are wide at the top of the curve. And every cohort
enrolled adults aged 40 or older, so the authors cannot say whether the levels apply to younger people
[@ekelundacc2019].

Devices and questionnaires give different numbers. The device-measured associations were **about twice as
large** as those in earlier studies that asked people how active they were
[@ekelundacc2019]. A questionnaire-based
hazard ratio is therefore not comparable with the ones above. All of these are observational
associations. People who are ill move less, and excluding early deaths only partly guards against that
[[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

### Fitness and cancer curves keep falling, so the shape depends on the outcome

Measured fitness behaves differently from activity. Mandsager followed 122,007 adults who had a
treadmill exercise test. Mortality kept falling into the top fitness band (elite vs high performers,
**HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.63-0.95**), with «no observed upper limit of benefit»
[@mandsager2018]. The authors
state that the association does not prove causation, because fitter patients may simply have been
healthier to start with [@mandsager2018].

For cancer, the World Cancer Research Fund judges that «For cancer prevention, it is likely that the
greater the amount of physical activity, the greater the benefit»
[@wcrf2018ter]. It names no plateau, though it
gives no studied range either. These curves do not contradict the
activity-mortality plateau. **Each shape belongs to its own outcome and its own measure**: activity
dose flattens for death from any cause, while fitness and cancer risk keep improving
 [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]. The outcomes this guide cares about most,
physical function and quality of life, have no dose-response curve in the evidence reviewed here
.

### Strength training has a tested floor, not a located knee

The main network meta-analysis of resistance-training prescriptions coded load, sets and frequency as
categories rather than as continuous doses. The authors say so, and suggest that future work model
them as continuous predictors
[@currier2023]. **No knee can be read off
categories.** The two-sets, two-sessions figure that later sections use is the floor of what was tested
and worked, not a measured optimum
 [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].

For mortality, studies of strength training put the lowest risk somewhere in a **region of about
30-80+ minutes a week**, a region read across several pooled curves, none of which gives a confidence
interval for the lowest point itself [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]. Shailendra's
dose curve found RR 0.74 (95% CI 0.64-0.86) at about 60 minutes a week, with smaller reductions above
that. The curve rests on 4 studies, and the authors say it «should be viewed with caution»
[@shailendra2022]. The apparent
upturn at higher volumes is not a reason to do less
[[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]].

### A located minimum is not needed to act

**You do not need a precise minimum to start.** Three things set how much evidence a choice needs: how
reversible it is, what it costs, and how big the lever is. The certainty grade is not one of them.
Starting to train is cheap and reversible, and it targets inactivity, one of the largest levers there
is for a sedentary adult [[Acting on a Direction Without a Magnitude]].

One limit covers every mortality figure in this section. The pooled studies report hazard and risk
ratios, not absolute risks for a group like this one, so this guide gives no per-1,000 figure for
activity and death [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

If the dose curves cannot say where to stop, the next question is what the program is meant to move.

## Function and quality of life are the goal; fitness and strength are the gauges

**Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and strength are worth tracking, but they are not what the program
is for.** Both predict how long people live. Both are partly inherited and partly lowered by
illness, so raising them is not the same as buying the survival gap seen between fit and unfit
people. The outcomes that matter are what a person can do and how they feel. Training probably
improves both, with limits set out below. -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]

### Fitness and strength stand in for the outcome

The US Preventive Services Task Force draws the line plainly. It counts as health outcomes the
«symptoms, functional levels, and conditions that patients can feel or experience»; markers that a
preventive service moves without the patient sensing them are intermediate outcomes, not health
outcomes in themselves [@uspstfmanual2022]. **By that test, a
chair stand, a walk to the shops or a pain score is an outcome; a VO2max reading or a one-repetition
maximum is a gauge.** A gauge becomes a target only once moving it is shown to move the outcome
. -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]

### Fitness predicts survival, and part of that link is real

Each 1 MET (3.5 ml/kg/min of VO2max) of higher fitness goes with lower all-cause mortality, RR 0.87
(0.84-0.90), in a meta-analysis of cohort studies
[@kodama2009]. The same analysis puts
a **minimal fitness level for substantial risk reduction at about 8 METs for men and about 6 for women,
at age 50 (SD 10)**
[@kodama2009]. These are
between-person comparisons, and **fitter people are partly fitter because they are healthier**. About
half the variation in CRF is inherited
[@ross2016], and undiagnosed
illness plausibly lowers measured fitness too. ->
[[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]

Two kinds of evidence suggest that changing your own fitness carries some of the benefit. Men in
Blair's cohort who went from unfit to fit between two exams had a 44% lower mortality risk than men
who stayed unfit [@ross2016].
In HF-ACTION, the largest randomized trial of exercise training in heart failure patients, every 6%
rise in measured fitness over 3 months went with a 4% lower risk of cardiovascular death or
hospitalization [@ross2016]. That
figure is an association within the trial, not a randomized contrast; the trial enrolled people with
heart failure and counted hospital stays in its endpoint.

**So fitness is partly something you can change for benefit and partly a marker of health: training
your way out of the least-fit group helps**, by less than the observational gradient suggests. -> [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]]

### Strength predicts too, but at midlife the absolute stakes are small

In UK Biobank, a large cohort of 502,293 middle-aged adults (40-69), each 5 kg lower grip strength
went with higher all-cause mortality: HR 1.20 (1.17-1.23) in women and 1.16 (1.15-1.17) in men. The
authors also ran a separate analysis excluding deaths in the first two years
[@celismorales2018]. **Deaths were uncommon, about
2.7% over 7 years, so the absolute difference behind those ratios is small** at this age.
Grip is also about half inherited
[@celismorales2018]. **Grip is a good number to
measure and a poor number to steer toward.** -> [[Grip Strength and Mortality]]

Weak muscle also predicts losing function. In adults over 60, sarcopenia (low muscle mass with low
strength or performance) went with functional decline, OR 1.9 (1.55-2.32), falling to 1.55
(1.18-2.04) after correction for publication bias
[@zhao2026sarcopenia]. These
cohorts show who is at risk; they do not show that rebuilding muscle reverses it. A review of
testosterone therapy in men shows the gap from the other side: lean mass rose, yet «increases in
muscle mass do not necessarily translate into clinically meaningful improvements in physical
function» [@lopezhilario2026]. ->
[[Low Muscle Mass and Mortality]], [[Testosterone Adiposity and Muscle]]

### Training improves function and quality of life in trials

Randomized trials measure the outcome directly, and they point one way. **Exercise probably improves
mobility, daily activities and quality of life in frail adults over 65.** Pooled against no-exercise
controls, mobility rose by SMD 0.60 (0.37-0.83), activities of daily living by 0.50 (0.15-0.84) and
quality of life (QoL) by 0.60 (0.13-1.07), and the risk of remaining frail fell, RR 0.58
(0.36-0.93), all at moderate certainty
[@racey2021frailtypa]. The
frailty figure rests on only four trials that measured it. -> [[Frailty]]

In knee and hip osteoarthritis, 77 trials found that **exercise improved function (SMD 0.50,
0.38-0.63) and, less, quality of life (0.21, 0.11-0.31)** compared with usual care, at or nearest to 8
weeks [@goh2019]. Goh did not grade the
certainty of the evidence and noted a high risk of bias in some domains
[@goh2019]. Obesity did
not blunt the effect on pain: 0.56 (0.28-0.84) with a BMI of 30 or more, against 0.46 below it (P
0.78) [@goh2019]. ->
[[Exercise Therapy for Osteoarthritis Pain and Function]]

Three further sources agree in direction, with smaller or thinner effects:

- In healthy adults over 65, power training improved function tests by SMD 0.73 against no training; no trial measured daily-life activity [@elhadouchi2022].
- In a network meta-analysis of resistance-training prescriptions, several improved mobility and gait speed in adults 55 and over, from few studies [@currier2023].
- In a review of 15 articles on interventions to extend healthspan, exercise, alone or within multidomain programs, went with better intrinsic capacity (IC) and QoL in 11 of 13 studies, but «the effect sizes were generally small» [@zheng2026healthspan].

-> [[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]], [[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]

### Nobody has measured the outcome you most want

**Keeping full function into old age, then a short decline, is the outcome this program aims at, and
no trial or cohort here measured it.** Trials report function at a few weeks or months; cohorts
count deaths and diagnoses. One figure on how fast decline runs from midlife: after 50, leg muscle
mass falls about 1-2% a year and strength 1.5-5% a year
[@cruzjentoft2019]. Strength falls
faster than mass, which is one more reason to track strength rather than size. ->
[[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]]

**Almost all the function and QoL trials enrolled older, frail or arthritic people, not middle-aged
adults with obesity.** The direction plausibly carries over to this group; the size of the effect is
not established for it. For dementia, the long-follow-up evidence genuinely disagrees.
One pooled analysis of individual cohorts found that once reverse causation was addressed, «physical
inactivity was not associated with all-cause dementia», with only an indication of excess risk in
inactive people who went on to develop cardiometabolic disease (HR 1.30, 0.79-2.14)
[@kivimaki2019inactivity]. A meta-analysis of 58 studies
found activity went with lower all-cause dementia, RR 0.80 (0.77-0.84), and the association held in follow-ups of 20 years or more
[@isomarkku2022]. ->
[[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]]

With function as the goal and fitness as its gauge, the aerobic half of the template comes down to two choices: how hard, and on what.

## Moderate effort on any low-impact mode raises fitness about as well as anything else

For a sedentary adult, **aerobic exercise at a moderate effort, around half of heart-rate reserve, is
enough to raise cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF)**, and the choice between walking, cycling, rowing and
water barely changes the result. Exercising at the level of standard activity guidance buys «≈10%
improvement in CRF in previously sedentary adults» [@ross2016].
Harder work buys more, and intervals are the fastest route, but they are an optional upgrade: the
mortality curve has already flattened at the moderate dose (see above). The gain itself is modest in
adults with obesity, and the largest pooled analysis could not separate it from chance.

### Moderate intensity is enough to start, and intensity moves fitness more than time

**Working harder raises fitness more than working longer.** The American Heart Association's review of the training trials
concludes that CRF is «more responsive to increases in intensity than increases in session duration or
frequency» [@ross2016]. How hard
you need to work depends on where you start. Below 10 METs of fitness, about 50% of heart-rate reserve
is adequate for a clinically meaningful gain of at least 1 MET; between 10 and 14 METs the range rises to
65-85%; above 14 METs it is over 85% [@ross2016].
The fitter you are, the harder you have to push to improve.

**The closest trial to this reader** enrolled 300 obese men and women (mean age 51.4, described as
«Sedentary obese adults») and trained them for 24 weeks [@ross2016].
Its intensity was set as a share of peak VO2, not of heart-rate reserve, so it illustrates the rule
rather than testing it directly. Fitness rose with both minutes and effort:

| Arm (5 days/week, 24 weeks) | CRF gain, text | CRF gain, table |
|---|---|---|
| \~30 min at 50% of peak VO2 | 9.4% | 7.7% |
| \~60 min at 50% of peak VO2 | 15.6% | 14.8% |
| same weekly energy as the 60-min arm, at 75% of peak VO2 | 19.6% | 19.6% |

The review reports the low-dose arm two ways, **9.4% in its text and 7.7% in its table**, for roughly 150
minutes a week at moderate effort [@ross2016].
STRRIDE, in overweight adults aged 40 to 65 training for 7 to 9 months, shows the same gradient: 6% for a
low amount at moderate intensity, 11% for a low amount at high intensity, and 18% for a high amount at
high intensity [@ross2016].

### In adults with obesity the gain is real in direction but modest

**Fitness rises in the expected direction in adults with obesity; the size is modest, and the largest
pooled analysis could not separate it from chance or rank the modes.** That analysis, a network
meta-analysis of 45 trials in 3,566 adults with a BMI of 30 or more, found absolute VO2max rose 7-15%
across the six programs it compared. Yet «Although all interventions resulted in an increase in
absolute VO2max, there were no statistically significant improvements in fitness found (Table 3).»
[@odonoghue2020] Its trials were mostly short,
about 20 weeks at 3 x 45 minutes a week, and leaned on indirect comparisons, while the 24-week trial
above is a single controlled test with significant gains. The two need not contradict each other; they
measure different things with different power ([[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]]).

**Track fitness in absolute terms.** If you are also losing weight, VO2 per kilogram rises
automatically as the kilograms fall. The same analysis warns that this can overstate fitness, so report
VO2max in litres per minute [@odonoghue2020].

### Pick the mode you will keep doing and are least likely to be hurt doing

**For fitness or function, the choice of mode makes little difference.** In 129 studies of
postmenopausal women, every mode raised CRF: aerobic (SMD 1.21), resistance (1.26), combined (1.47) and
water-based (0.83) [@khalafi2023]. The main text
gives no confidence intervals for these subgroups (they sit in the supplement), so combined training's
higher point estimate cannot be separated from the others here. Across both
that review and the obesity analysis, combined aerobic and resistance training sits at or near the top,
which makes it a defensible default ([[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]]).

**Low-impact options hold up.** In knee osteoarthritis, stationary cycling was «neither superior nor
inferior to other exercises» on pain, stiffness and function [@luan2020].
In older adults, water-based exercise did at least as well as land-based exercise: no different on
walking (6MWT) or dynamic balance (TUG), and better on the Berg Balance Scale, SMD 1.13 (0.25-2.00), and
the 30-second chair stand, 2.02 (0.50-3.54). Buoyancy reduces the load on painful joints [@deng2024].
So **choose the mode you will sustain and are least likely to be injured doing**
([[Is the Exercise Modality Doing Any Work]]).

The modes stop being interchangeable once the goal changes. For body fat and waist, programs that include
aerobic work beat resistance training alone [@odonoghue2020]. A
*fat-burning zone* program, set at about 42-49% of VO2max, beats doing nothing [@jiang2026].
But the review admitted only non-exercise or other fat-burning-zone controls, so it holds no comparison
with ordinary moderate aerobic work [@jiang2026].

### Hard intervals buy more fitness per minute, and feel worse while you do them

**High-intensity interval training (HIIT) raises fitness about as much as, or more than, moderate
continuous work.** In overweight and obese adults the reviews found similar or greater gains, from SMD
0.26 to 0.99, while sprint intervals were roughly equal to moderate work. Only 7 of the 24 reviews
addressed safety, and large safety studies are lacking. Most of the underlying reviews scored moderate
to critically low on quality [@poon2024].
In type 2 diabetes, HIIT raised peak VO2 by 3.37 ml/kg/min (1.88 to 4.87) more than continuous training,
at moderate certainty [@liu2019hiit].

**The cost is comfort.** During the session, HIIT felt worse than moderate continuous exercise by 1.07
points on a 10-point feeling scale (-1.47 to -0.67), and still felt worse just afterwards (-1.07,
-1.44 to -0.70), though enjoyment after the session favoured the intervals. Vigorous continuous
exercise felt the same as intervals [@niven2020], which
suggests the intensity drives the discomfort, not the interval format. Whether that discomfort makes people
quit is not established ([[Affective Response to High-Intensity Interval Exercise]]).

**Verdict: intervals are optional.** They suit someone short of time who tolerates hard effort. They are
not needed for the minimum, because the mortality benefit has already flattened at the moderate dose
.

### How you split the week matters less than the weekly total

**The weekend-warrior pattern**, cramming the week's activity into one or two sessions, carries a
similar mortality association to spreading it out. A meta-analysis found no difference between the two
patterns (meta-regression P 0.75 for cardiovascular and 0.51 for all-cause mortality), at very low
certainty [@kunutsor2022].
A large cohort found identical associations, HR 0.88 (0.83 to 0.93) for weekend warriors and 0.88 (0.84
to 0.91) for the regularly active [@odonovan2024].
In accelerometer data, cardiovascular outcomes did not differ between the patterns, though the
intervals were wide (myocardial infarction HR 1.12, 0.95 to 1.30): no difference was detected, not
equivalence shown [@khurshid2023].

**Session length tracked the benefit in one cohort.** «Stratified analyses showed that substantial
reductions in all-cause mortality risk only occurred when the duration of exercise sessions was at
least 30–60 min.» [@odonovan2024] Among
people whose sessions lasted under 30 minutes, weekend warriors showed HR 1.03 (0.94 to 1.14) and the
regularly active 0.95 (0.89 to 1.01) [@odonovan2024].
Despite the source's name, activity in this Mexico City cohort was self-reported. The
finding is an association across questionnaire categories, not proof that shorter sessions do nothing,
and the study did not assess total leisure-time activity volume [@odonovan2024].
The template's sessions of about 30 minutes are a practical choice consistent with it†, not a
requirement.

**Exercise snacks are a way in, not a substitute.** Brief vigorous bouts of 1-2 minutes spread through the
day [@wan2025] raised VO2max (SMD +1.43, 0.61 to 2.25), at very low certainty and only after one high-risk study was
excluded; bouts longer than 2 minutes worked better [@wan2025].
The authors judge it «unlikely that physically inactive individuals will meet the recommended physical
activity levels solely through this approach» [@wan2025]
([[Exercise Snacks and Cardiometabolic Health]]).

Activity done for other reasons counts unevenly. Walking to work was linked to lower cardiovascular
disease incidence only above «more than six miles a week» [@celismorales2017commuting],
and heavy occupational activity does not replace exercise ([[The Physical Activity Paradox]]). Breaking
up sitting lowers post-meal glucose (SMD -0.54, -0.70 to -0.37) and insulin (-0.56, -0.74 to -0.38), but
these are markers, not outcomes [@loh2019].

### The effort target is evidenced; the cheap ways to hit it are not

**Heart-rate reserve needs two numbers**: your resting heart rate and your maximum. The
sources describe three ways to measure fitness: a cardiopulmonary exercise test, a maximal test without
gas analysis, and an estimate from routine clinical numbers. A cycle test reads lower than a treadmill
test, «10% to 20% lower when using a cycle ergometer compared with a treadmill in untrained individuals»
[@ross2016].

**No validated field proxy is available.** None of the evidence behind this guide tests the talk test,
a rating of perceived exertion or an age-predicted maximum heart rate for this group. Perceived
exertion appears only as the way 57% of detraining trials set intensity [@buendiaromero2025].
So the 50% heart-rate-reserve target is evidenced; reaching it by feel, without a test, is a practical
choice the evidence leaves open†.

**For everyday movement, count steps first.** A pooled analysis of step cohorts found «inconsistent
evidence that step intensity had an association with mortality beyond total volume of steps»
[@paluch2022]. Pace is not irrelevant: the fastest 30-minute
and 60-minute cadences stayed linked to lower mortality after adjusting for steps (HR 0.67, 0.56-0.83,
and 0.67, 0.50-0.90) [@paluch2022] ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]).

Aerobic work does not build strength, and the evidence treats the two as separate adaptations. So the
resistance half of the program needs its own prescription.

## Two resistance sessions a week of two or more sets builds strength, and heavier loads build more

**Starting resistance training matters far more than which program you pick.** In Currier's network
meta-analysis of 178 strength and 119 hypertrophy trials, every one of 12 prescriptions beat no training
on average, by SMD 0.75-1.60 for strength and 0.10-0.66 for muscle size. Once people were training, the
prescriptions barely separated: «The 95% CrI contained zero for a striking 91% (101/111) of all
between-­RTx comparisons (table 2).» [@currier2023]
These are gains in strength and muscle size, not in health outcomes; what they buy for function and
survival comes below.

### Two sessions of two or more sets clears the tested floor for both strength and size

Currier's floor differs by outcome. Strength rose, at 95% probability, with **every program of at least
two sets or at least two sessions a week**; the interval crossed zero only for single-set once-weekly
training (heavy or light). For muscle size, lighter single-set programs and lighter once-weekly multiset
training separated from zero, while heavy single-set training two or three times a week and heavy
multiset training once a week did not.
[@currier2023] The template's two sessions
of two or more sets clears the strength floor and is not among the size programs that missed. The
authors attribute those misses to imprecise estimates from small study counts rather than to programs
that fail. [@currier2023]

So the evidence does not show that once-weekly training fails. It shows that single-set once-weekly
training is not shown to work for strength, and that heavy once-weekly multiset training is not shown to
work for size.
Neither result proves two sessions are *necessary*, and neither speaks to quality of life. Currier
coded load, sets and frequency in categories, so the floor marks the edge of what was tested, not a
located knee. -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]

### Heavy loads drive strength; sets drive size

Strength and size respond to different things. **Load drove strength:** heavy (≥80% of one-repetition
maximum), multiset training three or more times a week ranked first at SMD 1.60 (1.38-1.82), and all 9 of the 66 strength comparisons that excluded zero
favoured heavy multiset training (two or three sessions a week) over a lighter program. **Volume
drove size:** heavy multiset training twice a week ranked first at SMD 0.66 (0.47-0.85), but load mattered
little, and only 1 of 45 comparisons excluded zero (heavy multiset twice a week over light single-set three times a week). Training to failure did not explain the size gains in
these mostly untrained participants. [@currier2023]
That is why the template moves toward heavy loads after the build-up weeks, while two to three sets
already covers size.

**Free weights and machines build strength about equally.** Haugen pooled 13 trials comparing free weights with
machines: on a neutral test, dynamic strength differed by SMD 0.084 (-0.106 to 0.273). People got
stronger in whichever mode they trained. Whether injury risk differs between the two is uncertain.
[@haugen2023freeweight] No meta-analysis held
here compares bodyweight exercise with loaded training, so that choice is an open question, not a settled
equivalence. -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]

### Lifting is associated with lower mortality, but its stronger case is function

People who do any muscle-strengthening activity have lower all-cause mortality than those who do none,
RR 0.85 (0.79-0.93), adjusted for aerobic activity. But the evidence is very uncertain: self-reported,
observational, and graded very low. People who do both strength and aerobic work fare better still, RR
0.60 (0.54-0.67). [@momma2022]
Doing more than zero and up to two sessions a week was associated with HR 0.79 (0.66-0.95); more
sessions were not associated with further benefit. [@saeidifard2019]

In WHO's evidence review, meeting both the aerobic and strength guidelines, versus neither, gave HR 0.71
(0.57-0.87) at moderate certainty, from one pooled analysis. [@who2020]
These estimates pool overlapping cohorts, so they are one line of evidence refined three times, not
three independent confirmations. -> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]

Strength training's firmer evidence is on physical function. In older adults (mean age over 65), power training (lifting at high speed) improved function tests by SMD 0.43
(0.23-0.62) more than conventional strength training, at high certainty. That is a refinement for later
life, not part of the middle-age minimum. [@elhadouchi2022]
-> [[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]]

### For people with obesity, lifting protects muscle during weight loss

The trials closest to this reader enrolled older adults with obesity. In sarcopenic obesity (mean age
68), resistance training three times a week for about 12 weeks may have improved physical performance a
little, SMD 0.36 (0.03-0.69, low to very low certainty), and was «generally safe». Most participants were women.
[@poloferrero2025]

Dieting removes muscle as well as fat, and lifting stops most of that loss. In 6 trials of overweight or
obese older adults (mean age over 57) cutting calories, adding resistance training cut lean-mass loss by
93.5%, keeping 0.819 kg (0.364-1.273) more lean mass; fat and weight loss were similar with or without lifting.
[@sardeli2018] In the IDEA trial, adults with
obesity and knee osteoarthritis who lost weight by diet lost 4.2-4.7 kg of lean mass over 18 months, even
in the arm that also exercised, though lean mass as a share of body weight rose.
[@messier2013idea] Anyone dieting
should lift as well.

Training drives these gains; protein is a modest adjunct, and the gram target for people with obesity is
unsettled. -> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]

### Exercise protects bone, and a cuff is a partial workaround when heavy loads are not possible

In postmenopausal women, exercise training raised bone mineral density by SMD 0.27-0.41 depending on site
[@mohebbi2023] and cut
major osteoporotic fractures, RR 0.75 (0.59-0.94) [@hoffmann2022];
in the raw counts, about 5 fewer fractures per 1,000 participant-years (151 in 9,715 exercising vs 196
in 9,592 not). -> [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]

For someone who cannot load heavily, light loads with a blood-flow-restriction cuff build muscle size
about as well as heavy loads in older adults, but less strength (SMD -0.42, -0.70 to -0.14). No
adverse-event data were reported. It is a workaround, not an upgrade.
[@centner2019bfr]
-> [[Blood Flow Restriction Training]]

### Track the weight lifted, a chair stand and grip

**Log the load lifted for each exercise**; it is the variable that drives strength. Two simple tests
check strength outside the gym:

- **Chair stand:** the time to «rise ﬁve times from a seated position without using his or her arms».
  EWGSOP2 calls it a «qualiﬁed but convenient measure of strength», because it also tests endurance.
  [@cruzjentoft2019]
- **Grip:** measured on a calibrated handheld dynamometer
  [@cruzjentoft2019]. Weakness cut-offs of
  <26 kg for men and <16 kg for women flagged higher risk in UK Biobank.
  [@celismorales2018]

These cut-offs flag risk; they are not levels to steer toward. The evidence held here gives
no conversion from a percentage of one-repetition maximum to a repetition count, which is why the
template's rep gauge is marked †. -> [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]],
[[Grip Strength and Mortality]]

All of these doses work only if the person is still training, and uninjured, months later. That makes the
first eight weeks about building tolerance, not fitness.

## Start below the target and build up over about eight weeks, because tendons lag behind muscle

**Start below the full program, raise it in small absolute steps over about eight weeks, keep impact low at first, and then keep going**: a short break does not undo the gains, but stopping for good does.

### Tendons need about two months of heavy loading to catch up

Muscle gets stronger faster than the tendon that carries its force. A meta-analysis of tendon
training found that **high loads (>70% of maximum) stiffened tendons, SMD 0.90 (CI 0.71, 1.08),
while low loads did nothing measurable, 0.04 (CI −0.46, 0.53)**
[@bohm2015]. The type of contraction made no difference:
isometric 0.95, concentric-eccentric 0.82, eccentric 1.04 (p>0.5)
[@bohm2015]. The tendons responded «within 2 months», at
the authors' studied range of 8-14 weeks and 2-4 sessions a week, and the authors note the «slower
adaptation rates of tendon compared to muscle»
[@bohm2015]. That gap is the reason for the eight-week
build-up†.

These trials enrolled people aged 18-50 [@bohm2015],
237 of 264 of them men, mostly already recreationally active
[@bohm2015]. The low-load result rests on only 5
interventions, and programs of 12 weeks or more seemed more effective (0.91 vs 0.81, not a significant
difference) [@bohm2015]. Obese or deconditioned beginners
barely appear, so they should take **more caution in the first weeks, not less**
[[Tendon Adaptation to Mechanical Loading]].

### Raise the load in small absolute steps, not by a percentage rule

Nobody has pinned down a safe rate of increase. A review of running-load studies found «very limited
evidence» that a sudden jump in training load raises injury risk
[@damsted2018]. Novice and amateur runners who added
more than 30% a week had **HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96; 2.66)** against those who added under 10%, an
interval that crosses no effect; a 10% vs 24% comparison was null, HR 0.8 (0.6; 1.3)
[@damsted2018]. The review found «no evidence» for
the popular *10% rule* [@damsted2018].

This is missing evidence, not proof that jumps are safe. For a beginner, percentages also mislead: going from 2 km to 3 km a week is a 50% rise but a small absolute step. **Add a little at a time and judge by how the body responds the next day**† [[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]].

### Keep impact low at first, and leave running out of the minimum

Recreational running does not appear to wear out knees in the general population: OR 0.86 (0.69,
1.07) for hip and knee osteoarthritis vs sedentary people, 0.83 (0.70, 0.99) for the knee alone,
mostly from case-control studies at high risk of bias
[@alentorngeli2017]. But the same review states
it «still remains unclear» whether running is safe for joints in an overweight or obese person who also
has a high-impact occupational workload and a previous injury. It suggests, as a hedged judgement, that
this combination may raise osteoarthritis risk, and it recommends no safe running quantity
[@alentorngeli2017].

Those two features are the ones that raise knee risk. In observational data, each extra BMI unit
carried **aOR 1.17 (1.10, 1.24)** for radiographic knee osteoarthritis, and a prior knee injury
**OR 2.67 (1.41, 5.05)**
[@duong2025kneeoa]. So **walking,
cycling, swimming or rowing come first for a heavier beginner**, and running is an option to add
later, not part of the minimum† [[Knee Osteoarthritis Incidence and Risk Factors]].

Gait analysis and screening tests will not tell you who gets hurt. In a review of prospective
studies, «twenty-three of twenty-five pooled analyses detected no relationship» between
biomechanical measures and running injury, and the authors found insufficient evidence to use them
for screening [@peterson2022]. Bone is rarely
the limit for a beginner: in runners, a previous stress fracture (OR 4.99, 2.91 to 8.56) and female
sex (2.31) were the risk factors that held up
[@wright2015], and loading builds bone density
[@wright2015]. For an obese beginner, the tendons and
joints set the pace [[Stress Fracture Risk Factors in Runners]].

### Strength training protects, and exercise itself rarely causes harm

Lifting is part of the injury protection, not an added risk. Across 25 RCTs with 26,610
participants, **strength training cut sports injuries, RR 0.315 (0.207-0.480), I2 0%, while
stretching did not, RR 0.963 (0.846-1.095)**
[@lauersen2013injury].
The participants were mostly young athletes on sport-specific programs, so the size of the effect
for an older beginner is unknown [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]].

Exercise rarely hurts people who already have pain or arthritis. A Cochrane overview of 21 reviews
on chronic pain found that none of the exercise interventions «appeared to cause harm»
[@geneen2017chronicpain], and physical
function improved in 14 of the 21; only 25% of the included studies reported on possible harm
[@geneen2017chronicpain]. In a
review of 63 RCTs of people with arthritis (49 meta-analysed), «No detrimental effects of PA were reported in any study»
[@rauschosthoff2018exercise].

Two groups need a different start:

- **Osteoporosis:** in 8 trials of community-dwelling adults, exercise slightly raised adverse events, mostly minor such as mild muscle or joint pain, RR 1.08 (95% CI 1.04-1.13); «Serious adverse events were rare» and could not be attributed to the exercise. Activities that flex the spine (some yoga moves, sit-ups) were linked to vertebral fractures, though these were rare [@kunutsor2018adverse].
  Leaving loaded spinal flexion out is a cautious choice†.
- **Frailty:** the standard program may be too intense; start lower and adjust [[Frailty]]. This deliverable is not written for that group.

### Make it easy to keep going; supervision is optional

Advice alone does not get people moving. The Cochrane pain overview notes, citing SIGN 2013, that
simply giving advice to exercise is not enough to bring about significant change
[@geneen2017chronicpain] [[Chronic Pain and Physical Activity]]. A tracker
helps a little: wearables added **SMD 0.23 (0.15 to 0.32), about 627 steps a day (417 to 862)**, at
low certainty, and the two studies lasting 12 months or longer reported lower adherence to wearing the tracker
[@brickwood2019wearable].

Supervision is not required. In adults over 60, supervised programs beat home programs reliably on
only one outcome, **knee-extension strength, SMD 0.18 (0.07, 0.30)**; the quality-of-life edge, 0.21
(0.02, 0.40), was not robust in sensitivity analysis
[@gomezredondo2024]. Attendance was 81% in both
arms, with similar rates of adverse events and falls, and supervised arms usually trained harder
[@gomezredondo2024]. The authors call home
exercise «a safe and cost-effective alternative»
[@gomezredondo2024]. **A few supervised sessions
to learn lifting technique are a reasonable choice†, not a requirement.**

Expect dropout. In weight-loss trials lasting at least a year, attrition averaged 29% at one year [@franz2007]. Plan the program around a week that goes wrong.

### A short break does not reset you; stopping for good does

Adults over 60 kept much of their gain in physical function after a layoff. Across 15 RCTs (787
people, programs of 8-43 weeks, breaks of 4-36 weeks), function stayed better than in non-training
control groups, **ES 0.88 [0.47 to 1.29]**, I2 81%
[@buendiaromero2025]. The authors conclude that
training twice a week for over 2 months before a break of at least a month «can significantly
preserve functional capacity» [@buendiaromero2025].
An ES of 0.88 is a standardized effect size; it does not mean 88% of the gain was kept
[[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]].

Stopping for good is different. In knee osteoarthritis, exercise benefits «peak at 2 months» and
then fade until they are «no better than the usual care group at 9 to 18 months»
[@goh2019]. **Maintenance means continuing
the full program.** No study we drew on tested a reduced maintenance dose, so none is offered here
 [[Exercise Therapy for Osteoarthritis Pain and Function]].

### Exercise is for function and fitness; weight loss needs diet

People who start exercising offset part of the energy they burn (compensation). One
analysis put average compensation at 18%, with a very wide spread between people (SD ±93%)
[@riou2015]. Another, analysing daily and basal
energy expenditure across people, put compensation through lower basal energy expenditure at 28% on average, rising from 27.7% at the 10th BMI percentile to 49.2%
at the 90th [@careau2021].

In a network meta-analysis of
exercise in adults with obesity, weight loss was «minimal», −0.05 to −1.01 kg across exercise types,
and the authors, citing earlier reviews, note that a calorie deficit is necessary
[@odonoghue2020]. Exercise alone still
shrank visceral fat, ES −0.28 (−0.37 to −0.19)
[@recchia2023]. **Judge this
program by what it does for strength, fitness and daily function; if weight loss is the goal, change
the diet too.**

Put together, the template lands almost exactly on published guidance.

## The template matches guidance, and the evidence cannot yet see the outcome that matters most

**The program above lands on the published guidance: at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus
strength work on two days.** That agreement is itself a finding. Where this guide differs is in how it reads
the guidance number: for mortality it is a **target inside the flat part of the curve, not the dose where
benefit begins**. And the outcome this reader cares about most, keeping function into old age and then a
short decline, is one no study in this appraisal measured.

### The guidance number sits where the mortality curve has already flattened

WHO advises adults to do 150-300 minutes of moderate activity a week (or 75-150 minutes of vigorous
activity), plus muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week
[@shailendra2022]. The template sits at
the low end of both.

**150-300 minutes a week is about 21-43 minutes a day**, and that band overlaps the region, roughly 20-40
minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity, where device-measured mortality risk stops falling
[@who2020]
 -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]. Benefit starts far
lower, with the first step off the floor (see above). So the guidance figure is
a sensible place to aim, not a line below which effort is wasted.

**The two-day strength figure sits at the lowest dose the trials found effective.** Currier's network
meta-analysis says its results support the twice-weekly guidance, and also that resistance training below
the recommended levels still builds strength and size; its conclusion is that «adults should engage in RT,
even if they cannot meet existing recommendations»
[@currier2023]. On mortality, the 2-day figure
falls inside the broad region of roughly 30-80 or more minutes a week where the cohort data show most of
the benefit -> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]].

Two other pieces of common advice line up with this reading. Exercise snacks, the 1-2 minute vigorous
bouts [@wan2025], are a way in for people who cannot meet the guidelines, «rather than a replacement for these
recommendations» [@wan2025]. And the *10% a week*
progression rule, often attached to exercise advice, has no evidence behind it (see the build-up section)
[@damsted2018].

WHO rated the device-measured activity evidence (the pooled individual-participant analysis behind the
curve above) high even though it comes from observational cohorts, upgrading it because of
«no serious limitations in the body of evidence, individual participant-level data meta-analysis, and
evidence of a dose response relationship»
[@who2020]
-> [[Upgrading Observational Evidence]].

**Why this guide reads the same number differently.** The two do not disagree about the evidence, and the
guidance has not fallen behind it. A guideline body advises a whole population, so it writes one number
that is easy to communicate and safe for everyone. This guide reads that number as a point inside a range
that starts much lower. That is a difference of purpose, not of evidence.

### Drugs can match exercise on blood pressure, but not on function

For blood pressure, a drug is a realistic alternative. Across all trials, exercise lowered systolic
pressure by 4.84 mmHg and drugs by 8.80 mmHg, but the drug trials enrolled people with higher starting
pressure. In people with hypertension (140 mmHg or higher), exercise lowered it by 8.96 mmHg, and the
difference from drugs was 0.18 mmHg (95% CrI -1.35 to 1.68), an indirect comparison
[@naci2019exercise]. No drug builds strength or
improves physical function, so on the outcomes this program targets, exercise has no drug substitute
 -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### Balance training is where the program should grow later in life

In adults with a mean age of 76, exercise cuts falls by about 195 per 1,000 people per year (rate ratio
0.77, 0.71-0.83, high certainty). Balance and functional training carries that effect (rate ratio 0.76,
0.70-0.81, high certainty); resistance training alone shows no clear effect (rate ratio 1.14, 0.67-1.97,
very low certainty) [@sherrington2019]
-> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]. Balance work is the natural later addition. It is not
part of the minimum for a middle-aged adult.

### What the evidence cannot yet tell you

Besides the untested package itself (see the template section), five questions stay open:

- Almost all the function and quality-of-life trials enrolled older, frail or knee-osteoarthritis groups,
  not middle-aged adults with obesity. The direction probably carries over; the size is unknown for this
  group.
- No study gives a smaller dose that maintains the gains, so the plan is to keep going
  -> [[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]].
- No validated cheap way to hit 50% of heart-rate reserve without a test (talk test, perceived effort,
  age-predicted maximum) is evidenced here -> [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]].
- Bodyweight training has not been compared with loaded training
  -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].
- How much people eat more or move less to offset a program like this one is measured only in aggregate
  -> [[Exercise Energy Compensation]].

This appraisal checks that the evidence hangs together and that each source is reported faithfully. It
does not check whether anyone who followed the program ended up better off.

## What to do

- **Lift twice a week:** 5-6 whole-body exercises†, 2-3 sets each, at moderate loads for about eight weeks,
  then work toward heavy loads ([[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]).
- **Do about 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic work** — brisk walking, cycling, rowing or water
  exercise at about half of heart-rate reserve — in whatever split you will keep
  ([[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]]).
- **Walk more and sit less on top of that.** Any intensity counts ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]).
- **Start at about half the aerobic minutes†, with lighter loads, and add in small absolute steps**
  ([[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]]).
- **After two to three months†, swap one aerobic session for intervals** if you want faster fitness gains
  and can tolerate the harder effort.
- **Keep it going.** A short break does not reset you; stopping for good does ([[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]], [[Exercise Therapy for Osteoarthritis Pain and Function]]).
- **Track the gauges** — the weight you lift, a timed chair stand, grip strength, daily steps — but **judge
  success by what you can do** ([[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]]).

Two questions stay open: how much less training would hold the gains, and whether any of this keeps
function intact into old age.
