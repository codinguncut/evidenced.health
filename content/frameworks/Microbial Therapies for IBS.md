**The decision this page serves.** For an adult with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), does deliberately
modulating the gut microbiome — swallowing a probiotic, feeding resident microbes a prebiotic, a
synbiotic combining both, or taking a gut-selective antibiotic (rifaximin) — reduce symptoms enough to
be worth doing? IBS is in scope as a prevalent, modifiable-exposure-improvable condition with symptom
and quality-of-life endpoints; per-patient drug *selection/dosing* is not, but rifaximin's efficacy and
limitations as a standard option ARE (the pharmacotherapy taper). This is the **supplement/drug** lever
for the same condition [[Low-FODMAP Diet for IBS]] serves with the **dietary** lever — a companion, not
a competitor.

The evidence here is one 2018 gold systematic review with meta-analysis of 66 RCTs (67 trials); it is
also **the kind of underlying evidence the [[Gut Microbiome and Health]] recommendation (WGO 2023)
rests on** — so cite Ford for effect and certainty, cite the guideline for what-to-do. The two are the
*same evidence base viewed at two altitudes*, not independent corroboration.


## Probiotics — a real class-average benefit that is strain-blind, so no strain is proven

Fifty-three probiotic RCTs (5545 patients) were pooled. Combination probiotics showed a benefit on
symptom persistence: «Combination probiotics were assessed in 21 RCTs ... containing 1931 patients,
with a significant effect on symptoms (RR = 0.79; 95% CI 0.68 to 0.91)»
[@ford2018probiotics] (NNT 7). **But that pooled
number is the load-bearing trap**, not the finding: it carries significant heterogeneity (I2 = 72%,
P < 0.001) and funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger P = 0.06, so probable publication bias), and it averages
across combinations that point in opposite directions.

- **The strain-heterogeneity beneath the average.** Specific combinations diverge: a seven-strain mix
  RR 0.48 (95% CI 0.24-0.94) and LacClean Gold RR 0.59 (0.37-0.93) helped, while the
  L. paracasei F19 + L. acidophilus La5 + B. lactis Bb12 combination showed **no benefit** (RR 0.92,
  0.76-1.11) and VSL#3 was non-significant (RR 0.82, 0.52-1.30). Single genera mostly failed: whole
  Lactobacillus RR 0.82 (0.63-1.06, no clear benefit; I2 = 83%), Saccharomyces RR 0.92 (0.82-1.03, not
  superior), Bifidobacterium RR 0.70 (0.48-1.01, trend only). The positive single-strain signals
  (L. plantarum DSM 9843 RR 0.67, 0.51-0.87, NNT 3; E. coli DSM17252; S. faecium) each rest on **one to
  three small trials**, and the L. plantarum heterogeneity persisted (I2 = 63%).
  [@ford2018probiotics]
- **Verdict: benefit at the class-average level, but no specific product is robustly proven.** Ford's
  own conclusion: «Which particular combination, species, or strain of probiotics are effective for IBS
  remains, for the most part, unclear»; «it was not possible to draw definitive conclusions about their
  efficacy»; «it remains unclear which combination, species, or strain should be preferred in the
  individual patient.» [@ford2018probiotics] So
  *take a probiotic for IBS* is under-specified advice: the exposure is the **strain**, not the word.

This is exactly the discipline [[Gut Microbiome and Health]] states as WGO's central rule — effects are
strain- and dose-specific, and strain-blind pooling «should be avoided». Ford **quantifies and
demonstrates** that rule inside IBS: the pooled effect exists yet dissolves the moment you ask *which*
organism. `[inferred from @ford2018probiotics] — Ford is the granular evidence behind the guideline's discipline (type-F refinement), not an
independent route to it.`

## Prebiotics and synbiotics — insufficient evidence, not a demonstrated null

**Prebiotics: three small RCTs, no clear benefit.** A 20 g fructooligosaccharide trial (n = 98) found
58.0% vs 65.2% improvement (not significant); a 2.5 g short-chain FOS trial (n = 79) was underpowered
and null; a trans-galactooligosaccharide crossover (n = 60) improved global symptom scores in both
dose arms versus washout but had no effect on abdominal pain.
[@ford2018probiotics]

**Synbiotics: two RCTs (198 patients).** Individually positive but the pooled continuous estimate was
non-significant because of extreme heterogeneity (SMD -1.73; 95% CI -3.73 to 0.27; I2 = 96%).
[@ford2018probiotics]

Ford's summary — «this meta-analysis has demonstrated little evidence for the use of prebiotics or
synbiotics in IBS» [@ford2018probiotics] — is best
read as **insufficient evidence** (three and two tiny trials respectively), NOT a proven no-effect. The
expectancy test: with so few, small, underpowered trials, we could not yet expect to have seen the
gradient even if one exists. Held as a G-gap, not a null. — adequately powered prebiotic/synbiotic
RCTs would move this from insufficient toward benefit or no-effect; none is held.`

## Rifaximin — the best-evidenced, but modest and non-durable, drug option

The gut-selective antibiotic rifaximin is the cleanest result in the paper. «There were five trials of
similar design that used rifaximin in non-constipated IBS patients, which was more effective than
placebo (RR of symptoms persisting = 0.84; 95% CI 0.79-0.90)»
[@ford2018probiotics], with **no heterogeneity**
(I2 = 0%) and NNT 9. The low-risk-of-bias subset holds: «There were four low risk of bias rifaximin
trials, assessing 1966 patients ... There remained a significant effect in favour of active therapy»
(RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.82-0.93, NNT 11).
[@ford2018probiotics] Rifaximin is now licensed for
IBS-D in the US, and appeared safe (no excess adverse events; near-zero de-novo C. difficile).

The limitations that size this rock (pharmacotherapy taper — efficacy AND its limits):

- **Modest and non-durable.** Retreatment works but weakly: «the efficacy was modest after each course
  of treatment, and the long-term safety of repeated courses of rifaximin, and how many times to
  re-treat patients whose symptoms recur remains uncertain.»
  [@ford2018probiotics] Symptoms relapse; this is a
  repeatable short course, not a cure.
- **Mechanism unclear, and not via a lasting microbiome change.** «Studies that have evaluated the
  effect of rifaximin on the microbiome, show that any changes are limited, and are not sustained.»
  [@ford2018probiotics] The SIBO rationale is
  contested (breath tests are unreliable), so the drug works by an unresolved route.
- **Cost and stewardship.** A branded minimally-absorbed antibiotic taken in repeated courses carries
  cost and antimicrobial-resistance monitoring the dietary levers do not.

Neomycin (RR 0.73) and norfloxacin (RR 0.63) each had a single positive trial — too thin to conclude.
[@ford2018probiotics]

## Why honest uncertainty, not confident advice — the expectancy and bias discipline

IBS symptom endpoints are **self-reported and subjective** (global symptom relief, abdominal pain), so
these trials sit exactly where placebo/expectancy effects are largest — the same discipline
[[Low-FODMAP Diet for IBS]] is held to. Two mechanical warnings from Ford reinforce it: probiotic
analyses showed funnel asymmetry (probable publication bias inflating the pooled effect), and half the
probiotic trials (27/53) were only unclear risk of bias. There is also a **popularity-vs-evidence gap**:
Ford notes «most believe probiotics to be a benign therapy» and that over 90% of surveyed clinicians
use them, «despite the fact that any effect of probiotics in IBS is poorly understood»
[@ford2018probiotics] — attention runs ahead of
effect size, the anti-signal the telos warns of.

**Conflicts of interest, appraised on the same bar.** The study was funded by the American College of
Gastroenterology; two authors declare industry ties relevant here — one to probiotic companies, one
(advisory/speaker) to the maker of rifaximin. [@ford2018probiotics] Under symmetric standards this earns neither extra suspicion nor a pass: the review
argues *against* easy enthusiasm (it deflates the pooled probiotic effect and calls rifaximin only
modest), which is the opposite direction to the COIs, so the appraisal stands on its methods.

## Decision framing (Layer 3)

- **Who this is for.** An adult with diagnosed IBS whose symptoms are not controlled — a real remediable
  gap. None of these interventions has evidence in people without IBS.
- **Ranking the levers at the margin.** For **non-constipated / IBS-D**, rifaximin is the
  best-evidenced single option (consistent, homogeneous, NNT 9), but modest and needing repeat courses —
  weigh it against the dietary lever [[Low-FODMAP Diet for IBS]] (also short-term, also subjective
  endpoints) as the realistic alternative, not as a first reflex. **Probiotics** are worth a bounded
  trial only if a *specific* studied strain/combination is chosen (e.g. L. plantarum DSM 9843, or a
  studied multi-strain mix), with low expectations and a stop rule — a generic *probiotic* is a
  coin-toss. **Prebiotics and synbiotics** are not yet supported; no recommendation either way.
- **Adherence and reversibility.** All are reversible and low-harm short-term; the honest framing is a
  *time-limited trial with a planned review*, never an open-ended regimen, and never a claim that these
  fix the underlying disorder.

## Value type and standing

Provisional-C domain opener: a single gold meta-analysis induces the IBS microbial-therapy question
into the fabric, as a companion cluster to [[Low-FODMAP Diet for IBS]]. It also carries a **type-F
refinement** of [[Gut Microbiome and Health]] — quantifying the WGO strain-specificity discipline with
the actual IBS pooled/subgroup numbers (same evidence base, not independent-E). Confidence **medium**:
the rifaximin result is internally strong (I2 = 0%, low-RoB-robust) but modest; the probiotic result is
a heterogeneous, publication-biased class-average with no proven strain; prebiotic/synbiotic evidence is
insufficient. The banked cross-source synthesis is owed when a second independent gold SR (or a newer
strain-specific network meta-analysis) lands.
