## Verdict

Fibre is a real but modest supporting lever, not a big rock. Reaching roughly 25-30 g/day
from food -- whole grains, legumes, fruit and vegetables -- is worth doing, and most people
fall about 40% short of it, so *get there* is a genuine change in what someone eats rather
than a tweak. But the effect is neither one size nor one mechanism, and the honest reading
turns on keeping its parts apart.


- The large numbers -- **15-30% lower** risk of dying, heart disease, diabetes and
  colorectal cancer -- are **observational**, from self-reported cohorts. They are upgraded
  above bare correlation by a monotone dose-response and a concordant trial arm, but not
  RCT-proven; bias screening leaves only all-cause mortality and CVD "convincing," with the
  cancer signal weaker and contested.
- Where fibre is trialled as a dosable isolate, the **causal** effect is genuine and
  **small**: viscous/soluble fibre cuts LDL cholesterol by a few tenths of a mmol/L, and
  higher fibre trims body weight by about 0.37 kg.
- The effect is **type-specific** -- viscous fibre for LDL and glycaemia, bulking fibre for
  transit, fermentable fibre for the microbiome -- so a psyllium sachet, a bowl of bran and a
  plate of beans are not interchangeable across outcomes.
- **Isolates are better-evidenced than whole foods**, but that is a fact about study design
  (an isolate can be blinded and dosed; a food cannot), not evidence that supplements beat
  food -- and the well-graded object is itself small.
- Several outcomes are **gaps**, stated plainly: the glycaemia magnitude, GI transit and
  constipation, gastric emptying, and satiety measured as such.
- The **downsides** are minor and managed by preparation and pace -- gas, rare obstruction
  with too little fluid, phytate mineral-binding in named strata -- so they shift the *how*,
  not the *whether*.
- 30 g is the **edge of the evidence, not a biological knee**: more is fine and probably
  slightly better, bounded above by tolerance, not by a demonstrated optimum.

The ceiling is itself the finding. For someone whose big rocks are already handled, "your
remaining fibre lever is real but modest, and several of its outcomes are gaps" is a
decision -- it licenses stopping the search for a fibre miracle -- delivered as a result, not
a failure to find one.

The rest of this answers it type by type and outcome by outcome, because the effect is
neither one size nor one mechanism.

## Fibre acts by type

«Fibre» collects several polysaccharides that behave differently in the gut, and it is the
differences — not the shared label — that carry the outcomes. Three functional axes matter, and a
single food can sit on more than one:

- **Soluble / viscous** — beta-glucan (oats, barley), psyllium, pectin, guar, and the fibres in
  pulses. Dissolved, they form a gel, and that viscosity is the mechanism behind the lipid and
  glycaemic effects. «Soluble» and «viscous» overlap but are not identical (some soluble fibres form
  no gel), so **viscous** is the load-bearing term whenever the LDL/glucose route is the point.
- **Insoluble / bulking** — wheat bran, cellulose. These add faecal bulk and speed transit, and carry
  little lipid effect.
- **Fermentable** — much soluble fibre plus resistant starch and inulin. Gut bacteria ferment it into
  short-chain fatty acids; this is the prebiotic / microbiome channel.

This maps type to mechanism to the outcome each is best evidenced on:

| Fibre type | Mechanism | Where the outcome is taken up |
|---|---|---|
| Soluble / viscous | gel formation slows absorption and sequesters bile acids | LDL cholesterol; post-meal glycaemia |
| Insoluble / bulking | adds faecal bulk, shortens transit time | gut transit / constipation |
| Fermentable | fermented to short-chain fatty acids | the gut microbiome |

The axes are distinct, not nested: oat beta-glucan is soluble, viscous **and** fermentable at once,
while wheat bran is insoluble and poorly fermented. So a psyllium sachet, a bowl of bran and a plate
of beans are not interchangeable across outcomes — each loads a different mechanism.

It is the viscous *mechanism*, not the food source, that carries the LDL effect: oat, psyllium, pectin
and guar were each significant, and fibre type was not a predictor once dose was accounted for
[@brown1999]. Insoluble and
fermentable fibres act through other routes — bulk and transit, and fermentation to short-chain fatty
acids — that the observational whole-food data capture but the LDL trials do not.



Take the outcomes in turn, heaviest evidence first.

## Hard outcomes: mortality, heart disease, diabetes

These carry fibre's largest numbers on its weakest design. In prospective cohorts, higher total dietary fibre intake is *associated* with lower risk across the board. Reynolds' WHO-commissioned meta-analysis (185 cohorts) reports, highest-versus-lowest intake: all-cause mortality RR 0.85 (0.79 to 0.91), coronary heart disease incidence RR 0.76 (0.69 to 0.83), and type 2 diabetes incidence RR 0.84 (0.78 to 0.90) — each GRADE moderate. The source's own summary is «a 15–31% reduction in the risk of specified critical outcomes» [@reynolds2019].

In absolute terms the effect is modest. For all-cause mortality and heart disease, «this reduction translates into 13 fewer deaths (95% CI eight to 18) and six fewer cases of coronary heart disease (four to seven) per 1000 participants over the duration of the studies» [@reynolds2019]. A large relative reduction sitting on a small baseline is a small number of prevented events.

The dose-response *tracks* linearly with no plateau located on hard outcomes: «Figure 1 shows dose-response relationships for total fibre intake and total mortality, incidence of coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer, many of which are linear with no sign of a plateau within the available data ... the greatest benefits were observed for individuals consuming 25–29 g per day» [@reynolds2019]. So no knee is shown here; every increment appears to pay, and the burden falls on whoever would claim a plateau to locate it (what the 30 g figure means is a separate question, taken up later).

But this is self-reported intake in observational cohorts, and two biases pull in opposite directions. The healthy-user confound tends to inflate the association — high-fibre eaters differ in many other health-protective ways. Dietary measurement error runs the other way, attenuating real gradients toward the null ([[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]). Net, the wiki holds these at `confidence: medium`: upgraded above bare correlation by the monotone dose-response plus a concordant trial arm ([[Upgrading Observational Evidence]]), but not RCT-proven.

A bias-screening umbrella review (Veronese 2018, 18 prior meta-analyses) tightens *which* of these to believe. Applying Ioannidis-style credibility diagnostics, it finds «a higher intake of dietary fibers was convincingly associated only with a decreased likelihood of early mortality and CVD» — the two class-I "convincing" associations, while type 2 diabetes reaches only class-III "suggestive" [@veronese2018]. This is not independent confirmation — Veronese re-pools the same cohort literature — but it adds a bias diagnostic: the robust core is all-cause mortality and CVD, not the wider outcome list ([[Dietary Fibre and Health]]).

One discipline governs this section. These mortality and heart-disease magnitudes are *observational*. They must not be stacked underneath the causal LDL effect from controlled trials and read as one *powerfully proven* number: the observational hard-outcome leg and the trial-grade LDL leg are different quantities — different design, different exposure (total food fibre versus a viscous isolate), and different endpoint (a hard outcome versus a cholesterol surrogate). Where fibre can instead be dosed and blinded, the causal effect is real and small — the subject of the next section.

## LDL cholesterol: causal, small, viscous fibre only

Where fibre is dosed and blinded as a soluble/viscous isolate, its effect is
trial-grade and causal — and it is the one place on the whole outcome menu
that is. Brown's meta-analysis of 67 controlled feeding trials found soluble
fibre at 2-10 g/day cut LDL cholesterol by **-0.057 mmol/L per gram** (95% CI
-0.070 to -0.044), with total cholesterol close behind (-0.045 mmol/L per gram).
Read the magnitude, not just the sign: at a realistic dose this is a few tenths
of a mmol/L. Brown states it plainly — «For example, 3 g soluble fiber from oats
(3 servings of oatmeal, 28 g each) can decrease total and LDL cho- lesterol by
<0.13 mmol/L. Increasing soluble fiber can make only a small contribution to
dietary therapy to lower cholesterol.»
[@brown1999]

**The mechanism is gel-forming, and its detail is unsettled.** Brown proposes
that «some soluble fibers bind bile acids or cholesterol during the intraluminal
formation of micelles ... The resulting reduction in the cholesterol content of
liver cells leads to an up-regulation of the LDL receptors and thus increased
clearance of LDL cholesterol»
[@brown1999]
— but adds that «The mechanism by which fiber lowers blood cholesterol remains
undefined» and that increased bile-acid excretion may not fully account for the
drop. So the effect on the marker is causal; the pathway is a reasonable
bile-acid-sequestration story, not a settled one.

**Only the viscous fraction carries it.** Type of soluble fibre (oat, psyllium,
pectin, guar) was not a predictor once dose was adjusted, but insoluble fibre
does not lower LDL at all: «Water- insoluble wheat fiber and cellulose have no
effect unless they displace foods supplying saturated fats and cholesterol.»
[@brown1999]
So a bowl of wheat bran is not a cholesterol intervention; oats, psyllium and
pectin are, modestly.

**Pulses give a second, separate estimate — read it as a different evidence
class.** ESC's guideline states that «A single portion of pulses (legumes) a day
low- ers LDL-C by 0.2 mmol/L and is associated with a lower risk of CHD.»
[@esc2021] The two halves of
that sentence are not the same kind of claim, and ESC runs them together: the
**-0.2 mmol/L** is a magnitude on a surrogate (a whole-food *portion*, not a
per-gram isolate — a guideline summary estimate, with its underlying trials
borrowed), while «associated with a lower risk of CHD» is an *observational*
hard-outcome claim carrying no magnitude and no interval. Keep them apart:
one portion of pulses moves LDL by a stated amount; what pulses do to *events*
at that intake stays unquantified -> [[Whole Grains Refined Grains and Pulses]].

**Do not stack this under the mortality numbers.** The temptation is to place
Brown's causal LDL effect beneath the observational 15-30% risk reductions and
call fibre *powerfully proven*. They are different quantities on every axis —
different **design** (controlled RCT vs prospective cohort), **exposure**
(a dosed viscous isolate vs total self-reported whole-food fibre), **endpoint**
(the LDL surrogate vs mortality/disease), **dose metric** (per gram soluble
fibre vs per 8 g total fibre), and **curve shape** (Brown's LDL response
attenuates above \~8 g/day, while the hard-outcome curves stay linear). The
honest composite is *beneficial and at least partly causal, with a modest
measured effect on the endpoints that can actually be trialled* — the mortality
magnitude stays observational -> [[Dietary Fibre and Health]].


**And the well-graded object is itself small.** LDL is a surrogate; LDL/apoB
burden is a *validated* target for ASCVD, so a real particle-number drop does
transmit to events — but the transmission is route-specific (dietary lowering is
not automatically the statin case), and a few tenths of a mmol/L from viscous
fibre is dwarfed by the saturated-fat-replacement and drug levers
-> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. Viscous fibre is a legitimate LDL adjunct, and a
small one. The remaining outcomes are held with even less numerical grip.

## The softer outcomes: direction without magnitude

Past LDL and body weight, fibre's effects are held as directions rather than
numbers. Naming them as gaps is itself the result: it tells someone which claims
the evidence cannot yet size, so they are not oversold.

### Glycaemia

Viscous fibre blunts the post-meal glucose rise — the gel-forming matrix slows
carbohydrate absorption and flattens the hepatic glucose flux, the same
mechanism that makes carbohydrate *quality* matter more than quantity
-> [[Free Sugars Intake]]. That direction is well-motivated. **The magnitude is
not held.** No clean quantified viscous-fibre -> glycaemia effect on a
patient-important endpoint sits in the corpus, and the one chronic glycaemic
marker that is held runs null: Reynolds' RCT pool gives HbA1c «SMD -0·35 (-0·73
to 0·03)» (and, in a smaller set, -0·54, -1·28 to 0·20), both intervals crossing
zero at **Low** certainty.
[@reynolds2019]
So: direction yes, a usable magnitude no — a **GAP**.


### Weight and satiety

Body weight is the anchor, and it is trial-grade. Reynolds' randomised trials
give higher fibre a mean **-0.37 kg** (95% CI -0.63 to -0.11), graded **High** —
«In randomised trials, higher intakes of dietary fibre reduced bodyweight and
lowered blood cholesterol and systolic blood pressure.»
[@reynolds2019]
This is the dosable/trialable design class, like LDL — not the observational
leg — and, like LDL, the causal number is small.

**Satiety-as-a-measured-outcome is the gap, not weight.** Increased satiety
appears in Brown's list of *proposed* mechanisms for fibre's effects, but a
mechanism is not a measured endpoint, and no held source quantifies a
fibre -> satiety effect on its own terms. State it as a **GAP**; do not read the
-0.37 kg backwards into a satiety magnitude.


### Gut transit, constipation, gastric emptying

The mechanism is clear and the evidence is absent — a rare, honest combination.
Insoluble/bulking fibre (wheat bran, cellulose) adds faecal mass and speeds
colonic transit, easing constipation; viscous fibre slows gastric emptying,
which is part of how it blunts the glucose rise above. But **no dedicated held
source** grades any of these against a patient-important outcome — the nucleus
records fibre's colonic/transit mechanism as covered by neither Reynolds nor
Brown -> [[Dietary Fibre and Health]].

These are therefore **named gaps**, stated as such and not filled by inference:

- **Constipation / transit** — bulking fibre plausibly helps; magnitude unheld.
- **Gastric emptying** — viscous fibre plausibly slows it; a mechanistic claim
  only, with no outcome number.

Do not infer a direction-plus-magnitude here from the mechanism alone; a bulking
effect that is real in principle still needs an outcome source before it can
carry a recommendation.


## Colorectal cancer: the softest arm, and a grade clash

Colorectal cancer is the softest arm of fibre's outcome menu. Observationally it looks like the others — Reynolds pools colorectal cancer incidence at RR 0.84 (0.78 to 0.89), GRADE moderate [@reynolds2019] — but two gold-tier bodies read essentially the same literature to opposite grades.

WCRF's Third Expert Report grades fibre a *probable* (strong-enough-to-recommend) protector: «Consuming dietary fibre helps protect against colorectal cancer and weight gain, overweight and obesity» [@wcrf2018ter], and sets a plant-food goal of «Consume a diet that provides at least 30 grams per day of fibre from food sources» [@wcrf2018ter] — the same \~25-30 g target reached on the mortality axis.

Veronese's bias screen reads the same colorectal signal as *weak* — class III/IV, and heavily case-control-driven. And where fibre has actually been trialled on the precancerous lesion, it did nothing: increased fibre «did not reduce the incidence or recurrence of adenomatous polyps in ∼5000 participants», a result Veronese notes is «in agreement with a Cochrane review of randomized and quasi-randomized controlled trials» [@veronese2018]. The colorectal adenoma is a surrogate for cancer, and its one RCT test came back null — precisely the [[Surrogate Outcomes]] hazard.

This is a grade-and-standpoint clash, not a data clash. Both bodies read the same observational cohort base. WCRF, a precautionary cancer-prevention body, grades it strong-enough-to-act; Veronese's strict credibility-class lens grades it weak. The wiki adopts neither reading as its verdict — it neither banks "probable" nor dismisses the association: the fibre-cancer arm is genuinely the most contested outcome on the menu, and is not upgraded to causal here ([[Dietary Fibre and Health]]). A newer, subtype-specific outcome now joins the menu.

## Inflammatory bowel disease: a new, subtype-specific signal

A 2020 gold-tier meta-analysis (Milajerdi) adds inflammatory bowel disease, and the finding is that fibre does not act on IBD as a single effect. Highest-versus-lowest fibre intake is *associated* with lower Crohn disease risk — «a significant inverse association between dietary fiber intake and risk of CD (RR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.46, 0.74; I2 = 0.0%)» — but is null for ulcerative colitis — «no significant association was found between dietary fiber intake and risk of UC (RR: 1.09; 95% CI: 0.88, 1.34; I2 = 0.0%)» [@milajerdi2020ibd]. Both pooled estimates are homogeneous (I2 = 0.0%), so the divergence is real rather than a pooling artifact, and no single pooled IBD estimate should be reported — it would mask the Crohn benefit and blur the ulcerative-colitis null.

For Crohn the association is nonlinear: «the highest risk reduction was seen for fiber intake >22 g/d», with an «additional 10 g/d of fiber intake ... associated with a 14% reduction in CD risk» [@milajerdi2020ibd]; >22 g/d is a studied-category label, not a validated knee.

These are observational, FFQ-measured associations carrying one extra hazard beyond the healthy-user and measurement-error caveats: reverse causation, since preclinical IBD can itself alter intake. So IBD sits at low confidence — a plausible additional reason to reach adequate fibre (concentrated in Crohn disease), not a new lever ([[Autoimmune Disease and Modifiable Risk]]). The proposed mechanism is the short-chain-fatty-acid route — and that SCFA route is the microbiome lever.

## The microbiome: fermentable fibre is the dietary lever

Diet is the dominant modifiable influence on the gut microbiome, and within diet the active fraction
is **fermentable fibre**: gut bacteria ferment it into short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate,
butyrate). That is also most of what «prebiotic» denotes — WGO defines a prebiotic as «A selectively
fermented ingredient that results in specific changes in the composition and/or activity of the
gastrointestinal microbiota, thus conferring benefit(s) upon host health»
[@wgo2023] — so the prebiotic
evidence largely restates the fibre evidence rather than adding a separate lever.



Two disciplines keep this honest.

First, **a composition shift is a surrogate, not an outcome** ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). Almost all
microbiome research reports *which* bacteria and *how diverse*, not a patient-important endpoint;
Valdes states the limit plainly — «The strongest level of evidence is obtained from interventional
clinical studies — in particular, randomised controlled trials.»
[@valdes2018] So «it altered the
microbiome» earns belief only when followed through to an outcome.

Second, **the fibre-to-diversity link is not a simple gradient.** Valdes is two-sided on it: «Many
short term feeding trials with purified dietary fibres or even whole plant based diets either have no
effect on microbiota diversity or reduce it, but can still have clinical benefits, potentially through
metabolites such as small chain fatty acids.»
[@valdes2018] So the clinical benefit runs
through the fermentation products, not through a tidy «more fibre -> more diversity» rule — do not
assert that gradient.

The head-to-head sharpens this. In the Wastyk 2021 RCT, over a 10-week intervention the high-fibre
arm's alpha diversity «did not change cohort-wide over the course of the intervention» while the
high-fermented-foods arm «exhibited an overall increase in alpha diversity over the course of the
intervention»
[@wastyk2021]. On the diversity marker
over an actionable timescale, fermented foods were the more reliable lever — so fermentable fibre is
the **dominant** dietary microbiome lever, not the sole one. This refines rather than overturns the
fibre story: it is a distinction on horizon (a long-term association versus a 10-week trial), and
diversity is itself a surrogate ([[Gut Microbiome and Health]]).



The microbiome case also carries a recurring confusion about form.

## Isolate versus whole food: better evidence is not a better food

A striking inversion sits at the centre of the fibre evidence: the isolated component is
**better evidenced** than the food carrying it. SACN grades fibre isolates and gum supplements at its
top strength grade, `Effect · Adequate`, while whole-grain benefit is cohort-only and mostly limited:

> «• Effect • Adequate evidence • The direction of the effect demonstrates higher consumption of fibre
> isolates and gum supplements is beneficial to health • The effect is biologically relevant, but
> demonstrated at intakes achieved through supplementation»
> [@sacn2015]

Read the last clause, because it is the resolution: the effect is «demonstrated at intakes achieved
through supplementation». The grade gap is a fact about **design**, not superiority. An isolate can be
dosed at a chosen amount and blinded against a low-fibre placebo; a bowl of beans cannot. So the
better grade tracks the better *trialable form* — the [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]] point in
miniature — and does not license «take the supplement instead».



Brown is the worked case. Its meta-analysis of controlled trials pins soluble/viscous fibre to an LDL
reduction of **-0.057 mmol/L per gram**
[@brown1999] — trial-grade
precisely because a single fibre can be dosed and controlled. The whole-food fibre that tracks lower
mortality in cohorts ([[Dietary Fibre and Health]]) cannot be blinded, so it stays observational: same
design asymmetry, same trap. And even the well-graded object is small — Brown's *small contribution to
dietary therapy* verdict, quoted in the LDL section above, applies here too. So
«isolate is better-evidenced» is a statement about study design carrying a modest effect, not evidence
that a psyllium sachet beats food for outcomes.

The same component-not-category logic runs one level out to whole grains. Reynolds grades fibre above
whole grains and attributes the gap to fibre content — the whole-grain grade «could reflect the high
fibre content of whole grains»
[@reynolds2019] — so whole grains
probably act largely *through* their fibre, and the target to steer by is fibre content, not the label
«whole grain». This stays low-confidence and cohort-based; the whole-grain verdict itself lives on
[[Whole Grains Refined Grains and Pulses]].

The question also asks what fibre costs.

## The downsides: real, minor, dose- and preparation-managed

The costs of eating more fibre are genuine but small, and nearly all of them
shift with *how* fibre is prepared and how fast intake rises, not with
*whether* to eat it. The message is to change the how, not the whether.



- **Gas and bloating** are the flip-side of the fermentation that produces the
  microbiome benefit: fermentable fibre feeds gut bacteria, which release gas.
  The effect is pace- and dose-dependent, so ramping intake gradually (and
  raising fluid alongside) lets the microbiota adapt and blunts the symptom.
- **Obstruction** is a rare risk, and only with a large bulking-fibre load taken
  against too little fluid; adequate fluid removes it. It is a preparation
  failure, not a property of fibre.

The compound-level worries belong to the *antinutrient* literature, where
ordinary preparation defuses most of them -> [[Antinutrients in Plant Foods]].

- **Phytate** binds zinc, iron and calcium and lowers their bioavailability in
  isolation, but soaking, germinating, leavening/fermenting and cooking all
  reduce it, so in a varied diet the concern is academic. It bites only in a
  named route-(c) stratum: monotonous high-phytate diets with already-marginal
  mineral status. `[@petroski2020]`
- **Raw high-lectin legumes** are the one place preparation is mandatory, not
  optional — an acute food-poisoning risk, not a chronic-diet claim:

> «traditional processes such as soaking, sprouting, fermenting, boiling, and
> autoclaving are all methods that can significantly reduce lectin content. In
> the case of particularly high-lectin legumes, such as soybeans and kidney
> beans, boiling or autoclaving is required to eliminate lectins, as reduced
> cooking temperatures do not significantly affect lectin content.»
> `[@petroski2020]`

The generalisation worth keeping is that an isolated compound in an animal is
not a cooked food in a person:

> «Harmful (and even beneficial) effects of an individual, isolated compound or
> phytochemical are often quite different than when the same compound is within
> the complex food matrix.»
> `[@petroski2020]`

So the downsides are minor, largely preparation- and pace-managed, and the
real strata that still need care (low iron stores, stone-formers, marginal
iodine) are route-(c) contraindications, not a reason for a general population
to eat less fibre -> [[Antinutrients in Plant Foods]].

So how much, and what does the 30 g figure actually mean?

## How much, and the target confusion

Four guidance bodies name a fibre target, and they are not four estimates of
one quantity. Cite [[Whole Grains Refined Grains and Pulses]] for the assembled
table.

| Body | Fibre target | Construct | Analytic method named? |
|---|---|---|---|
| SACN | 30 g/day | point | yes — AOAC |
| WHO | >=25 g/day | floor | not held |
| NNR | 3 g/MJ (primary); 25 g/d women, 35 g/d men | energy-scaled + sex-split | no |
| ESC | 30-45 g/day | range | no |

`[@sacn2015]` · `[@esc2021]` · `[@nnr2023]`

A point, a floor, an energy-scaled value and a range are different constructs
answering different questions, and **only SACN names its analytic method**
(AOAC 985.29 / 991.43). Since AOAC and the narrower NSP definition count
different fractions, the raw numbers are not rankable against one another.
ESC's upper bound of 45 g is the highest figure in the corpus and carries no
stated warrant, so it is a held position, not evidence that more is better.

### 30 g is the edge of the data, not a biological knee

Read the 30 g as the edge of the evidence, not a threshold the curve turns at.
SACN's reference value sits where the confidence intervals widen and the cohort
data thin out, above which the dose-response is simply unobserved — the same
pattern the wiki flags whenever a guidance number is mistaken for a curve
feature -> [[The Underivable Optimum]]. And below that edge, Reynolds' outcome
curves are approximately linear with no plateau located within the studied
range -> [[Dietary Fibre and Health]], so no knee has been demonstrated at or
under 30 g. The operative default is therefore *every reduction of the shortfall
pays, and the burden falls on whoever asserts a knee to locate it*; more than
30 g is fine and probably slightly better, bounded above only by tolerance, not
by an evidenced optimum.

### The shortfall makes *get to 30 g* a real behaviour change

The target is not where people already sit:

> «Setting the dietary reference value for AOAC fibre at 30g/day for adults
> means that current mean intakes would be 10-11g below the dietary reference
> value for men and 13g below for women.»
> `[@sacn2015]`

That is a roughly 40% population shortfall, so *get to 30 g* is a substantial
change in what people eat, not a tweak — and, under diminishing returns,
adherence is part of the effect: a sustained partial move beats an abandoned
maximal one. Do **not** read NNR's 90 g/day whole-grain figure (a dry food
mass) against the 30 g AOAC fibre target; they are different quantities, the
same unit trap the corpus records for NSP-vs-AOAC
-> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]].

### What measurement error does to the numbers

[inferred from @sacn2015]

Two biases pull the observational fibre gradients in opposite directions.
Self-reported intake carries error large enough to attenuate a real
dose-response *toward* the null — non-differential error biases an association
toward no-effect in the simple case — so on the measurement axis the
hard-outcome effects are, if anything, **under-stated**
-> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]. But the healthy-user confound
runs the other way: high-fibre eaters differ systematically (more active,
less smoking, lower adiposity), which inflates the same associations. The two
do not cancel to a known residual, so the net position on hard outcomes stays
`confidence: medium` — neither dismissed as confounded nor upgraded to causal.

Set against mainstream guidance, this is agreement with sharper calibration.

## Where this sits against guidance

The wiki agrees with the guidance set on the two things that matter: roughly
25-30 g/day from food, and a real (if modest) benefit. SACN, WHO, NNR, ESC and
the cancer body WCRF converge there, and the wiki does not diverge from the
direction of travel — eat more fibre from whole grains, legumes and fruit/veg.



What it adds is calibration on three points the round numbers blur:

- The large hi-vs-lo reductions in mortality, heart disease and diabetes are
  **observational**, upgraded above bare correlation by a monotone dose-response
  plus a concordant trial arm, but not RCT-proven -> [[Dietary Fibre and Health]].
- The **causal**, trial-grade effects are small and type-specific: viscous
  fibre lowers LDL by a few tenths of a mmol/L, and higher fibre trims body
  weight modestly. The magnitude does not stack onto the observational one.
- 30 g is the **data edge**, not a threshold to hit exactly
  -> [[The Underivable Optimum]].

This is agreement with better warrant, not contrarianism: no guidance body is
strawmanned, and the disagreements are about *certainty and magnitude*, not
about whether fibre helps.

## Gaps (stated plainly)



Several outcomes are named gaps, and are stated as gaps rather than filled with
an inferred direction or magnitude:

- **Glycaemia magnitude.** The direction is held — viscous fibre blunts the
  post-meal glucose rise by slowing absorption -> [[Free Sugars Intake]] — but
  no clean, quantified fibre->glycaemia effect is held.
- **GI transit and constipation.** Mechanistically clear (bulking fibre speeds
  transit), but no dedicated source is held for a magnitude.
- **Gastric emptying.** Mechanistic only (viscous fibre slows it); no outcome
  evidence held.
- **Satiety as such.** Body weight is held as a small, trial-grade effect (the
  anchor), but satiety as a *directly measured* outcome is thinly quantified —
  the weight number is not a satiety measurement.
- **A whole-food fibre RCT on hard outcomes.** Structurally out of reach: you
  cannot blind a food, and the blindable form (an isolate) is a *different
  exposure* than the fibre-bearing food -> [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]].
  So the causal question on whole-food fibre and mortality stays observational
  by construction, not by neglect.
- **Type and source breakdowns.** Soluble-vs-insoluble and legume-vs-cereal
  outcome comparisons are too sparse to separate at the outcome level, so the
  page steers by fibre *type-to-mechanism* rather than by a graded per-source
  contrast.

## Mandatory caveats

- **This appraises; it does not prescribe.** It reports what fibre does to each outcome, for
  whom, how much and how certainly -- not a per-person gram target, and not a
  supplement-versus-food ruling. Selecting, dosing and screening for a condition (IBS,
  diverticular disease, a stricture, drug interactions) are prescriber acts needing this
  person's history.
- **Applied per person.** Baseline intake, gut conditions, medication and tolerance vary; the
  population-level reading here is the starting point for a recommendation, not the
  recommendation itself.
- **The loop is open.** These are appraisals of coherence and source-fidelity, not outcomes
  measured against a realized result -- nothing here has been graded against what actually
  happened to anyone.
