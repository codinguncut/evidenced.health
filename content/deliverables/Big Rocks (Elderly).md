> **Who this guide is for.** This guide is for an obese adult around 70 who still lives independently:
> often with high blood pressure, prediabetes or diabetes, fatty liver, lower bone density and some loss
> of muscle, and often on several medicines. It is not written for frail people or care-home residents;
> several of its numbers do not transfer to them, and the guide says where. It appraises lifestyle levers
> and the standard drugs as comparators. The prescriber, not this guide, chooses, doses, stops or
> combines medicines.

## Rank by what this age loses: function, falls, fractures and independence

- At 70 three outcomes matter most: **staying independent, avoiding a hip fracture and keeping
  function** [[Rating Outcome Importance]].
- Balance-challenging exercise is the best-tested lever: **about a quarter fewer falls (rate ratio
  0.77, 0.71-0.83), high certainty** [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].
- Raised blood pressure is still worth lowering: in trials, lowering it cut major cardiovascular
  events up to age 84 and slightly reduced dementia [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].
- Strength and power training comes next: gains in function and lean mass, not fewer falls
  [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].
- For bone, exercise programmes cut major fractures; routine vitamin D or calcium is not a fracture
  lever for someone at home who is not deficient [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]].
- With prediabetes, a lifestyle programme cut diabetes, at least as well at 60 and over
  [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]; at high cardiovascular risk, a Mediterranean diet
  cut cardiovascular events in one repaired trial [[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]].
- Weight loss cut mortality in mostly middle-aged trials. No trial in this guide's evidence tested it on
  falls or fractures at this age, so at 70 it is **worth doing only if muscle is defended**
  [[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]].
- A salt substitute cut stroke and death in rural China; elsewhere only its blood-pressure fall has been
  shown to transfer [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

Lower down sit levers resting on weaker study designs, the drug choices, and levers that turn harmful
at this age or add little:

- The protein floor rests on consensus and muscle markers [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]]; the steps
  plateau rests on cohorts [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].
- Without vascular disease, the evidence for starting a statin weakens after 75
  [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]].
- **Aspirin started for prevention turns from help to harm more clearly than any other lever**: modelled net
  life-years turn negative for starting at 70-79 [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]].
  Two narrower cautions rest on advice rather than shown harm: limit protein in advanced kidney
  disease, and avoid brisk walking at high fall risk.
- Supplements add little [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]], and multidomain
  memory programmes did not show a reduction in dementia (wide interval)
  [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]]. These mark the ceiling, and reaching it
  is itself a result.

**A fall matters less than what it leads to.** On the Canadian Task Force review's scale from 0 (full
health) to 1 (death), a fall in the past year cost about 0.09 (-0.04 to 0.22, low certainty). **A hip
fracture cost 0.53 in the first three months (moderate certainty) and still 0.16 at twelve months (high
certainty).** Admission to long-term care scored a median of 1, equivalent to death, from one
time-trade-off study, low certainty
[@pillay2024falls].
**This guide therefore ranks fracture and lost independence above the fall itself.** Almost no trial
measures how long function holds before it drops [[Rating Outcome Importance]].

**Length of life still counts, but fewer years remain for a benefit to build up.** In the European
Society of Cardiology's lifetime model, 1 mmol/L lower LDL cholesterol gives a non-smoking man aged
45-49 roughly 0.6 to 1.6 extra years free of cardiovascular disease, and someone aged 90 or over about
0.0 to 0.3 years. Both are modelled figures, not trial results
[@esc2021] ->
[[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

**This guide's advice stops where frailty begins.** In Vermeiren's pool of community-dwelling adults
aged 65 and over, **frail people died sooner than robust ones (RR 2.01, 1.82-2.22)**, and frailty also
predicted institutionalisation and fractures
[@vermeiren2016frailty]. These
cohorts show prognosis, not cause. Most of the trials behind the estimates below did not test frail
people or care-home residents; later sections point back to this limit [[Frailty]].

**This guide grades each lever on its own outcome and does not add them into one score.** The reader
chooses how much staying independent should count against living longer
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

## Train balance hard, about three hours a week, and keep it up

**Do high-challenge balance training inside about 3 hours a week of prescribed exercise, and keep it
up.** In a Cochrane review of 108 community trials (mean age 76), **exercise cut the rate of falls by
about a quarter, with high certainty: rate ratio 0.77 (0.71-0.83)**. At the review's illustrative
control rate, that is 195 (144-246) fewer falls per 1000 people per year
[@sherrington2019]. Trial control groups fell more
often than older people in general, and the relative effect did not differ by age or baseline fall risk
[@sherrington2019]. So **a lower-risk
70-year-old should expect fewer falls averted, but not none**.

**Balance is the active ingredient**: balance and functional exercise reduces falls, high
certainty. **Resistance training on its own has not been shown to reduce falls** (1.14, 0.67-1.97, very
low certainty: insufficient evidence, not a null)
[@sherrington2019]. *High-challenge* means moving the
body's centre of mass, narrowing the base of support and keeping hand support to a minimum: reaching,
shifting weight from leg to leg, stepping up
[@sherrington2017falls].

**The dose is a labelled default, not a tested dose, for community-dwelling people only.** In a
meta-regression of 61 community trials, 3 or more hours a week of prescribed exercise in total gave a
ratio of rate ratios of 0.77 (0.65-0.91), and benefits are lost when people stop
[@sherrington2017falls]. The
meta-regression compares trials, so it may be confounded, and it goes no higher than 3 hours.
High-challenge balance under 3 hours still cut falls
[@sherrington2017falls],
so 3 hours is a level to build toward, not a line below which effort is wasted.

**Harms were mostly minor but poorly watched.** Only one trial monitored adverse events closely in both
arms; the two serious events, a pelvic stress fracture and a hernia operation, came from a
single trial [@sherrington2019].

**A walking programme alone, or eye screening for everyone, is not falls prevention.** Walking programmes have
insufficient evidence of benefit (1.14, 0.66-1.97, very low certainty, the point estimate on the harm
side) [@sherrington2019]. The 2017 authors advise
that walking not be anyone's only fall-prevention exercise and that **high-risk older people not be
told to walk briskly**
[@sherrington2017falls].
**Universal vision screening and treatment, offered to everyone rather than to people with poor sight,
increased both fallers and fractures**, moderate certainty
[@pillay2024falls].

**Outside the community, trials have not shown the effect.** In care homes (0.90, 0.72-1.12), after
hospital discharge and after stroke, exercise alone
[@sherrington2017falls] has
insufficient evidence: not shown to work, not shown not to work, and not shown safe ->
[[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]], [[Frailty]].

## Lift for strength and power, and eat at least the protein floor that feeds it

**Do resistance training (RT) twice a week, start at low volume, progress, and add power work.** In
Radaelli's 151 trials of supervised RT in untrained adults aged 60 and over, most volume bands beat no
exercise on most outcomes. The lowest band, 5 to under 18 lower-limb sets a week, may improve the timed
up-and-go test: standardised mean difference (SMD) -1.20 (-1.57 to -0.82), low certainty. **Except for
strength, higher volume added no benefit on the outcomes examined**. Fast walking speed was the one
function test where it did better
[@radaelli2024rtvol].

**Labelled default, not a tested dose:** two sessions a week, two sets of each of three lower-limb
exercises, under 20 minutes, at a typical trial load of about 80% of the one-repetition maximum,
progressing from there. The authors translated it from the low-band median. For sarcopenia or frailty
the most effective dose «remains to be determined»
[@radaelli2024rtvol] ->
[[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]], [[Frailty]].

**Power training, done with fast movements, improves function tests more than conventional strength
training: SMD 0.43 (0.23 to 0.62), high certainty**, across 15 trials with a mean age over 65. That is
a small-to-moderate gain over a comparator that itself works. **None of these trials counted falls**, so
power training is not a proven falls lever [@elhadouchi2022]
 -> [[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]]. With sarcopenic obesity, RT
alone (9 trials, mean age 68) improved body composition, SMD 0.35 (0.12 to 0.57), moderate certainty.
But **lower body fat carried the gain; muscle mass did not change significantly**
[@poloferrero2025] ->
[[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]].

### Eat at least 1.2 g protein per kg body weight a day while training

PROT-AGE, a consensus endorsed by four geriatric societies, recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 g of protein
per kg body weight a day for healthy older adults and 1.2 g for those exercising, which includes anyone
doing RT. The consensus raises the floor to 1.2-1.5 g/kg with illness, says severe illness or marked
malnutrition may need up to 2.0 g/kg, and sets the per-meal threshold at 25-30 g
[@bauer2013].

**These floors rest on nitrogen balance, lean mass and short-term muscle protein synthesis; no trial
shows that reaching them changes fractures, disability or survival.** Which kilogram to count for an
obese person is unresolved [inferred from @bauer2013]. **PROT-AGE states no
upper limit for older adults with healthy kidneys.** For a light person the per-meal threshold sets the
total: at 50 kg, three meals of 25-30 g come to 75-90 g a day, 1.5-1.8 g/kg. No trial in this guide's
evidence tests that intake -> [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

**With severe kidney disease the floor turns into a cap.** With eGFR below 30 and not on dialysis,
PROT-AGE limits protein to 0.8 g per kg of ideal body weight a day. With eGFR 30-60 it calls more than
0.8 g/kg safe if GFR is monitored twice a year
[@bauer2013]. With normal kidneys, trials
averaging 1.81 g/kg (ages 23 to 72) found no difference in GFR change, very low certainty: no harm
detected on a surrogate, not proof of safety
[@devries2018]. **Above about 2 g/kg
after 65 the evidence is insufficient** -> [[Protein Intake and Kidney Function]].

## Get out of the least-active band; the steps plateau arrives earlier at this age

**If below it, walk toward about 6000-8000 steps a day.** In a harmonised meta-analysis of 15 cohorts,
mortality fell with daily steps and then levelled off (second quarter against the lowest, HR 0.60,
0.51-0.71; quarter medians 3553 to 10,901 steps a day). **Adults aged 60 and over reached the
plateau at about 6000-8000 steps a day**, against about 8000-10,000 under 60
[@paluch2022]. The plateau is read off a spline with no
confidence interval, so it is a region, not a cutpoint. The cohorts compare people with each other
rather than measuring the effect of adding steps, and **the gain lies in leaving the bottom of the
distribution** -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]. Steps do not substitute for falls
exercise. Fitness is a predictor that training moves, not a proven lever on death ->
[[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]].

**Start gradually; with diabetes, get cleared first.** Exercise-related cardiac risk is very low but
rises with age, and the AHA advises inactive starters to begin slowly, usually with walking
[@franklin2020acute]. Under
the ACSM consensus, an inactive person without cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, diabetes or
symptoms can start light-to-moderate exercise directly; **a person with one of those gets clearance
first** [@riebe2015screen]. Diabetes is common
in this group -> [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]].

**A painful knee is a reason to move, at lower impact.** In osteoarthritis trials whose participants
averaged 60 or over, exercise still eased pain (SMD 0.44, 0.34-0.55), less than in younger
trials: **smaller, but not reversed**. Obesity showed no sign of blunting the effect, though a
study-level subgroup cannot rule a difference out
[@goh2019]. For an obese beginner, a lower-impact
mode such as stationary cycling is the labelled default ->
[[Exercise Therapy for Osteoarthritis Pain and Function]], [[Stationary Cycling for Knee Osteoarthritis]].

## Lose weight only while defending muscle

**If losing weight on purpose, pair it with resistance training and the protein floor.** At this age
the trials in this guide's evidence tested weight loss on function only in knee osteoarthritis and
none tested falls or fractures. That is a reason to pair the levers, not evidence that weight loss
harms a 70-year-old.

**Excess weight still predicts death at 70, less steeply.** Among never-smokers without chronic
disease, each 5 kg/m2 above a BMI of 25 went with a death rate **about a fifth higher at 70-89 (HR 1.21,
1.17-1.25)**, against HR 1.52 at 35-49, an observational association
[@globalbmi2016] -> [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]]. In
54 RCTs in obese adults, **weight-reducing diets cut all-cause mortality (RR 0.82, 0.71-0.95), six fewer
deaths per 1000 (2 to 10) over a median two years**. Cardiovascular events did not fall significantly
(RR 0.93, 0.83-1.04), and only 14 of the 54 trials had a mean or median age of 60 or more
[@ma2017] ->
[[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].

**The one trial at this age on function also shows the muscle cost.** In IDEA (454 overweight or obese
adults aged 55 and over, mean 66, with knee osteoarthritis, 18 months), **diet plus exercise gave the
best function**, WOMAC function 4.29 points better than exercise alone (2.07-6.50). Diet alone did not
differ significantly from exercise alone. **The diet arms lost 4.2-4.7 kg (8-9%) of whole-body lean
mass.** The larger loss came in the diet-plus-exercise arm, whose programme included strength training.
Lean mass as a share of body weight still rose, because fat fell further
[@messier2013idea] ->
[[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]].

**What defends the muscle.** **Dieters who added RT kept 0.82 kg more lean body mass (0.36-1.27)** in
six RCTs of older adults (mean age over 57)
[@sardeli2018] ->
[[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]]. **Dieters who ate at least 1.0 g protein per kg a
day kept 0.83 kg more lean mass (0.47-1.19)**, in the trials that classified protein by g/kg among 20
RCTs of adults aged 50 and over
[@kim2016] ->
[[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]]. Both measure lean mass, a surrogate; **no trial in this
guide's evidence shows that the extra kilogram keeps function**.

**Pace is untested at this age; amount and rate interact in predicting regain.** In Turicchi's
between-study meta-regression (median age 44.8), **the larger the loss, the more the pace
predicted regain**, though that interaction fell to a trend after BMI adjustment. The authors say
someone attempting a substantial loss may wish to consider a more conservative method
[@turicchi2019regain]. That suggestion comes from
between-study data, not a trial of pace.

In one randomised trial (BMI 30-45, no diabetes), a 12-week very-low-calorie diet got more starters to a
12.5% loss than a 36-week diet (78% vs 51%). The arms also differed in diet form and in dietitian visits
(6 vs 18), so the trial compared two programmes, not pace alone. Among people who reached the target and
finished, **both arms had regained most of the weight by week 144**. One fast-diet participant developed
acute cholecystitis, rated probably related. The authors say people losing weight rapidly need properly
formulated supplements [@purcell2014rate]. **The regain comparison
covers only people who reached the target**: in them it rules out a large pace effect, not a small one, and
one case cannot size the gallstone risk. The trial took ages 18-70 and reports no count over 65
[@purcell2014rateappx], so **at around 70 the result
holds only by assumption** -> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]].

### The obesity drugs take off more weight; lean loss and regain come with them

**If a drug is considered, expect more weight lost, with lean loss and regain.** Against lifestyle
modification alone at one year, **tirzepatide took off 14.9% of body weight (-16.0 to -13.9) and
subcutaneous semaglutide 9.8% (-10.6 to -9.1)**, moderate to high certainty. **Lean mass fell 8.3%
(-12.9 to -3.7) and 5.8% (-8.7 to -2.9)** respectively, moderate certainty. In a review Nong cites,
weight returned at about 0.4 kg a month after stopping treatment. Semaglutide's mortality RR of 0.81
(0.72-0.93) comes from trials largely in people at high cardiovascular risk. No drug could be
tested by age [@nong2026obesity].

**On diet and on drugs alike, people lose lean mass and regain weight.** IDEA's 8-9% is a change within
each arm; the drug figures are differences over lifestyle. So **the evidence in this guide shows
neither that the drugs cost more muscle than diet nor that they cost less**. Neither mortality
figure transfers unchanged to a lower-risk 70-year-old -> [[Comparing Obesity Drugs]],
[[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]].

S-LiTE, in adults aged 18-65 keeping weight off after a diet, added mostly aerobic exercise to
liraglutide and cut body fat by a further 1.9 percentage points (-3.3 to -0.5), a secondary result on a
surrogate [@lundgren2021healthy].
Exercise-related injuries affected 5 of 49 on the pairing against none of 49 on the drug alone
[@lundgren2021healthyappx].
The authors say their results may not generalise to people over 65
[@lundgren2021healthy]. **At 70 the resistance
training and protein floor above stay the pairing to use**.

**Fatty liver and unintended loss.** EASL ties **7-10% sustained loss to less liver inflammation and 10%
or more to less fibrosis**, largely on one histology trial with no age-specific data
[@easl2024] ->
[[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]. **Unintentional weight loss is a warning sign, not a lever.**
For intentional loss without RT or extra protein the evidence on function at 70 is insufficient
 -> [[Frailty]].

## Treat blood pressure, glucose and lipids by absolute risk, not by age

**The benefit from each risk-factor lever depends on absolute risk, not on age alone.** Risk climbs
with age, so where the relative effect holds, the same treatment prevents more events at 70 than at 50
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. The prescriber decides drug choice, targets, doses
and stopping.

### Blood pressure: lower it if raised; the effect holds up to 84

**If blood pressure is raised, lower it.** Rahimi split 358,707 people from 51 trials by age: **per 5
mmHg lower systolic pressure, major cardiovascular events fell 0.91 (0.88-0.95) at 65-74 and 0.91
(0.87-0.96) at 75-84** [@rahimi2021bpage]. In the authors' reading the
absolute reduction appeared largest in the oldest groups; at 85 and over, 0.99 (0.87-1.12) from about 1000 events
[@rahimi2021bpage] is on its own **insufficient evidence,
not evidence of no benefit**.

This guide carries 0.91 forward to 85+ as a working default held with wider uncertainty, since the
effect is 0.91 in every band from 55 to 84. The placebo-controlled 85+ estimate, 1.07
(0.89-1.29), not scaled to a 5 mmHg fall, stays an unresolved counter-signal
[@rahimi2021bpageappx].

**Harms and limits.** The pooled analysis does not report falls or fainting by age
[searched: falls / syncope across the paper's chunks 01-02 and the appendix; syncope appears only in
exclusion criteria]. The authors know of no randomised comparison suggesting that a fixed
blood-pressure reduction does older people more harm than good. **For someone already on a
well-tolerated drug, they advise continuing while preventing cardiovascular events still matters.**
People who are frail, multimorbid, on many medicines or living in institutions were under-represented,
and the authors call it uncertain whether the findings reach them
[@rahimi2021bpage]. **A frail person and a clinician weigh
starting treatment as an individual trade-off** ->
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], [[Frailty]],
[[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].

### Salt substitute: hard outcomes in rural China, after a kidney and potassium check

**If the diet is Chinese-style, with most salt added at home, consider a salt substitute after a
kidney and potassium check.** SSaSS randomised 600 villages in rural China (20,995 people with prior
stroke, or aged 60 or more with high blood pressure; 72.6% after a stroke) to a salt substitute (\~25%
potassium chloride) or regular salt for 4.74 years: **stroke 0.86 (0.77-0.96), death 0.88 (0.82-0.95),
about 5.3 fewer deaths per 1000 person-years**
[@neal2021]. Without SSaSS, all-cause
mortality lost statistical significance and no non-Chinese trial contributed mortality data
[@lai2026salt]. So **the hard-outcome figures
belong to that Chinese pattern; the blood-pressure fall transfers more readily** ->
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**Check first.** Lai advises ruling out kidney problems and hyperkalaemia risk before recommending a
substitute [@lai2026salt]. In practice, use no
substitute with kidney disease, or where anyone eating the food takes a potassium-sparing drug or potassium
supplements; check first with diabetes or an ACE inhibitor or ARB
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]. Brand's Cochrane review adds low blood sodium as a concern
rather than an observed harm, particularly in older people and thiazide users
[@brand2022lsss]. Serious hyperkalaemia in
SSaSS was 1.04 (0.80-1.37), but the trial did not test potassium routinely, so **that result is
insufficient evidence, not shown safe**.

### Prediabetes: the lifestyle programme worked at least as well in the oldest group

**If prediabetic, join a DPP-style lifestyle programme:** at least 7% weight loss and 150 minutes of
activity a week. In 648 participants aged 60 or more the programme **cut diabetes incidence by 71% (51 to 83),
from 10.8 to 3.1 cases per 100 person-years**; metformin cut incidence
by 11% (-33 to 41) at 60 and over
[@knowler2002]. **At this age the drug
is no substitute for the lifestyle lever.** Over 21 years the middle-aged cohort showed no fall in
cardiovascular events in either arm, with the effects possibly diluted by advice to everyone and by
drugs taken outside the study [@goldberg2022] ->
[[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].

### Mediterranean diet: one trial cut cardiovascular events at 55-80

**If at high cardiovascular risk, consider a Mediterranean diet** with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts.
PREDIMED, in Spain, assigned 7447 men aged 55 to 80 and women aged 60 to 80 at high cardiovascular risk
to this diet or to advice to cut fat, with no calorie restriction. **Heart attack, stroke and
cardiovascular death: HR 0.70 (0.55-0.89), an absolute 1.7 to 2.1 percentage points over about 5
years**; death from any cause did not fall (0.98, 0.77-1.24). The authors withdrew the first report over
non-random assignment and re-analysed with propensity scores [@estruch2018]: **a randomised
trial with a statistical repair, not a clean randomised contrast** ->
[[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]].

### LDL: a statin's case weakens after 75 without known vascular disease

**Weigh a statin on absolute risk.** In CTT's age subgroups (99% intervals), with vascular disease the
benefit held past 75: 0.85 (99% CI 0.73-0.98) per 1.0 mmol/L lower LDL. **Without it, the estimates
were 0.84 (99% CI 0.70-1.01) at 71-75 and 0.92 (99% CI 0.73-1.16) over 75**
[@ctt2019]. **For a statin started after 75 without
vascular disease the evidence is insufficient, not a shown absence of benefit**, and at 71-75 the
decision rests on absolute risk
[[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]. Muscle aches are on
[[Statin Muscle Symptoms and the Nocebo Effect]].

### Aspirin: do not start it for prevention

**USPSTF recommends against starting low-dose aspirin for primary prevention at 60 or older (Grade D),
with moderate certainty of no net benefit**; bleeding risk rises with age
[@uspstf2022aspirin]. Across 13 trials aspirin cut
cardiovascular events (HR 0.89, 0.84-0.94), raised major bleeding (HR 1.43, 1.30-1.56), and was not
associated with fewer deaths (HR 0.94, 0.88-1.01; 95% credible intervals)
[@zheng2019aspirin]
[[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]. The prescriber decides whether to stop
aspirin already taken.

## Protect bone by loading it and preventing the fall, not with routine pills

**Use exercise for bone.** **Exercise programmes cut major osteoporotic fractures (hip, spine, wrist,
upper arm) by about a quarter: rate ratio 0.75 (95% CI 0.59-0.94)**, across 11 controlled trials in
adults over 50 on average, most of them women-only
[@hoffmann2022]. The trials mixed
bone-loading and falls-prevention programmes, so they cannot split the effect between stronger bone and
fewer falls, and one research group wrote the review -> [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]].

**With low bone density, swap repetitive loaded spinal flexion for extension.** A safety review advises
people with osteoporosis to avoid specific, repetitive forward-flexion exercises and focus on spinal
extension, while stressing that evidence of flexion causing vertebral fractures is limited
[@kunutsor2018adverse].
The rule covers programmed exercise such as sit-ups, not everyday bending ->
[[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]].

**For a person living at home who is not deficient, routine vitamin D or calcium is not a fracture
lever.** Vitamin D alone did not lower hip fracture (RR 1.14, 0.98-1.32); vitamin D plus calcium did (RR
0.84, 0.72-0.97), with reductions somewhat greater in older people in institutions
[@yao2019]. In the one large community trial (WHI,
postmenopausal women) the combination gave hip fracture HR 0.88 (0.72-1.08)
[@kahwati2018]. The combination raised kidney stones,
RR 1.18 (1.04-1.35), moderate certainty
[@kahwati2018]; calcium pills carry
a contested signal of more heart attacks, RR 1.24 (1.07-1.45)
[@bolland2011]. A measured deficiency is
different: test, then replete ->
[[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]], [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]].

**Hormone therapy protects bone, but started at about 70 it adds harm.** In WHI (postmenopausal women
aged 50-79), oestrogen plus progestin cut hip fractures, HR 0.66 (nominal 95% CI 0.45-0.98; 0.33-1.33
after adjustment for multiple testing)
[@rossouw2002]. Started after 65, it
added 23 dementia cases per 10,000 person-years, and NAMS calls use in older women not appropriate to
reduce heart disease or dementia
[@nams2022] ->
[[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]].

</div>

## Lower blood pressure, check hearing; expect little from multidomain programmes for memory

**Blood-pressure treatment slightly reduced dementia.** In five placebo-controlled trials (28,008
people, mean age 69.1), blood pressure fell by about 10/4 mmHg and dementia OR was 0.87 (0.75-0.99),
2.9% against 3.3% over a median 4.3 years, with no modification by age
[@peters2022bp]: about 4 fewer cases per 1000
treated -> [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]].

**Multidomain programmes at this age did not show a reduction in dementia, and the interval is wide.**
MAPT and preDIVA pooled 5,205 people over 70: HR 0.98 (0.80-1.21), with no responder subgroup found
[@coley2025] ->
[[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]].

**Get hearing checked; hearing aids help hearing, but the one trial was null on cognition overall.**
ACHIEVE randomised 977 people aged 70-84 with untreated hearing loss; 3-year global cognition differed
by 0.002 SD (-0.077 to 0.081) [@lin2023achieve].
Hearing aids stay an unconfirmed bet for dementia protection -> [[Hearing Loss and Dementia]].

**Long sleep looks like a marker of ill-health; short sleep may be a cause.** Across all ages, short
sleep went with slightly higher mortality (RR 1.12, 1.06-1.18); long sleep went with RR 1.46 (1.30-1.64)
in cohorts aged 60 or over. The authors leave cause open, reading indirect evidence as suggesting that
sustained sleep deprivation may harm health while long sleep may be a powerful additional marker of
ill-health [@cappuccio2010] ->
[[Sleep Duration and Mortality]].

## Stop optimising once these levers are in place: the ceiling and the open gaps

**Vitamin D and fish oil are not a lever for a person who is not deficient.** VITAL gave vitamin D3 2000
IU a day and marine omega-3 to 25,871 US adults chosen by age; both missed their primary cancer and
cardiovascular endpoints [@manson2019vitd]. USPSTF grades
multivitamins insufficient evidence (grade I) and beta carotene a harm (grade D)
[@uspstf2022supp]. **From 75, the Endocrine
Society conditionally suggests low-dose vitamin D** for a possible mortality benefit, RR 0.96
(0.93-1.00), about 6 fewer deaths per 1000 (from 11 fewer to 0 more)
[@demay2024vitamind] ->
[[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

Single foods and diet patterns beyond PREDIMED refine the levers above rather than add a new one
 -> [[Food Groups and Health Outcomes - A Dose-Response Matrix]]. **The remaining levers are
small, and that is itself the result:** it tells the person they can stop optimising ->
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

### Smoking goes to the top if present; heavy drinking likely does too

**Smoking, where present, outranks every lever in this guide.** Current smokers die at about three
times the rate of never-smokers (HR 3.0, 99% CI 2.7-3.3, in women; 2.8, 2.4-3.1, in men), and quitting
at 55-64 still gained about 4 years [@jha2013].
The data stop at a quit age of 64: insufficient evidence on the size of the gain at 70, not evidence of
no gain -> [[Smoking and Mortality]].

**Heavy drinking is a dominant lever on the same logic**, though no source in this guide gives an
age-specific magnitude. After adjustment for abstainer biases, low-volume drinkers showed no
lower mortality than abstainers, RR 0.97 (0.88-1.07)
[@stockwell2016]: **the J-shaped curve gives no
reason to start or keep drinking for health** -> [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]].

### What the evidence cannot yet answer

- Weight loss at \~70 with muscle defended, on function, falls or fractures
  -> [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]].
- Pace of weight loss around 70 -> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]].
- Whether activity itself prevents dementia ->
  [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]].
- Older-age cardiovascular trials: HYVET sits inside the pooled blood-pressure estimates; PROSPER,
  SCORE2-OP and ATEMPT are not among this guide's sources
  -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].
- Deprescribing and polypharmacy: not covered by this guide's evidence, and outside its scope.
- Falls exercise above about 3 hours a week -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]; the RT
  dose for frail people -> [[Frailty]].

## What to do, in order

**If the person smokes, put quitting before every line below** (HR about 3 for current smokers;
quitting at 55-64 gained about 4 years) [[Smoking and Mortality]]. Heavy drinking likely belongs there
too [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]. A line that starts with *If* applies only to that
group.

1. **Train balance with high-challenge exercise, building toward about 3 hours of exercise a week, and
   keep it up** (falls 0.77, 0.71-0.83; community-dwelling people). Walking should not be anyone's only
   falls exercise. [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]
2. **If blood pressure is raised, lower it; discuss treatment.** Per 5 mmHg, 0.91 (0.88-0.95) at 65-74
   and 0.91 (0.87-0.96) at 75-84; 85+ is insufficient evidence; with frailty, a clinician trade-off. [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]
3. **Do resistance training twice a week, start at low volume, progress, add power work**: two sets of
   each of three lower-limb exercises, under 20 minutes, a labelled default. Power beat strength, SMD 0.43
   (0.23-0.62). [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]
4. **Use exercise for bone; skip routine vitamin D or calcium as a fracture lever if not deficient.**
   Major osteoporotic fractures 0.75 (0.59-0.94); kidney stones rose on the combination, RR 1.18
   (1.04-1.35). With low bone density, swap loaded flexion for extension. [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]

Lines 5-8 apply only to the group each one names.

5. **If prediabetic, join a DPP-style lifestyle programme** (at least 7% weight loss, 150 minutes of
   activity a week). [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]
6. **If at high cardiovascular risk, consider a Mediterranean diet**: HR 0.70 (0.55-0.89) in one
   repaired trial, men 55-80 and women 60-80; deaths did not fall. [[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]]
7. **If losing weight on purpose, pair it with RT and at least 1.0 g/kg protein.** **Not for
   unintentional loss or a frail person.** [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]]
8. **If the diet is Chinese-style, with most salt added at home, consider a salt substitute after a
   kidney and potassium check.** Not with kidney disease, potassium-sparing drugs or potassium
   supplements in the household; check first with diabetes or an ACE inhibitor/ARB, and with a thiazide
   for low blood sodium. [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

Lines 9 and 10 rest on weaker designs: a consensus on muscle markers, and cohort data.

9. **Eat at least 1.0-1.2 g protein per kg a day, 1.2 if exercising, about 25-30 g a meal**; with eGFR
   below 30 and not on dialysis, 0.8 g/kg ideal body weight. [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]]
10. **Walk toward 6000-8000 steps a day if below it** (observational; medians 3553-10,901 steps).
    [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

The last two lines cover drug choices and what not to expect.

11. **Weigh a statin on absolute risk; do not start aspirin.** Statin 0.84 (99% CI 0.70-1.01) at 71-75
    without vascular disease, insufficient evidence after 75; aspirin raised major bleeding, HR 1.43
    (1.30-1.56), and is Grade D at 60 and over.
    [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]
12. **Get hearing checked, but do not expect a multidomain programme to protect memory**: dementia HR
    0.98 (0.80-1.21), a wide interval.
    [[Hearing Loss and Dementia]], [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]]

### Caveats

- This page grades how well its claims fit their sources, not whether they are true; no step here has
  been checked against what happened to anyone who followed it.
- It appraises evidence and does not prescribe: the drug sections select no drug and no dose.

- It is written for a stratum, and applying it to one person means weighing their own risks, goals and
  constraints.
