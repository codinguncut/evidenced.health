*How much protein should I eat?* splits into four separate decisions — **how much, from what
source, when, and for whom** — each with its own answer. Separate them and most of the
confusion dissolves.

**If you lift weights, more protein helps a little, and only a little.** It lets training build
slightly more muscle, and the benefit levels off somewhere around 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day.
Treat that as a broad region to aim into, not a line to hit — and the training, not the powder, does
the work.

**If your goal is a longer life, the *source* matters more than the amount.** Shifting protein-bearing
foods from animal toward plant sources tracks lower mortality; dialling total protein up or down does
little on its own. This is an observational association, not a proven cause, and the effect is modest.

**Older adults are the one group who genuinely need more** — and not just more per day, but more per
*meal*, because ageing muscle needs a bigger protein hit to switch on. This is a real, mechanism-backed
stratum, not a licence for everyone to eat more.

**The kidney scare is unfounded for healthy kidneys.** Higher protein nudges a filtration marker upward
briefly, with no sign of damage. The one hard exception is established kidney disease, where protein is
deliberately restricted.

**And a caution that runs under all of it:** almost every muscle number here is steered by a
*surrogate* — muscle mass, lean mass, a filtration marker — not by proof that hitting the number makes
you live longer or stay on your feet. Hold the targets loosely.

## Split the one question into four before answering it

The single lay question runs four distinct decisions together, each on its own evidence and its own
curve:

- **How much** — the dose-response of protein *amount* for muscle, function, and mortality.
- **From what source** — animal versus plant, which turns out to be a *substitution* decision, not a
  dose one.
- **When and how good** — per-meal distribution and protein *quality* (the DIAAS score).
- **For whom** — younger versus older, trained versus untrained, in an energy deficit, or with kidney
  disease.

Answered separately they mostly stop contradicting each other. Most of the apparent conflict in protein
advice comes from stacking an answer to one question onto a different question's curve.

## The famous numbers each sit on a different curve, so don't stack them

Four numbers get quoted as if they were rival answers to one question. They are not — they answer
different questions on different denominators, and you can compare only same-denominator numbers.

| Number | What it actually answers | Denominator |
|---|---|---|
| **0.8 g/kg/day** (the RDA) | the *floor* that prevents deficiency in \~97% of adults | g / kg body weight / day |
| **\~1.6 g/kg/day** | where *muscle-building* during resistance training plateaus | g / kg body weight / day |
| **\~1 g/lb (\~2.2 g/kg)** | a gym heuristic — near the top of the muscle range, not a requirement | g / kg body weight / day |
| **1.0-1.2 g/kg/day** | *maintenance* for a healthy older adult | g / kg body weight / day |

The RDA is a *deficiency floor deployed as if it were a target*: it is «too low for older people» and
answers a different question than muscle maintenance
[@bauer2013]. The daily figures form a coherent
ladder by *goal* — floor 0.8 -> older maintenance 1.0-1.2 -> muscle-building \~1.6 — same unit
throughout, so the higher number does not contradict the lower; they answer *prevent deficiency* versus
*maintain muscle* versus *build muscle*. A per-*meal* dose (below) is a different axis again, and must
not be read off the per-day ladder.
[inferred from @bauer2013]

## For building muscle, protein is a small adjunct to training that levels off near 1.6 g/kg

The muscle evidence is RCT-grade but sits on *surrogates* (muscle mass and strength), and its headline
is that **training is the driver and protein a modest adjunct**. Pooling 49 RCTs, adding protein to
resistance training beat placebo by «2.49 kg (0.64, 4.33)» on 1RM strength and «0.30 kg (0.09, 0.52)»
of fat-free mass [@morton2018].
Read the magnitudes before the significance: a few kg on a 1RM and a third of a kg of lean mass, over
weeks of training, are *small* increments on top of what the training itself delivers. Protein did not
move total body mass or maximal voluntary contraction at all. Most trainees already eat about 1.4
g/kg/day, so the move is *topping up to* roughly 1.6, not loading beyond it — the supplement's benefit
also «reduced with increasing age» and was larger in the already-trained
[@morton2018].

Protein even adds a little muscle *without* resistance training — an independent Japanese meta-analysis
(138 trials, no author overlap with the above) «demon- strates for the first time that protein
supplementation is significantly effective without resistance training in a di- verse population»
[@tagawa2020]. But the effect is
small and additive, not a substitute for training — relevant mainly where lifting is off the table
(the frail, the very elderly). [inferred from @tagawa2020]

## Treat 1.6 g/kg as a region, not a line — no study has actually pinned a knee

The plateau «beyond total protein intakes of 1.62 g/kg/day resulted in no further RET-induced gains in
FFM» [@morton2018] — but the fit
that locates that knee is *not statistically significant*: «break point=1.62 g protein/kg/day, p=0.079»
with a wide «95% CI, (1.03 to 2.20)» [@morton2018].
On the baseline-intake axis a straight line actually fit *better* than a knee, and a second,
independent-lab analysis in dieters likewise found a monotone line beat a curve. So *1.62* is a central
estimate inside a fuzzy region running from about 1.0 to 2.2, not a demonstrated threshold. The
underlying muscle-protein-synthesis mechanism *does* saturate, so a plateau is plausible — but its
existence and location are held loosely, not asserted.

This matters for how to hedge. Because the knee's location is uncertain and *undershooting forfeits the
muscle objective* while overshooting is low-harm for healthy kidneys, someone whose priority is
hypertrophy can reasonably aim the upper end — roughly 1.8-2.2 g/kg/day — with a ceiling where the
evidence thins and the practical costs (displacing other food, satiety, expense) rise. That is a
decision under uncertainty, not a claim that more protein raises the average effect.
[inferred from @morton2018]

**A denominator trap to name:** *1.6 g/kg* means grams per kg of *total* body weight. In a lean person
that barely differs from lean-mass scaling; in someone with obesity the two diverge roughly twofold, so
the gram target is undefined until you say which kg — and people with obesity were not studied here, an
open gap rather than a lower-target finding.
[inferred from @morton2018]

## Cutting weight raises the target, and training plus protein is what protects the muscle

Losing weight — by diet or by a GLP-1 drug — sheds some muscle along with fat, and a caloric deficit
*raises* the protein needed to hold onto lean mass. The one dedicated analysis (29 studies of nonobese,
resistance-trained adults, which the authors label **exploratory**) puts the retention target higher
than the energy-balance figure: «increasing protein intake up to 1.9 g/kgBM/day or 2.5 g/kgFFM/day, on
average, is asso- ciated with less FFM loss» [@refalo2025].
Two bounds keep this honest: the shape is again *linear with no proven knee*, and the protein dial is a
*small* lever next to the training stimulus and the size of the deficit — a well-programmed lift and a
modest (not aggressive) calorie cut do more for keeping muscle than the protein number does. The
evidence is on lean mass, a surrogate.

That raised \~1.9 g/kg *target*, and the BF%-scaled reasoning behind it, come from resistance-trained
*non-obese* dieters, so the specific number does not transport to people with obesity. The **direction**,
though, now does: two independent gold meta-analyses show higher protein preserving fat-free mass under a
deficit in the overweight and obese too, at a lower, more modest dose. Pooling 24 isocaloric high- versus
standard-protein weight-loss RCTs in overweight and obese adults (structured exercise excluded), the
higher-protein arm produced «mitigation of reductions in fat-free mass (FFM; 0.43 kg; 95% CI: 0.09, 0.78
kg)» plus more fat loss (-0.87 kg, 95% CI -1.26, -0.48) at only \~1.25 versus \~0.72 g/kg/day achieved
[@wycherley2012]. The same holds in
adults aged 50 and older: across 20 RCTs the higher-protein group lost less lean mass (+0.45 kg, 95% CI
0.20-0.71 by percentage of energy; +0.83 kg, 95% CI 0.47-1.19 by g/kg/day)
[@kim2016]. So higher protein sparing
muscle during a deficit reaches the obese as well — what stays off-support for them is Refalo's raised
*target* and its dose-response, not the direction, and both contrast trials show the benefit at a modest
\~1.0-1.25 g/kg, not near 1.9 -> [[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]].

The practical direction is robust even where the exact number is not: **during any weight loss, keep
training and keep protein up.** For someone on a GLP-1 drug this is the collision to watch — the drug
suppresses appetite exactly when the muscle-sparing protein target has gone *up*, not down.
[inferred from @refalo2025]

## Older adults need protein spread across meals, not just added to the day

This is the stratum where *eat more protein* is genuinely right — and it is right for a specific,
mechanistic reason. Ageing blunts the muscle's response to a protein meal
([[Anabolic Resistance|anabolic resistance]]): old
muscle needs a *bigger per-meal stimulus* to trigger the same building response. The signature shows up
in metabolic studies — muscle-protein synthesis «reached a plateau after ingestion of 0.40 ± 0.19 and
0.24 ± 0.06 g/kg body mass ... in older and younger men, respectively»
[@moore2014] (a higher per-meal threshold in
the old, though the old-vs-young difference is borderline, p=.055) — and in the finding that «increasing the proportion of leucine in a mixture of EAA can
reverse an attenuated response of muscle protein synthesis in elderly but does not result in further
stimulation of muscle protein synthesis in young subjects»
[@katsanos2006] — a lever specific to
the aged muscle, not a general one.

The consensus target that follows from this mechanism: healthy older adults «should consume an average daily intake in the
range of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg BW/d» (1.2 if active, 1.2-1.5 in acute or chronic illness), delivered as «25
to 30 g protein per meal, containing about 2.5 to 2.8 g leucine»
[@bauer2013]. The decision is therefore *raise and
distribute*: spread protein across breakfast, lunch and dinner so each meal clears the threshold,
rather than back-loading it at dinner (the usual Western pattern, which wastes the sub-threshold meals).
Because most older adults eat *less* protein than younger ones, the common failure is undershoot.

One transport error to avoid: the \~1.6 g/kg muscle-building figure is a younger-adult number, and
anabolic resistance runs the correction *upward*, not down. For an older adult who is actually training
for hypertrophy, 1.6 is a *floor, not a ceiling* — do not read the 1.0-1.2 maintenance target, or the
younger 1.6, as a cap on the older lifter.

Two honest limits. This target rests on *consensus plus a handful of small acute metabolic studies*
(surrogate endpoints — nitrogen balance and acute synthesis, no fracture or mortality trial),
and several of those sources carry protein-industry funding — so the direction is trustworthy but the
*eat more / supplement / leucine* emphasis is held loosely.
[inferred from @bauer2013; @moore2014; @katsanos2006]

## For living longer, the source outweighs the amount

The muscle story is about quantity; the *mortality* story is about **source**, and it is a different
decision on different (observational) evidence. Pooling 32 cohorts (715 128 people, 113 039 deaths),
total protein is weakly protective, **animal protein is flatly null** on every outcome, and the signal
tracks **plant** protein [@naghshi2020]:

| Exposure | All-cause | CVD | Cancer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total protein | «0.94 ... 0.89 to 0.99» (inverse) | «0.98 ... 0.94 to 1.03» null | «0.98 ... 0.92 to 1.05» null |
| Animal protein | «1.00 ... 0.94 to 1.05» null | «1.02 ... 0.94 to 1.11» null | «1.00 ... 0.98 to 1.02» null |
| Plant protein | «0.92 ... 0.87 to 0.97» inverse | «0.88 ... 0.80 to 0.96» inverse | «0.99 ... 0.94 to 1.05» null |

[@naghshi2020]

The dose-response confirms it: «an additional 3% of energy from plant proteins a day was associated with
a 5% lower risk of death from all causes (pooled effect size 0.95, 95 0.93 to 0.98, P<0.001)», while
total and animal protein were flat [@naghshi2020]. The
lever this points to is a **substitution** — shift protein-bearing foods from animal toward plant
sources (legumes, nuts, whole grains, soy) — not a total-protein dial.

Four cautions keep it modest and honest. The effect is *small* (\~5% relative per 3% of energy) and
*observational*, so it is an association, not a demonstrated cause — the authors' own leading caveat is
confounding by overall diet pattern and social class. The *animal protein* null is a null on the
*nutrient*, not a green light for animal *foods* ([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]): the bucket
pools fish, poultry, eggs and dairy with red meat, so a red-meat-specific harm can hide inside an
average of zero. *Plant protein* may partly be the *fibre and whole-grain* foods it travels in, wearing
another name — the data cannot separate them.

And the pooled cohorts are *not* independent replication:
the meta-analysis draws on overlapping US cohorts (the Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals
Follow-up Study recur across its inputs) and shares authorship lineage (Willett co-authors here and
across the parallel carbohydrate work), so the 715 128-person sample reads as more corroboration than
it is — a genuinely independent cohort is still owed.
[inferred from @naghshi2020]

## Protein *quality* tells you plant sources need more grams — but it moves no outcome you can feel

*Protein quality* is amino-acid composition (how well a food's indispensable amino acids match human
need — the *limiting* one caps the score) times digestibility, scored by the DIAAS
([[Protein Quality and the DIAAS Score]]). On FAO's own worked
example, plant sources score well below dairy:

| Food | Protein (g/100 g) | DIAAS | FAO quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wheat | 11 | 40 | Low |
| Peas | 21 | 64 | Low |
| Whole milk powder | 28 | 122 | High |

[@fao2013]

The decision consequence is real but bounded: a gram of pea protein delivers fewer usable amino acids
than a gram of milk protein, so hitting a protein target from unprocessed plant foods needs *more grams
of protein — hence more food* — or deliberate cereal-plus-legume complementation. Two things shrink the
lever. First, DIAAS is a **[[Surrogate Outcomes|surrogate]]**: the wiki holds *no* evidence that choosing higher-DIAAS protein
improves any outcome a person can feel, once they are already meeting their protein need from a mixed
diet — for anyone eating any dairy, eggs, fish or meat, the limiting-amino-acid problem is solved at the
*diet* level regardless of a single food's score. Second, traditional processing (soaking, fermenting,
sprouting; tofu and tempeh) raises plant digestibility, so part of what feels like a *plant* penalty is
really an *unprocessed* one.
[inferred from @fao2013]

## Higher protein does not harm healthy kidneys — established kidney disease is the one flip

The standing objection to every higher-protein target is that it damages the kidneys. For **normal**
kidneys, the direct evidence is a null. Pooling 28 RCTs of adults without chronic kidney disease
(including people with obesity, hypertension, or type-2 diabetes), the change in glomerular filtration
rate did not differ between higher- and lower-protein diets (SMD 0.11, −0.05 to 0.27; P=0.16), and the
authors conclude «a nonexistent or trivial effect of HP consumption on GFR in individuals with normal
kidney function» [@devries2018]. The
small filtration rise that higher protein *does* produce is read as benign kidney functional reserve
(the same adaptive rise seen in pregnancy), not damage, and kidney-damage markers do not move.

Two caveats sit on the null. The evidence is *[[Rating Certainty of Evidence|low-to-very-low certainty]]* on a *surrogate* (a filtration
marker over weeks to months, not hard kidney failure over years), and it comes from one research
lineage with dairy/meat-industry funding — enough to *defuse the objection* for healthy kidneys, not
enough to declare protein *proven safe at any dose forever*. The one hard exception is a genuine stratum
flip: in **established kidney disease** (the consensus draws the line at severe disease, eGFR below 30
and not on dialysis), protein is deliberately *restricted*. Screening for that is a clinical act outside
this wiki's scope; the decision-relevant fact is that the boundary exists and is narrow.
[inferred from @devries2018]

## Muscle and strength predict how long you live — but a predictor is not a lever

Why bother preserving muscle at all? Because both muscle *mass* and muscle *strength* independently
predict mortality — which raises the *stakes* of the protein-and-training lever without proving it moves
the outcome. Low grip **strength** predicts death across a wide range of causes in half a million adults
(«hazard ratio 1.48 and 1.57 per 1 SD lower grip strength» for all-cause and CVD mortality, out-ranking
blood pressure and physical activity in the same model)
[@celismorales2018]. Low muscle **mass** predicts it too
and «cannot be completely explained by differences in muscle strength» — those who died carried «ASMI SMD
= −0.18, CI95% −0.23 to −0.12» less muscle [@santana2021].
This is why the sarcopenia case definition now screens on *strength first* — «strength is better than
mass in predicting adverse outcomes» [@cruzjentoft2019]
— with mass a confirmatory add.

The load-bearing caveat: these are *predictors, not proven levers*. Grip and mass are partly *lowered
by* underlying ill-health, so a low value flags higher risk — but no trial shows that *raising* muscle
by protein and training lowers mortality. *Squeeze harder to live longer* over-reads the evidence. The mortality link
earns the *ranking*: preserving muscle and strength is worth doing for function,
falls, and independence in their own right — patient-important outcomes — with an associational
mortality rationale on top, not a promise.
[inferred from @celismorales2018; @santana2021; @cruzjentoft2019]

## The bottom line

- **Do resistance training first; protein is the small adjunct.** If you train, aim into a *region*
  around 1.6 g/kg body weight per day — topping up, not loading. For a hard hypertrophy goal, bias
  toward 1.8-2.2; nothing warrants going higher.
- **Do bias protein-bearing foods plant-ward for longevity** — the source, not the amount, is what the
  mortality evidence tracks. It is a small, associational lever, ranked below the big rocks.
- **Do treat older adults as a real stratum:** \~1.0-1.2 g/kg/day (more in illness), spread as \~25-30 g
  of protein *per meal* so each meal clears the raised threshold. Undershoot is the usual failure.
- **Do raise protein and keep training during weight loss** (diet or GLP-1 drug) — the deficit raises
  the muscle-sparing target even as appetite falls.
- **Don't hold protein back over kidney fear if your kidneys are healthy** — the exception is diagnosed
  kidney disease, where protein is restricted.
- **Don't read any single number as a precise target.** Name which question it answers, carry its
  uncertainty, and remember most rest on surrogates.

## Caveats

- **The loop is open.** This appraisal grades coherence and fidelity to its sources — never validity
  against the world. Almost every muscle target here is steered by a *surrogate* (muscle mass, lean
  mass, acute synthesis, a filtration marker); no operation here checks whether hitting a protein
  number changes what a person actually experiences. The mortality findings are *observational
  associations*, not demonstrated causes.
- **Appraise, not prescribe.** This page states what protein amount, source, timing, and quality *do* to
  each outcome, for identifiable strata. It does not set a personal gram target, screen your kidneys,
  or manage kidney disease — those need your labs, history, and a clinician.
- **Population-level by default.** This page names the strata explicitly (older adults, the resistance-trained,
  those in an energy deficit, established kidney disease); place yourself in the narrowest one the
  evidence supports, and weigh the outcomes by what matters to you.
- **One axis: health.** This page weighs longevity, function, and muscle. It holds no environmental, ethical,
  cost, or animal-welfare data; where the animal-versus-plant choice carries load on those axes, that
  trade-off exists but is not priced here.
