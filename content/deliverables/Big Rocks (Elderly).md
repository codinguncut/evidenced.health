> [!warning] Applies to a specific group
> This guide is written for **obese adults around 70**. If you are not in this group, the recommendations here may not apply to you — what holds for the wider population can differ.

> [!note] How to read the numbers
> - **HR, RR and OR** (hazard ratio, risk or rate ratio, odds ratio) compare one group's risk with another's. Below one means lower risk; above one means higher.
> - **The range in brackets** is the confidence interval: the span of results the data are compatible with. When a ratio's range crosses one, the study shows no clear effect.
> - **SMD, ES and g** are effect sizes without units, used to pool studies that measured on different scales. The further from zero, the larger the difference.
> - **MD and WMD** are the average difference between groups (weighted, for WMD), in the measure's own units.
> - **CVD** is cardiovascular disease: heart attacks, strokes and related conditions of the heart and blood vessels.
> - **Person-years** count follow-up time: one person followed for one year adds one person-year.
> - **Certainty** (high, moderate, low, very low) is how confident the reviewers are that the true effect is close to the estimate.
> - **Timed up and go** is a timed test: stand up from a chair, walk a short distance, turn, walk back and sit down. Faster is better.
> - **The six-minute walk** measures how far a person walks in that time. Farther is better.
> - **Relative effect** is the risk with a treatment as a fraction of the risk without it (a ratio of about three-quarters means about a quarter less risk); **baseline risk** is your starting risk.

At around 70, a fracture or a move into care weighs more than a fall itself, and a change that pays
back within months to a few years counts for relatively more than one that takes decades -> [[Rating Outcome Importance]].
This guide calls each such change — a habit, an exercise programme, a diet, a drug — a *lever*.

**If you smoke, quitting comes first; cutting heavy drinking does too, where present.** Quitting
recovered years of life at every quit age covered by the study this guide draws on. None of this
guide's studies measures that gain at 70, so this guide ranks quitting first at that age by inference.
Heavy drinking (six or more drinks a day) ranks beside it, on the strength of blood-pressure trials in
middle-aged adults and genetic evidence of stroke harm. Its rank at this age is again an inference
 -> [[Smoking and Mortality]],
[[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]. After that, the levers rank as follows.

- **For a non-smoker, a structured balance-and-strength exercise programme is the best-proven lever,
  and this guide ranks it first.** It reduces falls with high certainty, and the effect did not shrink detectably
  past 75. Balance and functional practice drives the effect; neither resistance training alone nor
  walking alone has been shown to prevent falls -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].
- **Resistance training keeps muscle working, and a low weekly volume matched or beat higher volumes
  on timed up and go and the 6-minute walk** (low certainty; fast walking speed favoured more volume). Reaching the older-adult protein minimum is the smaller second
  step; extra protein on top of training added nothing measurable in healthy
  older adults -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]],
  [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].
- **Lowering blood pressure still pays, and usually pays more at this age**: from 55 to 84 the relative
  effect holds level while baseline risk rises, and trials measured a larger absolute cut in older groups. It also slightly reduces dementia, the one lever with randomized evidence for that ->
  [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]],
  [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]].
- **People who move more through the day, light activity included, die at lower rates**; after 60 the
  step curve levels off at a lower daily count than in younger adults. These are cohort comparisons,
  not trials -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].
- **A structured lifestyle programme prevents type 2 diabetes in people over 60 at least as well as in
  younger adults**; metformin did little at this age -> [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].

The levers below help only under certain conditions, or help less.

- **Intentional weight loss depends on what it is for.** It is worth doing for a named target (painful
  knees, raised blood sugar, a fatty liver), with resistance training and protein to reduce the muscle it
  costs. No trial among this guide's sources tests whether losing weight at this age extends life. The
  trials that showed a survival benefit enrolled mostly middle-aged adults -> [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]],
  [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]].
- **Exercise probably reduces major osteoporotic fracture**, though the evidence in older men is thin.
  Vitamin D and calcium pills do not reduce fractures in people living at home who are not deficient
  -> [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]], [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]].
- **Starting daily aspirin for prevention gives no net benefit at this age. Starting a statin after 75
  without known heart disease is unproven either way**. With prior vascular disease, the case for a
  statin holds at any age -> [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]],
  [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]].
- **Most supplement pills add little, and brain-specific programmes have not been shown to prevent
  dementia.** Bundled brain-health programmes have not prevented dementia in unselected people in
  their seventies, and for multivitamins the evidence is insufficient. Once the large levers are in
  place, each has a limit to what it can add, and what is left to gain is small ->
  [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]],
  [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]].

## Count falls, fractures and lost independence first, because slow benefits have fewer years to arrive

**At around 70, losing independence and breaking a hip weigh more than a fall itself.** A review of
patient values done for the Canadian falls-prevention guideline put each health state on a scale where
1 equals death. Admission to long-term care scored a median of 1, «equivalent to death». That figure
comes from a single time-trade-off study of 194 adults aged 75 and over, and it is low certainty
[@pillay2024falls].

A hip fracture probably costs 0.53 in the first three months and 0.16 at twelve months. Impairment in at
least one activity of daily living (a basic task such as washing or dressing) may cost 0.12, and a fall
0.09 over a year (both low certainty). The
authors conclude that **a fracture is probably more important than a fall or a loss of function**, and
that function may matter somewhat more than falls
[@pillay2024falls]
-> [[Rating Outcome Importance]].

**Read these weights as an order, not a sum.** They say which outcomes count as critical at this age;
they do not add up to one health score. How a year with a mended hip trades against a year of life is
the reader's call, not the evidence's -> [[Rating Outcome Importance]]. The order also
shows what most trials miss. **Most falls trials count falls, the outcome patients weight least**;
people fear the fracture and the move into care that a fall can trigger. Lean mass and bone density
sit one step further back. They are markers (measurements that stand in for an outcome), and their
link to a fracture or to independence has to be shown before a change in them counts as a benefit ->
[[Surrogate Outcomes]].

**A lever that cuts disabling fractures or strokes has not failed just because it leaves all-cause
mortality (death from any cause) unchanged.** It buys years lived without disability, a separate good
from added years
-> [[Rating Outcome Importance]].

**Trials rarely measure the course people most want: keep function, then decline quickly.** They count
events: falls, fractures, deaths. A composite measure of function does exist.
WHO's intrinsic capacity scores cognition, locomotion, vitality, psychological state and the senses, and
a 2026 systematic review of 15 trials paired it with quality of life as its definition of healthspan.
Exercise, alone or inside a multidomain programme (one that combines several kinds of change), gave the
most consistent signal (11 of 13 studies); too few trials tested anything else to judge it. The effect
sizes were generally small, and the authors call their clinical significance unclear
[@zheng2026healthspan].

The quality-of-life half is self-reported in trials that could not be blinded, so this guide gives even
that signal less weight -> [[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]. None of this guide's
sources measures the shape of late-life decline; a survival result says nothing about it.

### Competing risk — dying of something else first — leaves less time for slow-payoff levers

**At 70, a lever that pays off over decades has fewer decades to pay off in.** The European Society of
Cardiology (ESC) calls age «the major driver of CVD risk». Almost everyone aged 70 or over already
exceeds the usual ten-year treatment thresholds, and the lifetime benefit of treatment is lower, so ESC
sets higher thresholds at that age [@esc2021]. Ten-year risk under
7.5% counts as low-to-moderate at 70 and over, against under 2.5% below 50. In the 7.5% to under 15%
band, ESC asks clinicians to weigh frailty, lifetime benefit, comorbidities (other illnesses),
polypharmacy (taking many medicines) and patient preferences. It says strict criteria for that
weighing cannot be defined
[@esc2021] -> [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].

**ESC's lifetime grids show how fast the years of benefit shrink with age.** For a non-smoking man aged 45-49, lowering
LDL cholesterol by 1 mmol/L buys **roughly 0.6 to 1.6 years free of heart attack or stroke; at 90 and
over the same reduction buys about 0.0 to 0.3 years** [@esc2021]
-> [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

**These figures come from a model (LIFE-CVD) multiplied by a hazard ratio of 0.78 from a meta-analysis
of lipid-lowering trials** [@esc2021],
not from anyone followed for a lifetime. The endpoint also counts death as well as a
cardiovascular event: ESC defines lifetime risk as the age by which a person has a 50% chance to
«either have experienced a CVD event or have died». So competing death — dying of other causes first —
is built into the shrinking gain [@esc2021]. A
ten-year absolute benefit can stay large at 75 while the lifetime gain shrinks; the two numbers answer
different questions. The blood-pressure section below takes up the statin decision.

**Older people start at higher risk, and that pulls the other way.** A lever's absolute benefit is its
relative effect applied to the person's starting risk (the baseline risk), and the relative effect is
usually similar across a wide range of baseline
risks [@grade] -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. A
70-year-old starts higher on falls, fractures and cardiovascular events, so a lever that pays back within
a trial's own follow-up removes more events per person than it would at 50. Together the two forces
favour quick payoffs: **competing death weakens the case for levers whose payoff takes decades, and
higher baseline risk strengthens the case for levers that pay off within months to a few years.**
Falls-prevention exercise is the clearest of the second kind.

**No study this guide draws on measures time-to-benefit — how long each lever takes to pay off — lever
by lever at this age.** The case for favouring quick payoffs rests on ESC's modelled lifetime frame, not
on a measured competing-risk analysis. The trials built for this age are not among this guide's sources
(they are listed with blood pressure below). **No single birthday switches a lifestyle lever
from worth doing to not**: decide by muscle status, remaining life expectancy and baseline risk, not by
chronological age
 -> [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]. Among this guide's sources, starting
aspirin for prevention is the clearest case of a lever whose balance of benefit and harm reverses with
age (the timing of hormone therapy is contested). The blood-pressure section below takes it up.

### Frail older adults face about twice the risk, so a lever that works buys them more

**Frail older adults die at about twice the rate of robust ones.** A meta-analysis of 31 prospective
studies in 158,764 adults aged 65 and over living at home (community-dwelling) found mortality RR 2.01
(1.82-2.22) for the frail and RR 1.47 (1.32-1.62) for the prefrail, each against the robust. Being frail
or prefrail, pooled together and set against being robust, also predicted the outcomes that head this
section: institutionalization (HR/RR 1.65, 1.48-1.84),
disability in basic daily activities (1.62, 1.50-1.76), fractures (1.37, 1.21-1.54) and falls (1.24,
1.12-1.37) [@vermeiren2016frailty].

These are observational associations with very high heterogeneity, meaning the studies' results varied
widely (I2 95-98% for mortality). So **frailty marks risk; it does not establish a cause** -> [[Frailty]].
None of this guide's sources gives baseline event rates for this group, so this guide cannot give
absolute figures (events per 1,000 people over a stated period).

**That higher risk makes a working lever worth more to the frail**, by the baseline-risk
arithmetic above -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].
Whether a given lever works in people already frail is a separate question, answered lever by lever
below; for falls, the evidence in the frail is much thinner than in older adults generally -> [[Frailty]].

**The same few levers pay into several of these outcomes at once.** Blood pressure, physical activity,
adiposity, blood sugar and smoking recur across the risk lists for dementia, cardiovascular disease and
cancer, and physical activity also preserves function and mobility
-> [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]. A lever that moves several critical outcomes
outranks one that moves a single outcome at equal effect -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].
These shared risk factors come from observational risk estimates, and the attributable fractions behind
them (the share of cases each factor accounts for) do not add up across factors.

Of these outcomes, falls have the strongest trial evidence for a lifestyle lever at this age; the
fractures and moves into care that a fall can trigger are measured far less well. One habit
comes before the falls programme: if you still smoke, start there.

## If you still smoke, quitting stays the largest lever you have

**Smokers die at about three times the rate of people who never smoked**: all-cause HR 3.0 (99% CI
2.7-3.3) in women and 2.8 (2.4-3.1) in men. The study followed 216,917 US adults at ages 25-79 and
adjusted for age, education, alcohol and adiposity (body fat). Quitting recovered life at every quit age
the study covered.
The latest band is 55-64 (median 59): those quitters **gained about 4 years of life** and carried a
residual HR of 1.7 (1.5-2.0), against 2.9 (2.7-3.1) for people who kept smoking. Jha gives the reason
the gain stays large late: «Because the absolute risks of continuing to smoke are large, the absolute
benefits of cessa- tion will also be large, particularly as death rates among those who have never
smoked continue to fall.» [@jha2013]

**None of this guide's studies measures what quitting at 70 buys**, so this guide gives no years-gained
figure for it. That quitting helps is not in doubt. Each later quit band in Jha's table recovers less,
but none recovers nothing. A 70-year-old smoker's absolute risk of death is higher than a 59-year-old's,
which keeps the absolute gain large even if the relative one shrinks. This guide ranks quitting above
the exercise programme in the next section by inference beyond Jha's table, not from a measurement
[INFERRED; [[Smoking and Mortality]], [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]].

**Heavy drinking is the other habit to change first, where present** [INFERRED; [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]]. In 36 trials (2,865 people, mean age 49.5), people
starting at six or more drinks a day (a drink = 12 g alcohol) who cut their intake by about half
lowered systolic blood pressure by 5.50 mmHg (95% CI 4.30-6.70). People starting at two or fewer saw
no measurable change (-0.18 mmHg, -1.02 to 0.66). Baseline intakes averaged 15 to 76 g a day across
those bands. This is a blood-pressure change; the trials measured no strokes or deaths
[@roerecke2017alcohol].

**Light drinking probably does not lengthen life.** Its apparent benefit (RR 0.86, 0.83-0.90, against
abstainers) falls to RR 0.97 (0.88-1.07) once the analysis makes two adjustments. One is for who was
counted as an abstainer (former and occasional drinkers); the other is for study characteristics such
as follow-up length, exclusion of people already ill, and control for smoking. Adjusting for the
abstainer definition, demographics and drinking-measure quality alone still left RR 0.89 (0.84-0.94)
[@stockwell2016].
Genetic analysis in Chinese adults, mostly men, finds no sign that drinking at lower levels protects
against stroke
[@millwood2019], though for heart
attack it had too few cases to exclude a small effect in either direction
[@millwood2019]
-> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

For the non-smoker, the best-proven lever is exercise of a specific kind.

## Do balance and strength training to prevent falls; it is the best-proven lever at this age

**A structured balance-and-strength exercise programme reduces falls, and the evidence for it is
high-certainty.** A Cochrane review pooled 108 randomised trials of 23,407 people living in the community,
mean age 76. Exercise cut the rate of falls by about a quarter (rate ratio 0.77, 95% CI 0.71-0.83; 59
trials, 12,981 people). That is **195
fewer falls per 1,000 people per year** (144 to 246 fewer), against a control rate of 850. It also cut the
number of people who fell (RR 0.85, 0.81-0.89): 72 fewer fallers per 1,000 per year (52 to 91 fewer),
against 480. Dropping the trials at high risk of bias left the result essentially unchanged, so the review
did not lower its certainty rating. [@sherrington2019]

Trials count falls directly and **can randomise people to exercise or to a control group**, so this lever
reaches a certainty that observational lifestyle evidence rarely does -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].

**The effect does not shrink detectably past 75.** Trials of people aged 75 or older gave a rate ratio of
0.83 (0.72-0.97), trials of younger people 0.75 (0.69-0.82). The test for a difference gave P = 0.24, and
the review reads this as probably little or no difference by age. The relative effect also did not vary
with baseline fall risk, so people at higher risk avert more falls each, while the general community still
benefits. [@sherrington2019] Higher-risk people
gain more because they start higher, not because exercise works differently in them ->
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

### Balance and functional practice drives the effect

The exercise types differ, and the choice between them matters. The falls figures below are the review's
own corresponding risks: its estimate of the fall rate on each programme, given the control rate.

| Programme | Rate of falls, ratio (95% CI) | Falls per 1,000 per year (control 850) | Certainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance and functional training (gait, balance, task practice) | 0.76 (0.70-0.81), 39 trials | 646 (595-689) | high |
| Multiple types, usually balance plus resistance training | 0.66 (0.50-0.88), 11 trials | 561 (425-748) | moderate |
| Tai chi | 0.81 (0.67-0.99), 7 trials | 689 (570-842) | low |
| Resistance training alone | 1.14 (0.67-1.97), 5 trials | 969 (570-1,675) | very low |
| General physical activity, including walking | 1.14 (0.66-1.97), 2 trials | 969 (561-1,675) | very low |
| Dance alone | 1.34 (0.98-1.83), 1 trial | 1,139 (833-1,556) | very low |

[@sherrington2019]

**Programmes that challenge balance safely reduce falls; resistance training on its own has not been
shown to.** The review's own summary: «Exercise programmes that reduce falls primarily involve balance and
functional exercises, while programmes that probably reduce falls include multiple exercise categories
(typically balance and functional exercises plus resistance exercises).»
[@sherrington2019] The resistance-only interval runs from
a large benefit to a large harm across five small trials, so that row shows insufficient evidence, not
evidence of no effect.
Resistance training still belongs in the programme for muscle and function, which the next section takes
up. -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]

In these trials, balance training meant «transferring bodyweight from one part of the body to another or
challenging specific aspects of the balance systems», and «Training is assessment-based, tailored and
progressed.» [@sherrington2019] So the programme should
get harder as the person improves rather than settle into a fixed routine. The exact drill progression is
a coaching call this guide does not make.

**Fast, power-style resistance training improves function tests more than slow, heavy lifting, but no
trial has shown it prevents falls.** Against conventional strength training it improved generic function
tests (walk distance, chair rises, balance) by SMD 0.43 (0.23-0.62), high certainty, 11 of the review's
15 trials. [@elhadouchi2022] A second meta-analysis
looked at the tests that predict falls. It found timed-up-and-go times 0.31 s faster than other exercise
(MD -0.31 s, 95% CI -0.63 to
0.00), below the 3.2-second change the test can reliably detect, and its authors call for trials that
count falls. [@jimenezlupion2023]
Training for power refines the programme; it is not a separate lever ->
[[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]].

### Fall-related fractures may fall too, with low certainty

**Exercise may reduce fall-related fractures, by about 17 per 1,000 people over the trials' follow-up**
. Fractures fell from 64 to 47 per 1,000 (36-61) over 4 to 42 months
(RR 0.73, 0.56-0.95, 10 trials). The review graded this low certainty, for imprecision (a wide range)
and likely publication bias (unfavourable results left unpublished). Balance and functional training
alone gave RR 0.44 (0.25-0.76; 29 instead of 64 per
1,000 over 6 to 30 months, 7 trials), also low certainty: a larger point estimate (the single best
guess), resting on thinner data.
[@sherrington2019]

Fewer people may have a fall needing medical attention: 129 instead of 211 per 1,000 over a year (100 to
167; RR 0.61, 0.47-0.79, 5 trials, low certainty). Falls leading to hospital admission are unclear (RR 0.78, 0.51-1.18, very low), and
quality of life barely moved (SMD -0.03, -0.10 to 0.04). [@sherrington2019]
**The fracture estimate stays low-certainty because trials rarely run long or large enough to count
fractures, not because the effect is shown to be absent.** Exercise that loads bone directly is a second
route to fewer fractures. Separate trials test it, and the bone section takes it up; neither route raises
the other's certainty. -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]

### Join a supervised programme, at least three sessions a week, and keep it going

**The effective programmes ran for months, not weeks.** Most trials lasted 12 weeks or more and nearly a
third a year or more. Most head-to-head trials were not powered to tell programmes apart, though
within-study comparisons gave «some indication that higher doses of exercise were associated with a
greater impact on the rate of falls and the number of people falling».
[@sherrington2019]

A network meta-analysis of 219 trials found two kinds of exercise with at least low-certainty evidence
of benefit across more outcomes than other exercise. One was supervised, long-duration balance and
resistance programmes (more than two supervised sessions, longer than three months); the other was
group tai chi. [@pillay2024falls]
The World Guidelines for Falls, which this evidence informed, recommend for people at intermediate risk
**supervised exercise at least three times a week for at least 12 weeks**, on functional balance and
resistance or tai chi. [@pillay2024falls]
Read that as the guideline's minimum, not a best dose that trials measured.

**Across trials, a health professional delivering the programme did somewhat better.** This compares
different trials rather than testing within one, so it is a hypothesis rather than a tested effect.
The rate ratio was 0.69
(0.61-0.79) with a health professional against 0.82 (0.75-0.90) with a trained non-health instructor
(P = 0.04), and both reduced falls. [@sherrington2019]
At the 850 control rate that is roughly 264 against 153 fewer falls per 1,000 people per year
.

For function tests rather than falls, a review of 34
studies from 30 trials (2,830 people aged 60 and over) found supervision added a small edge. Most of it
disappeared when the authors re-checked their analysis (a sensitivity analysis); only knee-extension
strength remained (SMD 0.18, 0.07-0.30). The review judged home exercise a safe alternative where
supervision is not feasible.
[@gomezredondo2024] -> [[Supervised vs Unsupervised Exercise]]

**For a painful obese knee or hip, balance work in water is a reasonable substitute for land.** One
meta-analysis of 29 trials (mean age 66) found only overall balance favoured water, after trials with an
opposite direction of change were excluded (Berg Balance Scale SMD 0.82, 0.17-1.48, I2 87%).
Chair-stand, timed-up-and-go and walking outcomes showed
no difference. [@deng2024] Buoyancy lets a person
with painful joints do the balance work at all, and that is the reason to choose water. Low-impact options
are the default for an obese person's painful knee, not a reason to skip exercise.
 -> [[Aquatic Exercise vs Land-Based Exercise for Balance in Older Adults]]

**Treat the programme as a standing habit.** The evidence of benefit is most certain over at least one to
two years of follow-up. [@pillay2024falls]
After a planned break of 4 to 36 weeks (mean 11), trained older adults kept a functional advantage over
untrained controls (ES 0.88, 0.47-1.29). But balance and gait speed, the measures closest to falls, did
not reach significance (P = 0.051 and 0.056), and older samples kept less.
[@buendiaromero2025] No trial among this guide's
sources measures whether the fall reduction outlasts the programme once it stops. ->
[[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]]

### What does not count as fall prevention

**Walking alone has not been shown to prevent falls, and some trials point the other way.** In the
network meta-analysis no walking intervention reached moderate certainty of benefit. Brief supervised
group walking gave low-certainty evidence of little-to-no difference or some harm, and the authors advise
caution with walking-only programmes for people at elevated fall risk.
[@pillay2024falls]

In one trial of postmenopausal women with a previous forearm fracture, the brisk-walking group had
significantly more falls in the first year, 42 per 100 person-years against 26 in controls (420 against
260 per 1,000 person-years).
[@kunutsor2018adverse]
So for falls, walking has insufficient evidence of benefit and some signals of harm; that is not the
same as showing it has no effect. Walking keeps its place in daily movement for other outcomes; it is not
the lever for falls. -> [[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]]

**Screening everyone's eyesight and correcting it increased falls.** For universal vision assessment and
treatment — screening a general population, not people selected for poor vision — the network
meta-analysis found moderate-certainty evidence that it increased both fallers and fractures. A
comprehensive multifactorial programme (one that assesses and treats several fall risks at once) also
had moderate certainty for more fallers. But its trials enrolled very high-risk people (mean ages 83 and
85, prior fallers, people on fall-risk-increasing drugs).
[@pillay2024falls]

The same review concludes: «Adding other interventions to exercise does not appear to substantially
increase benefits. Overall, effects appear most applicable to those with elevated fall risk.»
[@pillay2024falls]
**Universal vision correction is not a way to prevent falls**; targeted care for a person with a known vision
problem is a separate, clinical decision.

### The frail and prefrail improve in function; whether they fall less is unknown

**In people already identified as frail or prefrail, physical activity improves mobility and can reverse
frailty, but its effect on falls is very uncertain.** A meta-analysis of 26 studies (8,022 adults aged 65
and over) found mobility SMD 0.60 (0.37-0.83), activities of daily living SMD 0.50 (0.15-0.84), quality
of life SMD 0.60 (0.13-1.07) and frailty RR 0.58 (0.36-0.93), moderate certainty; none of this guide's
sources gives an absolute figure. Falls gave RR 0.80 (0.51-1.26), very low certainty, 7 studies.
[@racey2021frailtypa] That interval
is wide enough to include the community-wide effect (a rate ratio of 0.77, a related but different
measure), so the falls effect here is unestablished, not shown absent.

The programmes averaged 29 weeks; the authors warn that standard activity
advice may be too advanced or too intense for a frailer person.
[@racey2021frailtypa] **Start low and
supervise.** Nobody has estimated the resistance-training dose for the frail, so a low start is the
place to begin and build up from -> [[Frailty]]. A systematic review of 10 trials in
pre-frail and frail adults (Kidd and colleagues, 2019), whose abstract WHO's evidence profile
reproduces, names one reason the dose stays open: «A lack of consensus regarding the definition of frailty, and an absence of core measures to assess this
means any attempt to create an optimal intervention will be impeded.»
[@who2020]

### The harms are small; start gradually

**Exercise programmes for falls caused few serious harms.** Across the 108 trials the review found two
serious adverse events (a pelvic stress fracture and a hernia); most reported harms were minor
musculoskeletal complaints, though monitoring was poor.
[@sherrington2019] The network meta-analysis judged most
single-component exercise «probably associated with minimal harm».
[@pillay2024falls]
Two exercise interventions had moderate certainty for small harms, possibly 5 to 8 per 100 people for any
adverse event, with low- to moderate-certainty evidence of little to no harm on serious outcomes.
[@pillay2024falls]

**The cardiac risk of a single exercise session is very low in absolute terms and highest in the habitually inactive.** An AHA
statement puts exercise-related sudden cardiac death at 0.31 to 2.1 per 100,000 person-years. The relative
risk during exertion rises most in sedentary people with known or hidden heart disease doing unaccustomed
strenuous work, and loads that sound modest can still strain an unfit older heart.
[@franklin2020acute] ACSM
consensus advises an inactive person with known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, or with signs or
symptoms of one, to get medical clearance before starting. An inactive person without them can start
light to moderate exercise and progress gradually.
[@riebe2015screen]
-> [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]]

The same programme is also the main way to keep muscle, and muscle is what the weight-loss question
below turns on.

## Train against resistance to keep muscle; add protein as the smaller second step

For obese adults around 70, **resistance training is the lever that keeps muscle working**. Every
weekly volume studied in adults over 60 beat no training on timed up and go. A low-volume programme
matched or beat higher volumes on timed up and go and the 6-minute walk. Fast walking speed was the
exception: it favoured high volume (low certainty, in mostly healthy trial participants with a median BMI
of 26.8).

**Protein is the smaller second step.** Eating at least the amount recommended for older adults is a
cheap default. But extra protein on top of training added nothing measurable in healthy older adults,
and its clearest benefit shows up in people who already have sarcopenia (age-related loss of muscle
and strength). Both levers move strength, function
tests and lean mass. No trial of resistance training alone, or of protein, has yet shown fewer
disabilities, fractures or deaths; the fracture evidence below comes from mixed exercise programmes.


### Weak grip, not a small muscle scan, flags sarcopenia

The European consensus on sarcopenia (EWGSOP2) **diagnoses on strength first**, because «strength is
better than mass in predicting adverse outcomes». Imaging only confirms the diagnosis, and gait speed
grades its severity. After 50, leg muscle mass falls about 1-2% a year while strength falls 1.5-5% a
year, so a mass reading understates the loss. The cut-offs (grip under 27 kg in men and 16 kg in women)
are usually set 2 standard deviations below a healthy young reference; the gait-speed cut-off of 0.8 m/s
or slower is taken from earlier studies. They are
**normative cut-offs (statistical benchmarks), not points where risk was shown to rise**.
[@cruzjentoft2019]
[@cruzjentoft2019]
[@cruzjentoft2019]
-> [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]]

**Sarcopenic obesity has no agreed definition.** EWGSOP2 leaves it outside its scope and sets no
cut-off. One meta-analysis used an operational version: body fat above 30% or BMI above 30, plus low
muscle index, gait speed of 1 m/s or less, or grip of 21 kg or less. The authors cite a global prevalence
of about 11% in older adults. The same authors say the clinical data are still insufficient for a unified
definition, so the definition remains unsettled.
[@poloferrero2025]

### Weakness and low muscle mass predict death; neither is yet a proven target

Low grip predicts mortality at every age studied. In UK Biobank, a cohort aged 40-69, each 5 kg lower
grip carried an adjusted all-cause hazard ratio of 1.16 (1.15-1.17) in men and 1.20 (1.17-1.23) in
women, with no sign of a threshold. Across 48 cohorts aged 35-85, the weakest third had **HR 1.58
(1.40-1.78)** against the strongest third, over a studied grip range of 15-50 kg. The relative link was
moderately stronger in younger people, not weaker. These cohorts give no absolute risk for the group
this guide covers. Because baseline risk is higher at 70, the same relative gap means more deaths in absolute
terms: a difference in starting risk, not in the effect.
[@celismorales2018]
[@lopezbueno2022]
-> [[Grip Strength and Mortality]], [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]

Low muscle mass adds a small, separate signal. In nine cohorts of non-frail adults aged 65 and over
(n = 10,028), people who died had lower appendicular muscle mass than survivors (SMD -0.18, -0.23 to
-0.12). **The gap was larger in cohorts with a higher average BMI**, a between-study pattern that fits
the sarcopenic-obesity idea without proving it
[@santana2021]. Grip is about half heritable, and the
weakest people carried more illness at baseline, so grip is partly a marker of health
[@celismorales2018].
Whether building strength or mass to change the reading would change the risk is a separate question,
still untested.
-> [[Low Muscle Mass and Mortality]]

### Any resistance training beats none on timed up and go and 1RM strength; a low weekly volume matched higher volumes on two of three function tests

One network meta-analysis tackles the dose question for this age group directly: 151 randomised trials
(n = 6,306) of untrained adults over 60, median age 68.8 and 78.8% physically healthy, and median BMI
26.8, so overweight rather than obese. Every volume band beat no training on timed up and go, lean mass
and every outcome measured as 1RM strength (the heaviest weight a person can lift once). **The low band, 5 to fewer than 18 lower-limb sets a week (median 12), did as well as
or better than the high band** (27 or more, median 36) on timed up and go and the 6-minute walk. On fast
walking speed it did not beat no training (SMD -0.21, -0.57 to 0.14), and the high band beat it head to
head (-0.61, -1.08 to -0.15). Low versus high against control, as SMDs:

- **Timed up and go** (lower is better): -1.20 (-1.57 to -0.82) vs -0.63 (-0.99 to -0.26); low beat
  high head to head, -0.57 (-1.09 to -0.04).
- **6-minute walk:** 1.03 (0.33 to 1.73) vs 0.27 (-0.40 to 0.94), the high band not separating from
  control.
- **Lean mass:** 0.25 (0.10 to 0.40) vs 0.18 (0.06 to 0.30), no separation head to head; the authors
  report median gains of 0.6 to 0.8 kg.
- **Knee-extension 1RM:** 0.85 (0.55 to 1.14) vs 1.18 (0.86 to 1.49), head to head unresolved (-0.33,
  -0.73 to 0.07).

[@radaelli2024rtvol]

The authors' summary: «Except for muscle strength, prescribing a high resistance training volume did
not accrue additional benefits for the outcomes examined.» Certainty is **low for function and lean
mass, very low for strength**. Most trials carried a high risk of bias, and the volume comparisons lean
largely on indirect contrasts (made through a shared comparison group, not head to head). The authors translate the low band as **two sessions a week of two sets on each of three
lower-limb exercises**, under 20 minutes, at a typical load near 80% of one-repetition maximum.
[@radaelli2024rtvol]
-> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]

Read the low band as a minimum that worked, not as the point past which more training stops helping.
**The trials studied nothing below about 5 lower-limb sets a week**, and averages for three volume bands
(terciles) cannot show where the benefit levels off. Nothing shows that higher volume harms. It beat low volume on fast walking speed and ranked higher on strength (no head-to-head
strength difference was significant, very low certainty), with no timed-up-and-go or 6-minute-walk gain
[@radaelli2024rtvol]. That fits the 2011 ACSM position stand, which says a single set
«can be effective especially among older and novice exercisers».
[@garber2011acsm]
[inferred from @radaelli2024rtvol]

### With sarcopenic obesity, training trims fat and improves function more than it builds muscle

The meta-analysis that matches this group best pooled 9 RCTs (n = 513, mean age 68, mostly women) of resistance
training three times a week for about 12 weeks, with no diet in either arm. Training improved a pooled
body-composition score modestly (SMD 0.35, 0.12 to 0.57, moderate certainty). **Lower body fat carried
that gain (SMD 0.52, 0.19 to 0.86)**; the muscle-mass subgroups did not reach significance. Physical
performance improved by a similar small amount (SMD 0.36, 0.03 to 0.69, low certainty), and blood lipids
and CRP (a blood marker of inflammation) did not change. Every endpoint is a surrogate (a measurement
that stands in for a health outcome), and the trials were short and small.
[@poloferrero2025]
-> [[Resistance Training for Sarcopenic Obesity]]

### Start low if frail or load-intolerant; heavy loads are not the only route

**The right dose for frail or sarcopenic adults is unestimated.** Only about 13% of the network's trials
enrolled physically impaired adults
, and the authors write that «the most effective resistance training
dosage for this group remains to be determined». The evidence base of the ACSM 2026 position stand
defines healthy adults as excluding obesity, sarcopenia and frailty. So for an obese or frail novice, its
advice is carried over rather than studied. Starting light and adding load as sets get easier is
standard practice, not trial-tested. A low start is where to begin, not a limit to stay under.
[@radaelli2024rtvol]
[@acsm2026rt]

For someone whose knees or joints cannot take heavy loads, **light loads with a blood-flow-restriction
cuff matched heavy loads on muscle mass** in older adults (ES 0.21, -0.14 to 0.56). The cuff partly
restricts blood flow to the working muscle. On strength, it
fell short of heavy loads (ES -0.42, -0.70 to -0.14). The evidence is 11 small trials (N = 238). The
evidence on its safety in this age group is insufficient, and the review advises checking each person's
disease history before starting.
[@centner2019bfr]
-> [[Blood Flow Restriction Training]]

Resistance training alone has not been shown to prevent falls ->
[[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]. Self-reported strength activity tracks lower
mortality (RR 0.85, 0.79 to 0.93, very low certainty, observational). For people in this group,
better function is the stronger reason to train.
[@momma2022]
-> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]

### Protein: reach the older-adult minimum; supplements add little on top of training unless muscle is already low

The PROT-AGE consensus sets **1.0-1.2 g protein per kg body weight a day for healthy adults over 65**,
1.2 for the active and 1.2-1.5 during illness. It treats the general-adult RDA (recommended dietary
allowance) of 0.8 g/kg as a minimum for avoiding deficiency, too low for this age. Its per-meal
threshold is about 25-30 g of protein (2.5-2.8 g leucine, a protein building block), set high because
ageing muscle needs a bigger stimulus per meal. PROT-AGE is expert consensus based on surrogate
measures, funded by a Nestlé grant, not an outcome trial.
[@bauer2013] -> [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]],
[[Anabolic Resistance]]

Spreading protein evenly across meals is a cheap default, not a proven optimum: the two older-adult
comparisons Bauer tabulates both favoured a large midday meal. **PROT-AGE also states its range per kg of
body weight without saying which kilogram to use for an obese person**
[@bauer2013]. PROT-AGE mentions ideal body weight only
for critically ill underweight or obese patients (an ESPEN proposal) and for its kidney-disease targets
[@bauer2013]. So for a healthy obese older adult,
which weight to count the grams against remains an open question.

The \~1.6 g/kg figure often quoted is a soft, non-significant break point (where the fitted curve stops
rising; 95% CI 1.03-2.20, p = 0.079),
fit on total body weight across 42 trial arms of healthy, non-dieting adults, young and old.
In its over-45 subgroup (mean age 67), protein supplements added no measurable fat-free mass (MD 0.06,
-0.14 to 0.26), though those trials gave smaller supplement doses (about 20 g against 42 g a day in the
younger trials). Obesity was not reported, and dieting adults were excluded
[@morton2018],
so the figure does not carry over to obese older adults, dieting or not. Where it does apply, it is a
rough minimum rather than an upper limit, since its interval runs up to 2.20 g/kg.

Three meta-analyses of supplements in older adults, two of them on supplements added to resistance training, found:

- **Healthy older adults who train:** across 22 RCTs, adding a nutritional supplement to resistance
  training made «no significant differences between groups in muscle mass, muscle strength, or physical
  functional performance». Creatine was the one exception, adding lean mass (MD 2.61 kg, 0.51 to 4.72)
  in only 4 trials.
- **Older adults, healthy or sarcopenic (16 trials, 999 people):** leucine supplements raised lean mass
  by 0.99 kg (0.43 to 1.55) overall. The effect was significant only in the sarcopenic subgroup (1.14
  kg, 0.55 to 1.74); the healthy-subgroup estimate was imprecise (-0.05, -1.55 to 1.46). Grip and knee strength did not move
  measurably, so the evidence on strength is insufficient.
- **Sarcopenia, any supplement with training (12 trials, 713 people):** grip rose by a borderline WMD
  1.87 kg (0.01 to 3.74, P = 0.049), with no gain in muscle mass. The gain came from supplements that
  included protein and vitamin D, which also raised gait speed.

[@choi2021]
[@komar2015]
[@song2023]
-> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]], [[Creatine Supplementation]]

### Check the kidneys before raising protein; testosterone appears only as a comparison

**How far to raise protein depends on kidney function.** With healthy kidneys, higher protein showed no
meaningful harm to kidney function in the pooled trials, which included obese adults. But those
participants were aged 23 to 72 (mean 49), younger than the group this guide covers. PROT-AGE grades its
caution by the stage of chronic kidney disease, with targets per kg of ideal body weight. With eGFR (a
blood-test estimate of how well the kidneys filter) 30-60, intake above
0.8 g/kg is considered safe, with GFR checked twice a year. With eGFR below 30 and no dialysis, intake
may need to be limited to 0.8 g/kg. A clinician should do the testing and set the number.
[@bauer2013]
[@devries2018]
-> [[Protein Intake and Kidney Function]]

Some muscle creatine breaks down into creatinine
[@kreider2017], and researchers
conclude there is «no compelling evidence that creatine supplementation negatively affects renal function
in healthy or clinical populations»; some advise people with existing kidney disease to check with a
physician first, «in an abundance of caution» [@kreider2017].
Kidney function is commonly estimated from blood creatinine
[@devries2018], so a creatine user
should mention it before a kidney blood test.
-> [[Creatine Supplementation]]

Testosterone therapy appears here only as a drug comparison, not a lever this guide recommends (its
effect on lean mass is on [[Testosterone Adiposity and Muscle]]). In the TRAVERSE trial of hypogonadal
men (men with low testosterone) aged 45-80, clinical fractures occurred
in 3.50% on testosterone versus 2.46% on placebo over about 3.2 years. That is roughly **35 versus 25
per 1,000 men (HR 1.43, 1.04 to 1.97)**: a signal, not a settled harm.
[@snyder2024]

The trial found no rise in major adverse cardiovascular events, but testosterone was associated with
more atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism and acute kidney injury
[@snyder2024]. The exercise programmes in the
falls trials above went the other way, with fewer fall-related fractures (RR 0.73, 0.56-0.95, low
certainty) [@sherrington2019]; the two populations
differ, so the contrast is indicative only.
-> [[Testosterone Adiposity and Muscle]]

Training sessions build muscle. How much you move the rest of the day is a separate matter, and its
benefit also levels off.

## Move through the day; the step benefit levels off sooner at this age

People who move more through the day die at lower rates, and **movement of any intensity counts, light
activity included**. Eight cohorts that measured movement with accelerometers (36,383 adults aged 40 or
over, mean age 62.6, median follow-up 5.8 years) found mortality fell steeply out of the least active
quarter and then flattened. Against that quarter, the hazard ratio for total activity was 0.48 (95% CI
0.43-0.54) in the second quarter, 0.34 (0.26-0.45) in the third and 0.27 (0.23-0.32) in the fourth. **The
largest drop came with the first move up out of the least active quarter**, «for all activity intensities»: light activity
alone reached HR 0.60 (0.54-0.68) in its second quarter
[@ekelundacc2019]
-> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

The light-activity curve reached its largest reduction near 375 minutes a day
[@ekelundacc2019]. The
moderate-to-vigorous curve flattened within a broad zone of roughly 20-40 minutes
-> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]. Beyond those levels, the authors write, «No further risk reductions occurred with higher levels of activity except for low
light intensity physical activity where the risk appeared to decrease further»
[@ekelundacc2019]. Measured by the clock, most of
the movement behind these curves is therefore light activity, counted in hours rather than minutes
.

**These are comparisons between people, not trial results.** Sick people move less;
excluding deaths in the first two years left the hazard ratios materially unchanged, yet the authors
write that «bias from reverse causation might persist»
[@ekelundacc2019]. No trial among this
guide's sources tests daily movement against mortality, and none of the sources turns these hazard
ratios into an adjusted count of deaths avoided per 1,000 people at 70.

### After 60, roughly 6,000-8,000 steps a day captures most of the benefit

Steps are the unit most people can track. Fifteen cohorts (47,471 adults) with quarter medians of 3,553,
5,801, 7,842 and 10,901 steps a day gave hazard ratios of 0.60 (0.51-0.71), 0.55 (0.49-0.62) and 0.47
(0.39-0.57) against the lowest quarter. The curve flattened earlier with age: «Restricted cubic splines
showed progressively decreasing risk of mortality among adults aged 60 years and older with increasing
number of steps per day until 6000–8000 steps per day and among adults younger than 60 years until
8000–10 000 steps per day.» The study tested this age difference within the same analysis, and it was
significant (p=0.012) [@paluch2022]. **Age moves where the curve levels
off, not whether moving helps**, so a lower step goal for an older adult rests on a direct test rather
than a guess.

Read 6,000-8,000 as the region where the curve levels off, in cohorts whose quarter medians ran from about
3,500 to 11,000 steps a day, not as a precise number. The familiar 10,000 has no such basis: it is
«not based on evidence, but instead originates from a marketing campaign in Japan»
[@paluch2022]. **For an older adult already above about
8,000 steps a day, more steps buy little extra on this curve**; below 6,000, each added thousand sits on
the part of the curve that is still falling -> [[The Underivable Optimum]].

Two things suggest that adding steps yourself would deliver less than these curves imply. First, the
association was stronger in studies with under 6 years of follow-up (highest vs lowest quarter HR 0.32,
0.25-0.41) than in those with 6 years or more (0.57, 0.49-0.66). Excluding early deaths weakened it,
though it stayed significant [@paluch2022]. That is the
pattern expected if some low steppers were already ill.

Second, trials that set out to raise step counts produced only small gains. **App and tracker trials raise steps by only about
600-750 a day over control, for a few months**, at low to low-to-moderate certainty, and the trials have
not shown that the gain lasts -> [[Wearable Activity Trackers and Physical Activity]]. One pooled estimate was 627 steps a day
(417-862) [@brickwood2019wearable].
That is a fifth to a third of the gap between neighbouring quarters. And the quarter hazard ratios cannot
be read as the effect of adding steps to one person.

Walking counts toward these steps and toward survival. It has not been shown to prevent falls; that job
belongs to the balance-and-strength programme -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].

### Sitting is a separate exposure, and its harm speeds up past a threshold

Activity's benefit flattens; sitting's harm does the opposite. Across 34 prospective studies (1,331,468
participants), each extra hour of total sitting, adjusted for physical activity, carried an all-cause
mortality RR of 1.01 (1.00-1.01) below about 8 hours a day and 1.04 (1.03-1.05) above it
[@patterson2018sedentary].
**Treat about 8 hours as an approximate region, not a target.** It is a change point read off a fitted
curve with no interval of its own, and almost all the sitting data were self-reported
-> [[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]]. On accelerometers, risk rose gradually from about
7.5-9 hours a day and more steeply above 9.5 hours [@ekelundacc2019].

Sitting and moving barely correlate, so meeting a step count does not by itself cancel a long sitting
day -> [[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]]. In accelerometer data from more than 44,000
middle-aged and older adults, **about 30-40 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity (median 34)
attenuated the excess risk of heavy sitting**; the excess was concentrated in the less active
[@ekelund2020joint]. Both findings are
observational, and the sitting association weakened slightly once early deaths were excluded
[@ekelundacc2019].

### Fitness predicts survival at 70, but it is partly a marker

Cardiorespiratory fitness tracks mortality closely. Each 1-MET higher fitness level (a MET is a unit of
exercise intensity, set by the energy used sitting at rest) carried an all-cause RR of 0.87 (0.84-0.90)
in a cohort meta-analysis
[@kodama2009]. In a cohort of patients
referred for treadmill testing, those aged 70 or older in the elite fitness band (top 2.3% for age and sex) had lower
mortality than those in the band below, HR 0.71 (0.52-0.98) in a subgroup analysis
[@mandsager2018]. **Low
fitness is partly a sign of illness and partly inherited**, so raising your own fitness should buy less
than the gap between people suggests; no trial among this guide's sources measures how much less
-> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]].

Training does raise fitness at this age. A meta-analysis of 41 trials in healthy adults with mean age 60
or over found a net gain of 3.8 mL/kg/min (16.3%) over controls
[@ross2016], about 1 MET
. **Use fitness to check that the movement is working, not as a separate target**
-> [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]].

For someone with major depression, exercise also treats it. Across 218 trials, walking or jogging
reduced symptoms beyond active control by g -0.63 (-0.80 to -0.46), with confidence rated low for walking
or jogging and very low for other exercise
[@noetel2024exercise]. Those trials were not
limited to older adults, and the lever is the same movement already ranked, not a new one
-> [[Depression and Modifiable Exposures]].

Daily movement is also one of the two lifestyle routes to lower blood pressure and blood sugar, the
subject of the next section.

## Keep blood pressure and blood sugar down; at this age, starting a statin or aspirin does less good

### Lowering blood pressure cuts major cardiovascular events by about the same relative amount from 55 to 84

**Lowering blood pressure reduces heart attacks, strokes and heart failure whatever the starting
pressure.** The Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists pooled 48 trials and 344,716 people. Each 5
mmHg fall in systolic pressure cut major cardiovascular events by about a tenth: hazard ratio 0.91
(0.89-0.94) in people without prior heart disease and 0.89 (0.86-0.92) in those with it. The authors
found «no reliable evidence of heterogeneity of treatment effects» across starting systolic pressures
from below 120 to 170 mmHg and above
[@bplttc2021]. Of the
heart-and-metabolism levers this guide covers, lowering blood pressure is the one with a proven benefit
on hard outcomes (actual heart attacks, strokes and deaths, not just a better reading) in people without
heart disease ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]).

**The relative effect stays fixed, so the absolute benefit grows with baseline risk.** In trial
participants without prior heart disease, major events ran at 31.9 per 1,000 person-years in the
comparison group and 25.9 on treatment
[@bplttc2021]. Baseline
risk climbs with age, so an obese adult around 70 with high blood pressure usually gains more from the
same reduction than a younger adult does ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]). The
trialists' rule follows: decide on overall cardiovascular risk, not on the blood-pressure number alone
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

**From 55 to 84 the relative benefit stays level; past 85 the trials cannot say.** Rahimi and
colleagues re-cut the same collaboration's data by age: 51 trials, 358,707 people. Per 5 mmHg fall, the
hazard ratio for major events was 0.82 (0.76-0.88) under 55, then 0.91 in every band from 55 to 84,
including 0.91 (0.88-0.95) at 65-74 and 0.91 (0.87-0.96) at 75-84; at 85 and over it was 0.99
(0.87-1.12) [@rahimi2021bpage]. The 85-plus result rests on about 1,000 events over a
median 2.8 years [@rahimi2021bpage]. The absolute cut in major events was larger in
older groups (p for interaction 0.024) [@rahimi2021bpage], though the authors call their
absolute figures «only useful for internal comparisons of effect sizes across strata»
[@rahimi2021bpage].

No band showed evidence that the effect varied with starting systolic pressure
[@rahimi2021bpage]. Only 121 people aged 85 and over started below 120 mmHg
[@rahimi2021bpage].
At that age, then, the check is imprecise.

For major cardiovascular events, the 85-plus interval runs from a benefit as large as the younger bands'
to possible harm. On its own, the 85-plus band is insufficient evidence on events: it shows neither
benefit nor its absence. The test for a difference by age was borderline (p=0.050). The authors say a chance
finding cannot be ruled out, and also that interaction tests are conservative and large epidemiological studies
show the same fading with age [@rahimi2021bpage]. So this guide carries the 0.91 past
85 with wider uncertainty rather than treating age as a proven change in the effect. ->
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]

**Total deaths, non-fatal harms and frail people are less certain.** The authors found the relative cut
in all-cause death «marginally larger in younger than in older age, with no obvious effect in people
older than 75 years», and caution that more non-cardiovascular deaths would dilute that effect. They
conclude that «the lack of excess mortality risk in older groups suggests that harmful fatal effects of
the treatment are unlikely in any age group» [@rahimi2021bpage]. They do not pool falls or
fainting by age [searched: adverse / syncope / falls across chunks 01-02]. People with multimorbidity and
polypharmacy, frail people and care-home residents were under-represented, and the findings'
generalisability to them «remains uncertain». Citing «the absence of any strong evidence for excess
harms from randomised studies», the authors judge continuing a well-tolerated drug appropriate while preventing heart attacks and strokes still matters to the person
[@rahimi2021bpage].
For a frail person, starting is an individual trade-off made with a clinician ([[Frailty]]).

**Pushing pressure lower prevents more events but brings more side-effects.** SPRINT pushed systolic
pressure to about 121 versus 136 mmHg in 9,361 high-risk adults without diabetes (mean age 68; 28% aged
75 or over). All-cause death fell (hazard ratio 0.73, 0.60-0.90) in a trial stopped early, over a median
3.26 years. Over that time, for every 61 people treated to the lower target, one primary-outcome event
(heart attack, other acute coronary syndrome, stroke, heart failure or cardiovascular death) was
prevented, and for every 90, one death. The authors add: «These benefits with re- spect to both the
primary outcome and death were consistent across all prespecified subgroups, including participants 75
years of age or older.» Serious hypotension (blood pressure too low), fainting, electrolyte problems and
acute kidney injury rose, «but not of injurious falls»
[@sprint2015].

The brain section below covers what blood-pressure lowering does for dementia, including the preDIVA
trial. **How low to go for one person is a prescriber's decision**, made with their other drugs and their
fall risk in view. This guide sets no target.

### A potassium-enriched salt substitute cut strokes and deaths; its safety is unclear in several common conditions

**A potassium-enriched salt substitute cut strokes and deaths in a trial of older, mostly post-stroke villagers who salted food at home.** SSaSS
randomised 600 villages in rural China, covering 20,995 people who had had a stroke, or were 60 or older
with poorly controlled blood pressure (systolic 140 mmHg or more on treatment, 160 or more untreated;
mean age 65; 72.6% with a prior stroke). Households used a substitute of 75% sodium chloride and 25%
potassium chloride, or regular salt, for a mean 4.74 years. Stroke ran at 29.14 versus 33.65 per 1,000
person-years (rate ratio 0.86, 0.77-0.96), major cardiovascular events at 49.09 versus 56.29 (0.87,
0.80-0.94), and death at 39.28 versus 44.61 (0.88, 0.82-0.95)
[@neal2021].

Those rates come to roughly 21 fewer
strokes and 25 fewer deaths per 1,000 people over the 4.74 years (rate difference times mean follow-up,
an approximation). Those figures come from people at high risk, most after a stroke; someone at
intermediate risk would expect fewer (Lai's modelled figure, below).

**The substitute moved two things at once, so sodium alone cannot take the credit.** Urinary potassium
rose by 20.6 mmol a day while sodium fell by 15.2 mmol, and systolic pressure fell 3.34 mmHg
[@neal2021]. In the drug trials, each 5
mmHg fall cut strokes by about 13%
[@bplttc2021], which
predicts a stroke rate ratio near 0.91 for SSaSS. That lies inside the trial's own interval (0.77-0.96),
so SSaSS does not show a stroke benefit beyond its blood-pressure fall. The trial's authors
read the stroke effect as «in ac- cordance with a mechanism of action mediated through blood-pressure reduction»
[@neal2021]. Since potassium rose as sodium
fell, the trial cannot split that fall between them ([[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]).

**The pooled reviews add blood-pressure trials, but their all-cause death figures rest mostly on
SSaSS.** Lai's network meta-analysis of 34 trials found that substitutes with 25-40% potassium chloride lowered systolic pressure by 4.64 mmHg (3.24-6.05; 18 trials,
moderate certainty). Applied to modelled baselines, they probably prevent 7 deaths per 1,000 people over
5 years at intermediate cardiovascular risk (5-10% over 5 years; 3 to 11 fewer) and 16 at high risk
(20-30%; 7 to 24 fewer), graded moderate to high certainty
[@lai2026salt].

SSaSS supplies about 87% of the people in that mortality pool (20,995 of 24,106). Without
SSaSS, the all-cause benefit lost significance, though the point estimates still showed 4 and 10 fewer
deaths per 1,000, and cardiovascular deaths stayed significantly lower, with 9 and 29 fewer per 1,000 over
5 years at intermediate and high risk. No trial outside China measured mortality
[@lai2026salt]. Lai judges that this evidence transfers poorly beyond
Chinese dietary patterns with «high discretionary salt use and home food preparation», and found no
significant difference between formulations, so a higher-potassium blend has not been shown to add benefit
[@lai2026salt].

Brand's Cochrane review (26 trials) found systolic pressure 4.76 mmHg lower (3.50-6.01) and
cardiovascular death at a rate ratio of 0.77 (0.60-1.00), both moderate certainty
[@brand2022lsss];
converted from Brand's per-100,000 figures, that is about 2 fewer cardiovascular deaths per 1,000
person-years (0 to 3 fewer). But 25 of its 26 trials are also in Lai, so this is the same
evidence counted twice, not a second confirmation. Brand marked the hard outcomes down because
SSaSS, a secondary-prevention trial, drives them and applies less directly to the general population
[@brand2022lsss].

Older people's pressure may also respond more to the sodium half: across 133 sodium-reduction trials, the
same cut in urinary sodium lowered systolic pressure more in older people
[@huang2020]. That comparison is between
trials, not within one, so it is a lead for this age group, not a measured effect.

**The trial's safety result does not reach several conditions common at this age.** High blood potassium
(hyperkalaemia), counting definite, probable and possible events, was not significantly more common,
overall (3.35 versus 3.30 per 1,000 person-years; rate ratio 1.04, 0.80-1.37) or «in any participant
subgroup» [@neal2021]. Only two events were definite or probable; 302 of the
others were deaths classed as possible hyperkalaemia
[@neal2021].
The trial's hyperkalaemia count is therefore loose. But SSaSS excluded known serious kidney
disease, potassium-sparing diuretics and potassium supplements, screened by history alone, and did not
measure blood potassium routinely
[@neal2021]. Lai calls the safety evidence in kidney
disease and on potassium-sparing drugs «insufficient», and adds that «patients with type 1 or 2 diabetes
may also be at risk of hyperkalaemia»
[@lai2026salt].

People
with diabetes were enrolled in at least seven trials by this guide's count of Brand's trial tables
, 10.6% of SSaSS's substitute arm [@brand2022lsss], although Brand's discussion
states that all its trials excluded them [@brand2022lsss]. No diabetes-specific safety
estimate is pooled and SSaSS's subgroup results are in a supplement not held here. The same goes for
ACE-inhibitor or ARB users, 22.8% of SSaSS, with no potassium monitoring
[@neal2021]. For both groups the evidence
is insufficient, not shown safe.

Brand pooled hyperkalaemia separately, RR 1.04 (0.46-2.38)
[@brand2022lsss], and concludes that substitutes «likely result
in little to no difference in hyperkalaemia», while noting that the interval «included important
benefit and important harm» [@brand2022lsss]. The estimate rests on about 20 events by this
guide's count, and Brand notes that most of the information comes from a study at high risk of
bias, a trial run in nursing homes [@brand2022lsss]. This guide
reads it more cautiously than Brand does: an interval that wide does not show the absence of harm in
the conditions the trials left out. No trial reported low blood sodium (hyponatraemia). Brand
judges it unlikely overall while salt intakes stay high, but flags the individual risk «particularly in older people and those using thiazide diuretics»
[@brand2022lsss]. Older age is itself on that list, so at this age the switch is worth
mentioning at the next clinic visit even without a thiazide.

**Anyone in the household with reduced kidney function or diabetes, or taking an ACE inhibitor, ARB,
potassium-sparing diuretic, potassium supplement or thiazide, needs a clinician's check before
switching**, and anyone this age should mention the switch at the next visit; a referral, not a dosing rule ([[Chronic Kidney Disease and Modifiable Exposures]]).
The thiazide entry is this guide's addition, from Brand's hyponatraemia warning. For someone who clears
the check, the tested form is a blend of about a quarter potassium chloride, used in place of salt at the
stove and the table; it helps only where salt is added at home.

### Exercise probably lowers high blood pressure about as much as a first-line drug, in trials of younger adults

**In people whose pressure is high, structured exercise probably lowers it about as much as a
first-line drug, in trials of people around 50.** No trial has compared the two head to head. Naci's
network meta-analysis set 197 exercise trials against 194 drug trials. In people starting at 140 mmHg or above, exercise lowered
systolic pressure by 8.96 mmHg (credible interval -10.27 to -7.64), and the gap to drugs was 0.18 mmHg
(-1.35 to 1.68): no difference [@naci2019exercise].
Participants in the exercise trials averaged roughly 48-54 years, and only 56 of those trials (3,508
people) enrolled people with hypertension [@naci2019exercise];
whether the match holds at 70 is untested. The exercise trials were smaller and unblinded,
and none counted heart attacks or strokes.

So **exercise is a credible partner to a drug, or a stand-in while a drug is not yet called for**.
But nobody has shown that its pressure drop prevents events at the rate a drug's does
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Because blood-pressure drugs have a proven
benefit on cardiovascular events, chasing the last few mmHg through salt alone matters less. Exercise,
unlike a blood-pressure drug, also moves fitness, glucose and weight. Which route to take is the
reader's choice, made with a clinician.

### A structured lifestyle programme prevents diabetes at least as well in older adults; metformin did little

**A structured lifestyle programme prevents type 2 diabetes in people over 60 at least as well as in
younger adults.** The Diabetes Prevention Program randomised 3,234 adults with raised glucose (mean age
51, mean BMI 34) to placebo, metformin, or a lifestyle programme aiming for at least 7% weight loss and
150 minutes of activity a week. Lifestyle cut diabetes incidence by 58% (48-66). Among the 648
participants aged 60 or older, incidence was 10.8 cases per 100 person-years on placebo and 3.1 on
lifestyle, a 71% reduction (51-83) [@knowler2002].
That is about 77 fewer cases per 1,000 person-years.

The authors write that «The intensive lifestyle intervention was at least as ef- fective in older
participants as it was in younger par- ticipants.»
[@knowler2002] The trial found no significant
difference by age in the effect of lifestyle against placebo (no significant age interaction), so the
larger figure at 60+ is not proof of a stronger effect ([[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]).

**Metformin, the drug alternative, did little at this age.** In the over-60s metformin cut incidence by
11% (-33 to 41), against 44% (21-60) in 25-44-year-olds. The authors report that the advantage of
lifestyle «over metformin was greater in older persons»
[@knowler2002]. **For an obese adult around 70 with
raised glucose, the drug does not substitute for the programme.**

**The programme's benefit on diabetes has not been shown to extend to heart attacks and strokes.** Over 21 years of
follow-up in the same middle-aged cohort, neither lifestyle (hazard ratio 1.14, 0.87-1.50) nor metformin
(1.03, 0.78-1.37) reduced major cardiovascular events
[@goldberg2022]. The authors note that offering
lifestyle to all arms, wide out-of-study use of statins and blood-pressure drugs, and metformin crossover
«may have diluted the effects of the interventions»
[@goldberg2022], so this is a diluted comparison, not
a clean test that found no effect.

**No trial has tested at this age whether total diet replacement (formula meals in place of all food,
for a period) puts established diabetes into remission.** DiRECT
recruited people aged 20 to 65 within six years of diagnosis; 46% of the programme group were in
remission at one year against 4% of controls
[@lean2018], and 36% (53 of 149) against 3.4% (5 of 149) at two years
[@lean2019]. For a 70-year-old with diabetes that result
applies only by assumption ([[Total Diet Replacement and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]]).

**A Mediterranean diet reduced cardiovascular events in a trial of people aged 55 to 80.** PREDIMED gave
7,447 Spanish adults at high cardiovascular risk a Mediterranean diet with extra olive oil or nuts, or
advice to cut fat. Over about five years the composite of heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death
fell from 5.7% to 3.8% (hazard ratio 0.70, 0.55-0.89)
[@estruch2018], an absolute difference of 1.7 to 2.1
percentage points, or 17 to 21 fewer per 1,000 people.
Stroke accounted for most of it, and all-cause death did not change (0.98, 0.77-1.24)
[@estruch2018]
([[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]]).

### Statins and aspirin: starting is a different decision from continuing

**Starting a statin after 75 without known heart disease is unproven either way.** The Cholesterol
Treatment Trialists' analysis of 28 trials (186,854 people, 14,483 of them over 75 at entry) split older
people by their history of vascular disease. With vascular disease, the relative benefit persisted over 75: rate ratio 0.85 (99% CI
0.73-0.98) per 1 mmol/L of LDL lowering. Without it, the over-75 estimate was 0.92 (0.73-1.16; 99% CIs throughout), on 295
versus 308 events (2.7% versus 2.8% a year). Even at 71-75 the estimate (0.84, 0.70-1.01) did not reach
significance, and benefit tended to shrink with age (trend p=0.05)
[@ctt2019]. That interval runs from a 27% reduction to
a 16% increase: **insufficient evidence, not proof of no effect**
([[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]).

**The two guidance bodies lean in different directions, both at their weakest grade.** USPSTF finds the evidence
insufficient for starting a statin at 76 or older [@uspstf2022].
ACC-AHA 2026 judges starting a moderate-intensity statin possibly reasonable for adults over 75 with a
life expectancy of at least 2.5 years, after a discussion of benefits and risks
[@accaha2026]. European guidance sits at the same weak
grade: the ESC rates starting a statin for primary prevention at 70 or over as Class IIb, saying it «may
be considered, if at high risk or above»
[@esc2021]. With prior vascular disease the case for a
statin holds at any age ([[LDL Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Continuing a statin already taken
is a separate decision this guide does not grade.

**Two common worries about statins, muscle pain and diabetes, are weaker than they look.** Across 19 blinded trials, muscle pain
or weakness was reported by 27.1% on a statin and 26.6% on placebo (rate ratio 1.03, 1.01-1.06). The
excess, about 11 extra reports per 1,000 person-years, was largely confined to the first year
[@reith2022muscle], so **most aches on a statin are not caused by
it** ([[Statin Muscle Symptoms and the Nocebo Effect]]).

**Statins do raise new diagnoses of diabetes, mostly in people already close to the line:** rate
ratio 1.10 (1.04-1.16) on low- or moderate-intensity regimens, about 1.2 extra per 1,000 person-years.
About 62% of new cases arose in people whose glucose was already in the top quarter
[@reith2024diabetes]. For an obese adult around 70 with raised
glucose, a statin may bring that diagnosis forward; the trialists judge the cost outweighed by fewer
vascular events ([[Statins and New-Onset Diabetes]]).

**Starting daily aspirin for prevention gives no net benefit at this age.** Across 13 trials and
164,225 people without heart disease (median age 62), aspirin cut the composite of cardiovascular death,
heart attack and stroke by 0.41 percentage points (0.23-0.59). It raised major bleeding by 0.47 points
(0.34-0.62), and did not change all-cause death (hazard ratio 0.94, 0.88-1.01)
[@zheng2019aspirin]. That is about 4 fewer
events and 5 more bleeds per 1,000 people over trial follow-up (about 6.4 years on average).

**Bleeding risk climbs with age while the relative benefit does not.** USPSTF therefore concludes, with
moderate certainty, that starting aspirin at 60 or older has no net benefit, and recommends against it;
its modelling shows a loss of life-years at essentially every risk level modelled for people aged 70 to
79 [@uspstf2022aspirin]. **Aspirin offers some
40-59-year-olds a small net benefit but comes to do more harm than good with age**. Rising bleeding risk
drives the change, not a measured change in its relative effect
([[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]).

**Most trials that would measure these levers directly at 70 are not among this guide's sources.** The
ESC recommends its older-adult risk tool SCORE2-OP for people aged 70 and over
[@esc2021]. But that tool's derivation and the elderly
blood-pressure and statin trials HYVET and PROSPER are not among this guide's sources. For blood pressure,
Rahimi's pooled analysis by age reports results at 65-74 and 75-84 directly
[@rahimi2021bpage]; the
guide otherwise relies on the CTT over-75 split, SPRINT, preDIVA and SSaSS ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]])
. Among weight-loss drugs, the semaglutide trial of cardiovascular events in this guide's
sources (SELECT) enrolled only people with established cardiovascular disease. In obese people without
it, the effect on events is untested, not shown absent ([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]).
Choosing a drug, its dose, a blood-pressure or glucose target, and whether to stop a medicine already
taken are prescriber decisions outside this guide.

Weight loss is the lever most people expect here; its payoff at 70 depends on what it is for and what it
costs in muscle.

## Lose weight for a named target, and protect muscle while you do it

For obese adults around 70, intentional weight loss is **not a default; it is worth doing for a named
target** (painful knees, rising blood sugar, a fatty liver). Pair it with resistance training and enough
protein, and track it by function rather than on the scale. No trial among this guide's sources tests
whether losing weight at this age extends life. The randomized trials that showed a survival benefit
enrolled mostly middle-aged adults. The cost is muscle; resistance training reduced it in 12-24-week
trials, and higher protein in trials of up to two years. **The idea that weight loss harms frail or
sarcopenic people rests on mechanism (how the body works), not on a finding**: no trial among this
guide's sources measured it. from [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]],
[[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]] and [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]].

### Excess weight still costs survival at 70, but less per unit of BMI

In the Global BMI Mortality Collaboration's pooled cohorts, each 5 kg/m2 above a BMI of 25 raised the
hazard of death by **HR 1.21 (1.17-1.25) at ages 70-89**, against 1.52 (1.47-1.56) at ages 35-49
[@globalbmi2016]. The slope flattens with age but **stays
harmful**; in never-smokers the lowest-risk bands spanned a BMI of 20-25, with 22.5-25 the nominal lowest-risk point
[@globalbmi2016].

Above age 60, waist size (per
10 cm) no longer had a significant link with death in a subgroup analysis of a dose-response
meta-analysis: HR 1.03 (0.98-1.08), 14 studies, I2 = 81%
[@jayedi2020central]. None of this
guide's sources converts the 70-89 slope into deaths per 1,000. Because death rates are higher at this
age, **the absolute excess may not shrink** as much as the relative one does.

The apparent survival advantage of being overweight in later life, often called the obesity paradox,
is a **suspected artifact, not a finding**. In the same data the overweight band moved from HR 0.96
(0.95-0.97) in all participants to 1.11 (1.10-1.11) once smokers, early deaths and people with prior
disease were set aside [@globalbmi2016]. Illness that
causes weight loss before death makes thin people look riskier and heavier people look protected; see
[[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

That correction was not run separately in people over 80 or in the frail, so **for them the question
stays open**. The Nordic recommendations say as much: «For older adults, the associations between
overweight and health outcomes are less clear, and the available data are inadequate to make precise
recommendations for optimal BMI in this age group» [@nnr2023].

### The trials that show a survival benefit enrolled mostly middle-aged adults

Ma and colleagues pooled 54 randomized trials (30,206 adults, all followed for at least a year). Weight-loss programmes, nearly all low-fat reducing diets, **reduced all-cause mortality: RR 0.82 (0.71-0.95), or six fewer deaths per 1,000 participants (2 to 10 fewer)** over trial follow-up, rated high-quality evidence [@ma2017]. Cardiovascular events did not clearly fall (RR 0.93, 0.83-1.04). In Look AHEAD, intensive lifestyle change in adults with type 2 diabetes left cardiovascular events unchanged, HR 0.95 (0.83-1.09) [@look2013].

Ma's review flags the age problem itself: «Most trials recruited predominantly middle aged adults»
[@ma2017]; 14 of the 54 trials had a mean or
median age of 60 or more. Ma did prespecify an age split (under 60 vs 60 and over), and **its text names
no age difference for mortality**, though this guide does not have the per-subgroup estimates. A
comparison between trials, not within one, can only suggest a hypothesis, so it neither shows that the
benefit carries to 70 nor that it fails to. The survival benefit at 70 is therefore **probable by extension, not shown**.

### At this age the evidence for weight loss is about knees, blood sugar and liver

The strongest trial in people like this guide's readers is IDEA: 454 overweight or obese adults aged 55
or over with knee osteoarthritis (wear-and-tear arthritis of the joint), followed for 18 months. Diet plus exercise lost 10.6 kg, diet alone 8.9 kg, exercise
alone 1.8 kg [@messier2013idea].

«Post hoc analysis revealed that 38% of the D + E group reported little or no pain after 18 months with
scores of 0 or 1 compared with 20% and 22% of the participants in the D and E groups, respectively.»
[@messier2013idea] That is **about
180 more per 1,000 nearly pain-free** than diet alone, and 160 more than exercise alone.
Those who **lost 10% or more of body weight** improved function and pain more than those losing less
[@messier2013idea], a comparison by
weight lost, not by trial arm, so confounded by adherence: people who stuck with the programme may differ
in other ways. See
[[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]].

Exercise for knee pain still works at this age, only less. For pain, across 57 trial comparisons (knee
and hip), the effect was a standardized mean difference of **0.44 (0.34-0.55) in trials with a mean age
of 60 or over**, against 1.32 (0.79-1.86) under 60
[@goh2019]. The effect shrinks with age but does
not reverse, and the comparison is between trials, not within one. Body weight did not modify the effect on pain (BMI 30
or over 0.56 vs under 30 0.46, P = 0.78). See [[Exercise Therapy for Osteoarthritis Pain and Function]];
chronic pain beyond the knee and hip is on [[Chronic Pain and Physical Activity]].

Heavier people do face more new knee osteoarthritis. One study found **RR 3.6 (1.7-7.7)** for the
heaviest fifth of women with high leisure activity
[@duong2025kneeoa]. That is a single study,
and it measured new disease rather than symptoms in an existing knee. For an obese person's painful
knee, the default is **low-impact exercise such as cycling or water exercise, not avoiding exercise**
. In a meta-analysis of knee osteoarthritis trials, stationary cycling relieved pain against
no exercise. It was «neither superior nor inferior to other exercises» on the WOMAC pain, stiffness and
function subscales and on every KOOS subscale (WOMAC and KOOS are standard knee-arthritis
questionnaires) [@luan2020]. See
[[Knee Osteoarthritis Incidence and Risk Factors]] and [[Stationary Cycling for Knee Osteoarthritis]].

For blood sugar, each kilogram lost in the Diabetes Prevention Program cut risk by about 16%, adjusted
for diet and activity changes [@hamman2006]; the trial's
over-60 results are in the blood-sugar section above. See [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].

For a fatty liver, the European guideline sets graded targets: **at least 5% weight loss to reduce liver
fat, 7-10% for inflammation, 10% or more for fibrosis (scarring)**
[@easl2024]. These targets rest largely on one
biopsy trial, and none of it is age-specific. See [[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]; why fat in the
liver and pancreas matters more than total weight is on [[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]].

### The cost is muscle; resistance training and protein reduce it

Weight loss takes muscle with it. In IDEA, diet plus exercise lost 4.7 kg of lean mass (9%) and diet
alone 4.2 kg (8%) over 18 months, even though the share of the body that was lean rose
[@messier2013idea]. The authors flag
why that matters at this age: lean loss is «associated in older adults with muscle weakness, greater
risk of falls and injury, and loss of independence and mobility, although exercise can attenuate it».
**So the real choice is diet alone versus diet plus resistance training plus protein.**

Resistance training (RT) during calorie restriction **kept 0.819 kg more lean mass (0.364-1.273)** than
restriction alone, across 6 trials in older adults (mean age over 57). In the authors' words, that was
«almost 100%» of the loss in those trials, and it came without slowing fat or weight loss
[@sardeli2018]. Higher protein, **at
least 1.0 g/kg of body weight per day**, kept 0.83 kg more lean mass (0.47-1.19) in adults aged 50 or
over without changing weight lost
[@kim2016].

Those resistance-training trials ran 12 to 24 weeks, and restriction alone lost about 0.76 kg of lean
mass in them [@sardeli2018]. Over IDEA's 18
months, diet plus exercise still lost 4.7 kg against 4.2 kg on diet alone, so the share kept over a
larger, longer loss is not known. See [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]] and
[[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]]; the kidney caution on protein is covered in
[[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

As an outcome, lean mass is a surrogate (a stand-in measure, not the outcome itself). **No trial among
this guide's sources tested a muscle-sparing weight-loss programme in this age group on falls,
fractures or survival.** The evidence on strength is weak. In Sardeli's pooled trials,
strength per kilogram of lean mass tended to fare better with resistance training (p = 0.07), and the
authors note the review «was not designed to test muscle function»
[@sardeli2018]. So the argument that
unprotected weight loss harms a frail or sarcopenic person rests on mechanism: they have the least
muscle to spare and the most to lose from weakness. Treat it as a reason to pair weight loss
with resistance training and protein and to monitor function, not as evidenced harm.

### A weight-loss drug speeds the loss but does not remove the muscle cost

People on GLP-1 drugs (the class that includes semaglutide) lose lean mass in roughly the same
proportion as people who diet. In a meta-analysis, absolute
lean mass fell 1.74 kg (0.45-3.04), while lean mass as a share of body weight rose 1.81 percentage
points (1.1-2.52) [@laverde2026]. Function was
not measured, and older people were not studied: «the effects in patients with diabetes, as well as in
older and multimorbid populations, should be evaluated in future studies»
[@laverde2026].

**Stopping the drug brings the weight back**: one year after semaglutide was withdrawn, in STEP-1's
adults (mean age 48-50 across the arms), «participants regained two-thirds of their prior weight loss»
[@wilding2022]. The same resistance training and
protein apply to someone on the drug. See [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]]; the heart-and-metabolism section
covers the cardiovascular case for semaglutide.

### Regain is not a reason not to try, and the right pace is unknown

For someone with obesity, the risk of losing and regaining weight is a weaker reason to hold back than it
sounds. Its harms «are more readily seen in people of normal body weight rather than in those who are
overweight or obese» [@montani2015cycling].
A meta-analysis of weight fluctuation concluded: «we cannot conclude whether obese individuals should be
deterred from eﬀorts to control their body weight by concerns about the hazards of weight cycling»
[@zou2019fluctuation]. See
[[Weight Cycling and Cardiometabolic Risk]].

Across 80 trials, people kept off a mean **3-6 kg (3-6%) at 48 months**, but those trials were not
age-specific [@franz2007]; see
[[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]. None of this guide's sources tests **how fast to
lose at 70**: *gradual, not rapid* rests on mechanism, not on a trial result.

Weight loss may also cost bone. Sardeli's review raises it as a concern, but its one trial that measured
bone was too short to tell [@sardeli2018]. The
next section takes up fractures on their own terms.

## Load bone through exercise; calcium and vitamin D pills do not prevent fractures at home

For obese adults around 70, **exercise is the fracture-prevention lever with direct evidence on fractures
themselves**: it probably reduces major osteoporotic fracture, and it works in two ways, by loading bone
and by preventing falls.
**Vitamin D and calcium pills do not reduce fractures in people living at home**, and the calcium carries
heart and kidney-stone signals. Hormone therapy is a women-only drug question that this guide assesses
but does not prescribe. — the ranking joins [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]] and
[[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]].

### Exercise probably cuts major osteoporotic fractures

A meta-analysis of 11 controlled exercise trials (9,715 exercise vs 9,592 control participant-years, adults
over 50 on average) found **exercise reduced major osteoporotic fracture** (hip, spine, forearm, upper arm):
RR 0.75 (95% CI 0.59-0.94; the abstract prints 0.54-0.94), with negligible heterogeneity
[@hoffmann2022]. The crude counts were 151
fractures against 196, roughly **16 vs 20 per 1,000 participant-years, about 5 fewer**. That arithmetic
is unadjusted and only gives a sense of size [inferred from @hoffmann2022].

This guide says *probably*, not more, because one research group produced both this review and the
bone-density review below. The two do not confirm each other independently, and no other group's
exercise-fracture meta-analysis is among this guide's sources [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]].
**No feature of the exercise programmes made a measurable difference**: neither intensity progression
(p = .133) nor trial length (p = .883) changed the effect, and the authors declined to write an exercise
prescription for fracture [@hoffmann2022].

Bone density itself moves only a little. In postmenopausal women, exercise raised total-hip bone mineral
density (BMD) by SMD 0.41 (0.30-0.52) and spine and femoral neck by less, effects the authors call
moderate at best
[@mohebbi2023].
BMD is a surrogate. The decision rests on the fracture trials above, not on the density gain
[[Surrogate Outcomes]].

**Much of the fracture benefit comes from exercise preventing falls, which a bone-density scan cannot
measure.** Falls-prevention trials show fewer fall-related fractures at low certainty (detailed in the
falls section, [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]). The two bodies of evidence measure
different outcomes in different trials, so neither raises the certainty of the other.

### Keep exercising with fragile bones; swap loaded forward bending for back extension

Osteoporosis changes which movements to load, not whether to move. A review of adverse events found
serious harm from structured exercise rare and not attributable to the programme. It also found that
none of the trials was powered (large enough) to detect fractures, and that three-quarters reported
adverse events as blank or zero
[@kunutsor2018adverse].
So the trials show **no evidence that exercise is dangerous**, which is not proof that it is safe
[[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]].

**Repetitive or loaded forward bending of the spine** is the main movement to watch. In a small observational
study, 89% of the flexion-exercise group (9 people) had vertebral fractures against 16% of the extension
group, and the review advises focusing on spinal extension instead
[@kunutsor2018adverse].
Ordinary bending is not the problem: «there is no evidence that single forward flexion movements in daily
activities or ‘roll down’ Pilates movements cause vertebral fractures – a common misconception»
[@kunutsor2018adverse].
Teach safe lifting; do not ban daily bending. Brisk walking is a weaker second flag, because in one trial
it raised falls (see the falls section).

### Vitamin D and calcium pills do not prevent fractures in people living at home

**Vitamin D alone does not reduce fractures in replete adults (people who are not deficient).** In VITAL, 25,871 adults took 2,000 IU of
vitamin D3 daily or placebo for a median 5.3 years: total fractures HR 0.98 (0.89-1.08), hip fractures HR
1.01 (0.70-1.47) [@leboff2022]. That is about 59 vs 60
fractures per 1,000 people over 5.3 years [inferred from @leboff2022]. Age, sex,
body-mass index and baseline vitamin D level did not change this lack of effect
[@leboff2022].

**Vitamin D with calcium also fails in community-dwelling adults.** The USPSTF evidence review excluded
people in institutions, people who were deficient or had osteoporosis, and people with a prior fracture.
It found no fracture reduction: one large trial in postmenopausal women gave an absolute difference of
3.5 fewer fractures per 1,000 (from 10 fewer to 3 more)
[@kahwati2018]. The review and VITAL
share no trials, so **two independent sets of trials agree that supplements do not lower fractures in
people who are not deficient** [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]].

The combination does work in a different group. A broader meta-analysis found vitamin D plus calcium
reduced hip fracture, RR 0.84 (0.72-0.97). Effects on any fracture were somewhat larger in trials of
people aged 80 and over living in institutions (P for heterogeneity = .02) and in trials that raised
blood vitamin D more (P = .04). Five of the six combination trials carried a high risk of bias
[@yao2019]. **Taking vitamin D to correct a confirmed
deficiency is a different matter from taking it when levels are already adequate.** Establishing
deficiency needs this person's labs and is a clinician's call [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]].

### Calcium pills carry heart and kidney-stone signals; food calcium does not

**Vitamin D plus calcium raised kidney stones**: RR 1.18 (1.04-1.35), about 3 more per 1,000 (0.6 to 6)
over the trials, the review's only moderate-certainty finding. Calcium alone showed no stone excess, and
vitamin D alone was not studied for stones
[@kahwati2018]
[[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]].

Calcium supplements, with or without vitamin D, raised myocardial infarction, RR 1.24 (1.07-1.45)
[@bolland2011]. A later pooling of 13 placebo-
controlled trials in healthy postmenopausal women found coronary heart disease up by 8.8 per 1,000
[@myung2021]. **Treat this as a warning signal, not
a settled harm**: the two analyses share most of their trials, and the result leans on one reanalysed
subgroup of the WHI trial [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]].

The signal comes from calcium pills, which deliver a large dose at once. Higher dietary calcium goes
with fewer kidney stones [@eau2026uro]. Total dairy shows no
hip-fracture effect either way in prospective cohorts (RR 0.90, 0.73-1.11). A small milk signal in
cohorts (RR 1.09 per 200 g, 1.07-1.11, 8 cohorts)
[@malmir2019] is judged fragile on
[[Dairy and Bone Health]]. **Dairy neither prevents fractures nor puts bone at risk**
[[Dairy and Bone Health]].

### Hormone therapy protects bone mainly while taken, and starting it late is contested

This applies to women only. In the WHI trial (women aged 50-79), oestrogen plus progestin meant 5 fewer hip
fractures per 10,000 person-years (HR 0.66, 0.45-0.98), with no change in total mortality, though WHI
concluded that «Overall health risks exceeded benefits»
[@rossouw2002]. The fracture benefit fades
once treatment stops [@niceng23]
[[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]].

NAMS and NICE disagree on whether the age at starting matters. NAMS reports pooled WHI mortality HRs of 0.69 (0.51-0.94)
at 50-59, 1.04 (0.87-1.25) at 60-69 and 1.13 (0.94-1.36) at 70-79 (P for trend = .01)
[@nams2022]; NICE finds no significant variation
by starting age [@niceng23]
[[Does Timing Modify HRT Cardiovascular and Mortality Effect]].

For a woman of 70, oestrogen plus progestin started after 65 doubled dementia (23 more cases per 10,000
women), while oestrogen alone did not significantly raise it
[@nams2022], and NAMS calls use in older women
«not appropriate for reduction in the risk of CHD or dementia»
[@nams2022].

NAMS says hormone therapy «may be considered for prevention of fracture in healthy older women at
elevated fracture risk when bothersome VMS persist or when hormone therapy remains the best choice
because of lack of efficacy or intolerance of other fracture-prevention ther- apies». Its key point
on long-term use, graded at its lowest evidence level (III: consensus and expert opinion), says such use
past 60 «may be considered in healthy women at low risk of CVD and breast cancer» with persistent VMS or
at elevated fracture risk where other therapies are not appropriate
[@nams2022].

That point is about continuing. On starting after 60, NAMS's key point graded Level I (its highest: good
and consistent scientific evidence) says initiation «requires careful consideration of individual
benefits and risks» [@nams2022].
VMS are vasomotor symptoms such as hot flushes. Whether to start, which formulation and what dose are
prescriber decisions this guide does not make.

### Acid-suppressing drugs link to hip fracture, and confounding may explain much of it

In observational data on over 2 million people, those taking proton pump inhibitors (acid-suppressing
drugs) had more hip fractures, RR 1.20 (1.14-1.28). In the COMPASS trial, 17,598 people randomized to pantoprazole or placebo showed
no excess of any listed adverse event except enteric infection
[@salvo2021ppi]. **Sicker people are more
likely to be prescribed these drugs, which may explain much of the fracture signal**
[[Proton Pump Inhibitors and Adverse Outcomes]].
Whether to stop one is a deprescribing decision; this guide flags it but does not assess it.

### What the evidence cannot yet say

Seven of Hoffmann's 11 fracture trials enrolled only women, and the bone-density review enrolled only
postmenopausal women, so **the evidence for older men is thin**
[@hoffmann2022]. This guide has no review of
bisphosphonates (the standard bone-strengthening drugs) among its sources, so it cannot say how much of
the bone benefit such a drug would already capture. Exercise acts
on falls, muscle and function as well, which no bone drug replaces [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]].

People at this age also ask about the brain, where most of the trials this guide draws on found no effect.

## Protect thinking through blood pressure and hearing; programmes built for the brain have not prevented dementia

For obese adults around 70, **one lever has randomized evidence against dementia: lowering blood
pressure.** Hearing loss is the other correctable risk factor; treating it plausibly helps, but that is
unproven. The programmes built specifically for the brain have not lowered dementia in people this age, and
several popular associations probably run backwards, from early disease to the behaviour
[[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]].

### Lowering blood pressure slightly reduces dementia

**Lowering blood pressure slightly reduces dementia.** Five placebo-controlled antihypertensive trials,
pooled at the level of individual patients (28,008 people, mean age 69.1), gave an adjusted odds ratio of
0.87 (95% CI 0.75-0.99) for incident dementia after a mean fall of about 10/4 mmHg
[@peters2022bp]. Dementia occurred in 2.9% on
treatment and 3.3% on placebo [@peters2022bp] —
**about 4 fewer cases per 1,000 people over a median 4.3 years**
.

Age did not modify the effect [@peters2022bp]:
«There was no evidence of a U-shaped re- lation of the effect at any age, nor any increase in risk of
dementia with treatment in the oldest age.»
[@peters2022bp] The authors also took a graphical,
non-randomized look at pressure achieved after a year, pooled across both arms. In it, lower pressure
tracked lower dementia risk linearly down to at least 100/70 mmHg, with no interval reported.
Participants started at a mean of 155.8/82.9 mmHg
[@peters2022bp].

Observational studies report a U-shaped
link between blood pressure and dementia in older age
[@peters2022bp]. The trials enrolled people with
high blood pressure, so they did not test the low end of that U, people whose pressure is already low
.

The trials stopped early on cardiovascular endpoints, so they likely under-count dementia, which arrives
later [@peters2022bp]. The same blood-pressure
treatment prevents strokes and heart attacks; dementia is a second outcome of it, not a separate lever
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### Bundled brain-health programmes have not prevented dementia in unselected people in their seventies

**Bundled brain-health programmes (several interventions delivered together) moved a test score in one selected trial and did not prevent dementia
in unselected people in their seventies.** FINGER randomized 1,260 Finns aged 60-77, chosen for high
dementia-risk scores, to diet, exercise, cognitive training and vascular monitoring. Over 2 years the
programme improved a combined cognitive-test score by 0.022 SD per year (95% CI 0.002-0.042), a small
effect on a surrogate. Dementia itself was not measured
[@ngandu2015].

preDIVA gave nurse-led vascular care to 3,526 unselected Dutch adults aged 70-78 for a median 6.7 years.
**Dementia developed in 7% of each arm (HR 0.92, 0.71-1.19).** The programme lowered systolic pressure
only 2.06 mmHg more than usual care, and the authors call the trial underpowered for the 2-9% reductions
that blood-pressure drugs plausibly give
[@mollvancharante2016].
Its authors write that the results «mitigate fears that antihypertensive treatment in older age groups
evokes cognitive decline» [@mollvancharante2016].
With so small a difference in pressure between the groups, the trial says little about pushing pressure
much lower. Read it as insufficient evidence on dementia, not proof of no effect. It is also
consistent with the blood-pressure trials, whose difference in pressure was about five times larger
.

**When researchers pooled preDIVA with a second late-life trial, they found no group that responds.** An individual-patient
analysis of MAPT and preDIVA (5,205 people over 70, up to 12 years) gave HR 0.98 (0.80-1.21) for all-cause
dementia. Neither the pre-specified subgroups (including high risk scores and untreated hypertension)
nor an unconstrained search algorithm found a subgroup with a different effect
[@coley2025]. On test scores alone, a
network meta-analysis of 109 trials (median age 70) ranked **exercise plus cognitive training highest
(SMD 0.26, 0.10-0.42)**, above the fuller diet-exercise-training-education bundle (0.14, 0.02-0.27)
[@mendes2025multidomain]. Adding
more components did not automatically add benefit, and no trial among this guide's sources shows the
pair prevents dementia
[[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]].

### Whether activity itself protects against dementia is unsettled

**Whether activity itself protects against dementia is unsettled.** Pooled cohorts show active people
with a lower dementia risk (RR 0.80, 0.77-0.84), but that is an association
[@isomarkku2022]. An individual-participant analysis of
19 cohorts split follow-up in two: inactivity predicted dementia when measured under 10 years before
diagnosis (HR 1.40, 1.24-1.59) and not when measured 10 or more years before (HR 1.01, 0.89-1.14). The
lack of a link persisted in participants aged 60 or over at baseline
[@kivimaki2019inactivity].

The cohort meta-analysis reads its own long-follow-up data the other way. Its 16 studies with at least
20 years of follow-up gave RR 0.79 (0.71-0.87; mean baseline age 50.5), and its four high-quality
studies gave RR 0.82 (0.67-0.99). Three high-quality studies with young baselines and over 20 years of
follow-up gave RR 0.79 (0.62-1.01), which the authors call «also similar to the pooled RR in all
studies, but not significant»
[@isomarkku2022]. They write that «we did not find
evidence to suggest that reverse causation or regression dilution bias» affected the associations,
while noting that funnel plots suggested some publication bias
[@isomarkku2022].

The two analyses disagree, and the long-follow-up cohorts began in midlife, not at 70. Activity ranks
high in this guide because of falls, muscle and blood pressure. Whether it directly prevents dementia is
insufficient evidence, not shown absent, and this guide leaves the disagreement unresolved
-> [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]].

### Treat hearing loss, but do not count on it to prevent dementia

**Treat hearing loss because it is cheap and correctable, but do not count on it to prevent dementia.**
Hearing loss is a consistent risk marker (HR 1.35, 1.26-1.45, 30 cohorts)
[@yu2024hearing]. Hearing-aid
users show less cognitive decline than people with uncorrected loss (HR 0.81, 0.76-0.87). Those studies
are observational and GRADE certainty was low
[@yeo2023hearingaids]; people who
choose hearing aids may differ in other ways.

The one randomized trial, ACHIEVE, enrolled 977 adults aged 70-84 with untreated hearing loss. **It found
no difference in 3-year cognitive decline overall** (0.002 SD, -0.077 to 0.081) and no reduction in
incident cognitive impairment (HR 0.90, 0.61-1.33). In a pre-specified subgroup at higher baseline risk,
decline was smaller (difference 0.191 SD, 0.022-0.360)
[@lin2023achieve]. That is one subgroup of one
trial: a hypothesis, and a reason to put high-risk people first, not proof
[[Hearing Loss and Dementia]].

### Diet, sleep, social contact and weight: the links rest on observation, and some may run backwards

**The MIND diet's brain benefit is observational; its one trial found no effect.** In cohorts, each 1-SD higher
MIND score tracked a 0.042-unit higher global-cognition z-score
[@huang2023mind]. A 3-year trial of 604 overweight
adults aged 65 or older with a family history of dementia found a difference of 0.035 SD (-0.022 to
0.092, P=0.23), with both arms losing about 5 kg
[@barnes2023mind]. The trial rules out a large effect;
it neither confirms nor refutes a small one [[MIND Diet - Observational Benefit vs Randomized Null]].

**Long sleep looks like a sign of early dementia, not a cause.** The Lancet Commission concludes that
«further evidence indicates that prolonged sleep is not a risk factor for dementia, although dementia and
its prodrome may cause prolonged sleep. People should not curtail their sleep to reduce dementia risk.»
[@livingston2024] No trial among this guide's sources tests
whether treating a sleep disorder lowers dementia [[Sleep and Cognitive Decline]].

**Social contact and late-life weight are associations that may run backwards.** Less frequent social
contact is associated with higher dementia risk (RR 1.57, 1.32-1.85, in one review; 1.18, 1.08-1.30, in a
second)
[@livingston2024]. For mortality, living alone carries no
measurable excess in older adults (RR 1.05, 0.91-1.22), against 1.41 in younger ones; the authors
suggest healthier older people are the ones who live alone
[@zhao2022livingalone]
[[Social Connection and Mortality]]. People with a late-life BMI of 23-30 have less dementia (RR 0.81,
0.75-0.88)
[@zhang2022dementia].
Weight lost in the years before a dementia diagnosis would produce this pattern. So the
association is not evidence against intentional weight loss, which the weight section handles on its own
terms
[[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

Beyond blood pressure and hearing, this guide's sources support no further lever for the brain. Beyond
the steps in this guide, what remains to gain is small.

## Stop optimizing once these are in place; the remaining levers are small

**Most supplement pills do not prevent disease in people who are not deficient.** For healthy adults aged
50 to 74, the Endocrine Society's commissioned meta-analyses judged, with high certainty, that vitamin D
has little or no effect on fractures, cardiovascular events, cancer or death
[@demay2024vitamind]. The US Preventive
Services Task Force recommends against beta carotene (harm outweighs benefit) and vitamin E (no net
benefit). For multivitamins it found **insufficient evidence**, which is not the same as a shown lack of effect:
«The USPSTF concludes that the evidence is insufficient to determine the balance of benefits and harms
of supplementation with multivitamins for the prevention of cardiovascular disease or cancer.»
[@uspstf2022supp]
-> [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

**Supplements may help in a few narrow cases, each small or tied to a diagnosis.** From age 75 the
Endocrine Society suggests a low daily vitamin D dose for a borderline mortality benefit (RR 0.96, 95% CI
0.93-1.00; about 6 fewer deaths per 1,000 people; conditional recommendation, moderate certainty)
[@demay2024vitamind]. The mortality
trials averaged about 900 IU a day (range 400-3,333 IU), and the guideline prefers a daily lower dose
over intermittent large ones [@demay2024vitamind].
A person already diagnosed with intermediate macular degeneration probably slows its progression with an
AREDS-type antioxidant-and-zinc formula (OR 0.72, 0.58-0.90, for progression to late AMD; 3 trials,
moderate certainty) [@evans2023amdsupp]
-> [[Age-Related Macular Degeneration and Modifiable Exposures]].

**Vitamin D only slightly lowers the chance that prediabetes becomes diabetes, and it did not help the
heavier participants.** It cut progression to
diabetes by 15% (HR 0.85, 0.75-0.96) in trial participants averaging 61 years with a mean BMI of 30
[@pittas2023vitamindipd], well short of the Diabetes
Prevention Program's lifestyle effect in the blood-sugar section above. In the pooled
cholecalciferol trials the benefit appeared only below a BMI of 31.3 (HR 0.74, 0.60-0.90), **not at or
above it** (HR 1.01, 0.84-1.22) [@pittas2023vitamindipd]. These
trials enrolled people about a decade younger than this guide's reader, so the result applies to obese
adults around 70 only by assumption. For them, with high blood sugar, the lifestyle programme remains
the lever.

**A mildly raised TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone, a blood test of thyroid function) is not worth
treating for tiredness or low mood.** Across 21 trials in 2,192
non-pregnant adults, levothyroxine brought TSH into range but did not improve quality of life (SMD -0.11,
95% CI -0.25 to 0.03) or thyroid symptoms (SMD 0.01, -0.12 to 0.14). Certainty was high for both. Mood,
cognition and muscle strength did not move either
[@feller2018thyroid]. Trial mean ages topped out
at 74, so the result may not carry to people over 80 or to a TSH above 10 mIU/L. Cardiovascular events
and death were measured in one trial only, at low certainty
[@feller2018thyroid]
-> [[Levothyroxine for Subclinical Hypothyroidism]].

**Once a diet is broadly sound, switching to a named diet (Mediterranean, DASH-type or plant-based) adds
little to survival that studies can detect.** The
US Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee's review graded diets rich in vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts,
whole grains and fish as associated with lower all-cause mortality (Strong), with Mediterranean,
DASH-type and plant-based scores performing alike
[@boushey2020].

All but one of its 153
articles were observational, and it pooled no effect size
[@boushey2020]. Its cohorts mostly
excluded people with chronic disease, so its results lean toward people healthier than average
[@boushey2020]. Improving an already decent
diet further can add only a small gain, almost by definition. That gain is hard to separate from
confounding, the other ways in which people who eat that diet differ
-> [[Dietary Patterns and All-Cause Mortality]].

**The large levers also have limits to what they add; the sections above set out what is known for
each:**

- **Steps.** The step region where the mortality curve levels off after 60 is set out in the daily-movement
  section -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].
- **Resistance training.** The volume band that matched higher volumes on most function tests, and what
  higher volume added (fast walking; a higher but unconfirmed strength ranking), are set out in the muscle section
  -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].
- **Protein.** Above the PROT-AGE minimum, no study among this guide's sources finds where extra protein
  stops helping an older adult -> [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

Reach each level and hold it. No study among this guide's sources shows that going further keeps paying
.

**This guide does not answer one question: when to stop medicines already being taken.** Deprescribing
needs the person's full medication list and history, which makes it a prescriber's decision. The
evidence behind this guide does not cover it. Below is a short, ordered list of things to do.

## What to do, in order

**If you smoke, stop; if you drink heavily, cut down** -> [[Smoking and Mortality]],
[[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]. Then:

- **Join a supervised balance-and-strength programme**, at least three sessions a week for at least 12
  weeks (the minimum the falls guideline sets, not a tested best dose), and keep it going. If frail, start low
  and supervised; for an obese person's painful knee, choose water or cycling
  -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]], [[Frailty]].
- **Add resistance training if the programme lacks it.** Two sessions a week of two sets on each of
  three lower-limb exercises matched or beat higher volumes on timed up and go and the 6-minute walk. Fast walking speed favoured higher volume (low certainty; mostly healthy participants, median BMI
  26.8). Start light and add load as sets get easier (standard practice, not
  trial-tested); power-style lifting improves function tests more than slow, heavy lifting
  -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]],
  [[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]].
- **Eat at least 1.0-1.2 g of protein per kg a day (the PROT-AGE minimum; which kilogram to count in obesity is open, see below).** With reduced
  kidney function, a clinician sets the number -> [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]],
  [[Protein Intake and Kidney Function]].

Next:

- **Keep moving through the day.** After 60, the mortality curve levelled off at about 6,000-8,000
  steps a day in cohort comparisons; above about 8,000, more steps buy little extra
  -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].
- **Treat blood pressure with a clinician.** A potassium-enriched salt substitute is one evidenced
  option where salt is added at home, after a clinician's check for anyone in the household with
  reduced kidney function or diabetes, or on a drug that raises potassium or a thiazide; at this age,
  mention the switch at the next visit in any case
  -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]],
  [[Chronic Kidney Disease and Modifiable Exposures]].

Take the next steps when they apply to you; the last one lists what to skip.

- **With raised blood sugar, use a structured lifestyle programme**; metformin does not substitute for
  it at this age -> [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].
- **Lose weight only for a named target**, with resistance training and protein alongside, and track
  function rather than the scale; the same package applies on a weight-loss drug
  -> [[Resistance Training During Caloric Restriction]], [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]].
- **Treat hearing loss** because it is cheap and correctable, without counting on it to prevent dementia
  -> [[Hearing Loss and Dementia]].
- **Skip routine vitamin D and calcium pills for fractures** if you live at home and are not deficient;
  confirming a deficiency takes a clinician and labs. **Do not start aspirin for prevention.** Starting a
  statin after 75 without heart disease is a decision to make with a clinician
  -> [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]], [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]].

**What the evidence cannot yet say.** It cannot say whether muscle-sparing weight loss at 70 changes
strength, falls, fractures or survival, or how fast to lose. It cannot say whether to count protein per
kilogram of actual weight or of a lower reference weight in obesity, or what resistance-training dose
suits the frail. It cannot say whether the fall reduction outlasts a programme once it stops, or when to
stop medicines already being taken, which is a prescriber's decision
-> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]], [[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]].
