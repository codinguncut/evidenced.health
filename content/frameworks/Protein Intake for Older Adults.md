The **target** side of the protein question for the over-65 stratum: how much, and distributed how,
to maintain and regain lean mass and function. The lever is [[Anabolic Resistance]]: PROT-AGE reports
that in the fed state «most researchers now agree that there is an impairment of the muscle protein
anabolic response to meal intake in older adults,46,49,50 although some studies found no difference
between older and younger adults, especially when high amounts of amino acids or proteins were
administered.» On that basis PROT-AGE holds the general-adult RDA too low for this stratum. The evidence is
**expert consensus on surrogates** (Bauer PROT-AGE, endorsed by four geriatric societies), backed by
small metabolic studies — *not* a hard-outcome (fracture / disability / mortality) trial.
[@bauer2013]

## The target ladder — by health and activity status

PROT-AGE recommends, for adults >65:

| Stratum | Daily protein target | Locus |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy older adult | **at least 1.0-1.2 g/kg BW/d** (a floor) | «consume an average daily intake in the range of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg BW/d»; the key-recommendations box says «at least in the range of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg BW/d» [@bauer2013] |
| Exercising / otherwise active | **at least 1.2 g/kg BW/d** | «higher protein intake (ie, 1.2 g/kg body weight/d) is advised for those who are exercising and otherwise active»; the exercise recommendation box: «to achieve total daily intake of at least 1.2 g protein/kg BW» [@bauer2013] |
| Acute or chronic illness | **1.2-1.5 g/kg BW/d** | «who have acute or chronic diseases need even more dietary protein (ie, 1.2e1.5 g/kg body weight/d)» |
| Severe illness or injury, marked malnutrition | **up to 2.0 g/kg BW/d** | «people with severe illness or injury or with marked malnutrition may need as much as 2.0 g/kg BW/d» [@bauer2013] |
| Severe kidney disease (eGFR <30, **not** on dialysis) | **limit to 0.8 g/kg BW/d** (ideal body weight; the RDA) — the exception | abstract: «these individuals may need to limit protein intake»; key-recommendations box: «these individuals need to limit protein intake»; Table 6: «Limit protein intake to 0.8 g/kg BWy/d» [@bauer2013]. For diabetic kidney disease ADA 2019 says protein need not be cut below average intake, with no eGFR band or age group given (*Diabetes with kidney disease*, below) |
| Moderate CKD (30 < GFR < 60) | **>0.8 g/kg BW/d safe (ideal body weight), monitor GFR 2x/year** | Table 6: «Protein >0.8 g/kg BWy/d is safe, but GFR should be monitored 2x/year» [@bauer2013] |
| Mild CKD (GFR >60) | per patient needs | Table 6: «Increase protein intake per patient needs» [@bauer2013] |

The ladder rises with catabolic load (illness > active > healthy) and is capped at the RDA (0.8 g/kg/d)
only at severe renal impairment; moderate CKD keeps >0.8 but adds GFR monitoring. All figures are g
protein per kg **body weight** per **day**; the CKD tiers are per kg of **ideal** body weight —
Table 6: «yRecommendations are based on ideal body weight.»
[@bauer2013].
Bauer says «PROT-AGE recommendations for older people reﬂect the ISRNM guidelines, providing as much
protein as possible for patients not no dialysis based on actual kidney function (measured as GFR).119»
The ISRNM figures it reports for non-dialysis CKD are: «In patients not on dialysis, protein
intake of 0.6 to 0.8 g/kg BW/d is recommended for people who are well and 1.0 g/kg BW/ d for those with
disease or injury.» Table 6's dialysis columns (hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis) give >1.2 g/kg/d of
ideal body weight, or 1.5 g/kg/d if achievable; dialysis care is outside this page's scope
[@bauer2013].

### Floors, not ceilings — reading «at least 1.0 to 1.2»

PROT-AGE words the healthy-adult figure as a minimum in its abstract («at least in the range of 1.0 to
1.2 g protein per kilogram of body weight per day»), its key-recommendations box («at least in the range
of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg BW/d») and its body text («a range of at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg BW/d»); one recommendations box drops the *at least* («in the range of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg BW/d»). Its exercise box
gives one number, «at least 1.2 g protein/kg BW». The other rows are worded differently: the illness row
is a range (1.2-1.5), severe illness or marked malnutrition is «as much as 2.0», and the CKD rows are
conditional on kidney function. [@bauer2013] PROT-AGE states no upper limit for older adults with healthy kidneys
`[searched: upper limit/maximum/tolerable/excess/too much across chunks 01-05; 0 hits]`. Its renal
reassurance is general in its first sentence, then scoped to its own target and marked provisional in the
second: «reviews of research studies reveal little or no evidence that
high-protein diets cause kidney damage in healthy individuals, including those who are older.6,54,55
Given the available data, a recommendation of protein intake at 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg BW/d is expected to help
maintain nitrogen balance without affecting renal function, especially until results of additional
studies are available.» [@bauer2013]

PROT-AGE's diabetes section is two-sided. It opens: «For older people with diabetes, dietary
recommendations, including protein recommendations, depend on the individual’s nutritional status, as
well as on comorbid conditions.»; it grounds a higher need in «However, dia- betes is associated with a
faster loss of muscle strength and a higher rate of disability.»; it states «An older person with
diabetes and sarcopenic obesity may beneﬁt from increased dietary protein intake, whereas someone with
diabetes and severe kidney nephropathy may need to follow a protein-restricted diet.»; and for diabetes
with normal kidney function it relays one caution from the ADA's 2002 statement (its reference 104),
stated in energy terms, not g/kg: «The American Diabetes Association recommends normal protein intake
(15%e20% of daily energy) as long as kidney function is normal. Not enough is known about the effect of
high-protein diets (>20% of daily energy) to evaluate their safety.», followed by «However» and one study
in older patients (upper age limit 75) with type 2 diabetes eating about 30% of energy as protein who
«tended to require fewer glucose-lowering medications after 1 year, compared with their baseline
medication levels.» (Bauer's Table 5 lists it as an RCT of 99 older adults, aged 60; the reported result
compares the high-protein eaters with their own baseline, on medication use, not a safety outcome)
[@bauer2013].

The ADA's later consensus, held here, does not repeat the >20% caution
`[searched: evaluate their safety across chunks 01-02; 0 hits]`. It states «Evidence suggests that there is not an ideal percentage of
calories from carbohydrate, protein, and fat for all people with or at risk for diabetes; therefore,
macronu- trient distribution should be based on individualized assessment of current eating patterns,
preferen- ces, and metabolic goals.», and gives 15-20% as the typical intake, not a limit: «The average
daily level of protein intake for people with diabetes without kidney disease is typically 1–1.5 g/kg
body weight/day or 15–20% of total calories (45,146).» The sentence after it is about kidney disease and
runs against PROT-AGE's restriction (*Diabetes with kidney disease*, below)
[@evert2019]

[inferred from @bauer2013] A minimum stated as a range is ambiguous.
Read with the exercise box, the healthy-adult and active rows say: at least 1.0 g/kg if not exercising
or otherwise active, at least 1.2 g/kg if exercising or otherwise active. PROT-AGE does not itself assign
1.0 to inactive adults (it recommends exercise for all older adults «at individualized levels that are
safe and tolerated», chunk 01, and names people who may not tolerate it, chunk 04); this split is the wiki's reading of the two
boxes.

**How far above the floor is tested for harm.** The held kidney evidence pools higher-protein arms
averaging «1.81 ± 0.60 g · kg BW−1 · d−1» in adults aged 23-72 (mean 49) without kidney disease
[@devries2018]; there the change in
GFR (a surrogate, in interventions of 4 days to 104 weeks; very low certainty) did not differ between
higher- and lower-protein diets, and the GFR rise higher protein does produce (post-intervention GFR, low certainty) is read as functional
reserve, not damage -> [[Protein Intake and Kidney Function]]. Above 3 g/kg, Devries's Table 1 holds two
crossover trials in young men (mean age 26; n = 12 over 8 weeks, n = 14 over 24 weeks) of 3.3 g/kg
against usual intakes of 2.5-2.6 g/kg; end eGFR was 101 vs 102 and 98 vs 101 (higher vs usual intake),
a 1-3 point gap. Its two older-adult arms (labelled *old* in the table) point opposite ways: Wagner's (mean age 60, n = 10, 2 vs 0.5 g/kg)
ended with eGFR 77 vs 69, and Walrand's (mean age 70, n = 9, 2.1 vs 1.0 g/kg for 1.5 weeks) with GFR 74
vs 81 [@devries2018]. The second review tabulates the Walrand older arm at 1.70 vs
0.89 g/kg, and reads it with both sides: «GFR of younger adults increased significantly within the normal range, but older adults
did not respond with increased GFR and, in fact, GFR was lower at the end of the study period although the
change was nonsignificant (P = 0.13). Given the limited duration of the study (10 d), it is impossible to
determine if the nonsignificant change in GFR in older adults would be sustained or if GFR would increase
were the study prolonged. Data such as these have led to concerns that elderly subjects may be at greater
risk of kidney disease in response to higher levels of protein intake (52). However, recent cross-sectional
data, although not meeting eligibility criteria for the current review, suggest otherwise.» The data behind that *otherwise*: in elderly Cardiovascular Health Study
participants, GFR was 74 in the highest protein quartile (1.63 g/kg) vs 71 in the lowest (1.00 g/kg), a
cross-sectional comparison. The review's next sentence keeps the question open: «Given the importance of
protein intake to the maintenance of body pro- tein with age (54), additional research is needed to
determine if healthy older adults are at increased risk for kidney disease in response to dietary protein
intake.» (its reference 54 is PROT-AGE). The review reports blood markers as mostly within normal limits;
the exception it names is BUN just past its upper limit at 3.6 g/kg in a crossover of 5 young men, which
the review's authors qualify: «Given the very small sample size, it is difficult to know if the observed
increase is real or an artifact.» [@vanelswyk2018] (Both
renal reviews carry beef/dairy-industry ties, detailed on the kidney page and under *Sponsor exposure*
below.)

 So the no-harm-detected reading near 2 g/kg rests on mostly middle-aged adults and a kidney
surrogate. The held over-65 trial data near that dose are one 9-person, 10-day arm with a non-significantly
lower GFR; a cross-sectional comparison in elderly people (highest intake quartile 1.63 g/kg) found a slightly
higher one.
Neither settles an age difference. At \~3 g/kg the held data are young trained men over
8-24 weeks (eGFR 1-3 points lower than at their usual 2.5-2.6 g/kg) and one 5-man study; in older adults
nothing is tested. For over-65s that is insufficient evidence on harm at high intakes, not evidence of harm.

### Low body weight — an absolute per-meal figure vs a per-kg one

PROT-AGE's per-meal anabolic threshold is an absolute 25-30 g of protein
[@bauer2013]. Moore's older-adult per-meal breakpoint
is relative to body mass, 0.40 g/kg (± 0.19, 95% CI; three such meals give \~1.20 g/kg/d, *The parameter
table*, below). Moore set out to test whether a per-body-weight recommendation can be made, because in the
earlier acute studies «absolute doses of protein were provided with no account for differences in BM».
He reads the breakpoint as an average requirement: «the acute protein intake may be as high as \~0.60 g/kg
for some older men (depending on the presence of potential contributing factors to the 'anabolic
resistance' of MPS)», so the breakpoint «could be considered a minimum target for meal protein intake with
the upper 95% CI satisfying the majority men», adding that «the present data set is likely constrained to
the conditions of studies utilized». Moore's older men weighed 55.1-108.1 kg
[@moore2014]. Moore and Bauer share an author,
so the two figures are not independent. For guidance on underweight patients PROT-AGE reports, in a critically-ill context,
that «In the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines, Weijs et al72
propose using 'ideal body weight' to more accurately estimate protein requirements for underweight (body
mass index [BMI] <20 kg/m2) and obese (BMI >30 kg/m2) patients.», and places «marked malnutrition» in the
up-to-2.0 g/kg band. [@bauer2013]

[inferred from @bauer2013; @moore2014]
The two figures sit on different scales and the held sources do not reconcile them. At 50 kg (just
below Moore's studied range), meeting Bauer's absolute threshold at three meals takes 75-90 g a day, 1.5-1.8
g/kg; Moore's average gives 20 g a meal (1.2 g/kg/d) and the top of his interval \~30 g. Neither source
tests a light person, and neither shows that lightness itself raises the per-kg need. PROT-AGE leans
toward even distribution but leaves even versus pulse feeding open (*Per-meal distribution*, below); and
which body weight to count for an
underweight person who is not critically ill is not settled (the ideal-body-weight proposal, made for
critically ill patients, would raise the per-kg amount at BMI <20). For marked malnutrition PROT-AGE says
intake «may need» to reach as much as 2.0 g/kg (its 1.2-1.5 band is for acute or chronic disease).

### Lower-intake evidence in CKD

Alongside the CKD tiers in the ladder table above, Bauer also reports lower-intake evidence for CKD 3-4: «In patients with nondiabetic
CKD stages 3 and 4 (moderate to severe) up to age 70, there is evidence that low-protein diets can slow the
progression of CKD.112e114 Compared with a noneprotein- limited diet, a low-protein diet of 0.6 g/kg BW/d can
prevent a decline in GFR of approximately 1 mL/min per year per 1.73 m2 and is associated with a 30% decrease
in reaching a dialysis- dependent stage.114,115» — with a safety caveat: in an RCT of well-controlled CKD,
«a small but signiﬁ- cant decline in nutrition indicators, essentially muscle mass, has been observed.»
Bauer qualifies the malnutrition risk, not the muscle-mass finding: with nutritional counselling toward 30
kcal/kg/d and renal-dietitian follow-up, «Under those conditions, the development of malnutrition during a low-protein diet
is an extremely rare event.115 As a safety precaution, malnourished patients and patients under stress
conditions (eg, hospitalization or surgery) should not follow a protein-restricted diet.» Two studies in older
patients follow: an Italian RCT (patients aged 70, close to dialysis; 0.3 g/kg/d plus keto-analogues) that
«delayed the start of dialysis by approximately 11 months» against a control group that «followed a
nonrestricted protein diet and immediately started dialysis», with «similar mortality rates» and maintained
nutritional status («patients enrolled in the study were not malnourished at baseline», counselled to 35
kcal/kg/d), and a retrospective Dutch study (average age 65, «uncomplicated advanced CKD»; 0.6 g/kg/d with
counselling) that
«helped delay the start of dialysis by 6 months, with no difference in mortality». Then, for older CKD patients
with sarcopenia: «Nonetheless, some experts remain concerned about prospects for survival in older patients with CKD
with sarcopenia, or depleted muscle mass. These experts call for 0.8 g protein/kg BW/d as a measure to help
maintain fat-free mass and improve survival prospects (Table 6).113,118»
[@bauer2013]. (corrected 2026-10-22, round 4: was
*«essentially muscle mass», which Bauer bounds* with the «extremely rare» line — that line is about
malnutrition under stated conditions; the stress/malnourished exclusion, the two older-patient studies and the
sarcopenia 0.8 g/kg concern were missing.) (corrected 2026-10-22, ladder table above: the
severe-CKD row had no amount and the moderate/mild CKD tiers of Bauer's Table 6 were missing; was *inverts
only at severe renal impairment*. Bauer's Table 6 limit is *to* 0.8, not below it; amended same day: the
ideal-body-weight basis and the 0.6 g/kg CKD 3-4 evidence were added.)

### Diabetes with kidney disease — the two held sources may disagree, most clearly at severe CKD

PROT-AGE's diabetes section says «someone with diabetes and severe kidney nephropathy may need to follow
a protein-restricted diet», its Table 6 limits severe CKD (eGFR <30, not on dialysis) to 0.8 g/kg/d, and
it reports «Accordingly, the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative of the American National Kidney
Foundation (KDOQI) guidelines call for adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and diabetes to follow the
same low-protein diets (0.8 g protein/kg BW/d) as people with CKD, although there is little evidence for
adults older than 75.100» It also reports the other side: «Other experts argue that low-protein diets may
not be appropriate for all people with Type 2 diabetes. Koya et al106 conducted a clinical trial in 88
people (average age approximately 57  8 years) with Type 2 diabetes and nephropathy, randomizing patients
to follow a diet with low protein (0.8 g/kg BW/d) or higher protein (1.2 g/kg BW/d) for 5 years. Findings
showed that the low-protein diet did not appear to slow the rate of progression of nephropathy.» and «For
older adults with diabetes and mid- to late-stage CKD, some experts109 argue that the effect of the modest
delay in progression of diabetic CKD is too small, with a beneﬁt that accrues across a term that may be
longer than an older patient’s available time horizon.»
[@bauer2013]

The ADA 2019 consensus, in its *Diabetic Kidney Disease* section: «In individuals with diabetes and
non–dialysis-dependent diabetic kidney disease (DKD), reducing the amount of dietary protein be- low the
recommended daily allow- ance (0.8 g/kg body weight/day) does not meaningfully alter glyce- mic measures,
cardiovascular risk measures, or the course of glomer- ular ﬁltration rate decline and may increase risk
for malnutrition.» Its text notes that «Historically, low-protein eating plans were advised to reduce
albuminuria and progression of chronic kidney dis- ease in people with DKD, typically with improvements in
albuminuria but no clear effect on estimated glomerular ﬁltration rate.», then, after giving 1-1.5 g/kg/d
as the typical intake for diabetes without kidney disease: «Evi- dence does not suggest that people with
DKD need to restrict protein intake to less than the average protein intake.»
[@evert2019]

[inferred from @bauer2013; @evert2019]
For diabetic kidney disease the two held sources may disagree on what to do, most clearly at severe CKD (PROT-AGE also relays KDOQI's 0.8 g/kg/d for
adults with CKD and diabetes, with no stage band): PROT-AGE says restriction may be

## The number this replaces — and why the RDA is a floor, not a target

[@bauer2013]
The general-adult RDA is **0.8 g/kg BW/d**, defined as the minimum daily protein to prevent deficiency
in 97% of the population, derived from nitrogen-balance studies. PROT-AGE holds this «too low for older
people»: N-balance likely *underestimates* requirements (it cannot see muscle redistribution and uses
short adaptation windows), and in one 14-week study of 10 healthy older adults «consuming the RDA for
protein resulted in the loss of mid-thigh muscle area» even while whole-body composition looked stable. So
0.8 answers *deficiency prevention*, and for maintaining muscle in this stratum it is the start line,
not the finish. The RDA is a **floor that gets read as a target** (e.g. as a label's
100% Daily Value — not a Bauer claim) -> [[The Descriptive-Normative Category Error]],
[[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]].

## The parameter table — daily and per-meal are DIFFERENT quantities (do not collapse them)

[@bauer2013]
The single most common error with these numbers is treating a *per-day* target, a *per-meal* dose, and a
*hypertrophy* target as one quantity. They sit on different denominators; only the same-denominator rows
are directly comparable.

- **The PROT-AGE daily rows are a coherent ladder** (RDA 0.8 -> healthy older at least 1.0-1.2, at least
  1.2 if exercising or otherwise active -> ill 1.2-1.5), same unit throughout, ordered by stratum. Morton's
  \~1.6 shares the unit but comes from a different population and objective, so it is not a rung on this
  ladder (third bullet below).
- **The per-meal rows are a different axis** — the *distribution* constraint, not the daily total. They
  reconcile with the daily target only through Moore's own within-programme arithmetic (three meals ×
  \~0.40 g/kg = \~1.20 g/kg/d), which is a consistency check, not independent confirmation
  -> [[Anabolic Resistance]].
- **Morton's RT break-point does not transport to older adults as a floor or as a ceiling.** The \~1.6
  g/kg-total knee is statistically non-significant (p=0.079), and Morton reports that the added protein
  bought *less* lean mass with age: «The impact of protein supplementation on gains in FFM was reduced with
  increasing age (−0.01 kg (−0.02,–0.00), p=0.002)»
  [@morton2018]. The older trials (Morton's *older* group is over 45, mean age 65)
  supplemented less («young: 42±32 g/day; old: 20±18 g/day»), and Morton reads the age result as a
  higher need, not a lower one: «older persons have an increased need for higher protein intakes to
  optimally respond to this effect and see gains in FFM.» (which he frames as speculation)
  [@morton2018].
  [inferred from @choi2021] A meta-analysis of
  healthy older adults found no significant difference in muscle mass, strength or function when a
  nutritional intervention of any kind was added to resistance training (creatine on lean mass the one
  exception), and its meta-regression found
  mean age not associated with the outcomes; extracted on
  -> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]].
 So for an older adult who trains, the floor on this page stays PROT-AGE's at least 1.2
  g/kg/d. A higher target such as \~1.6 is untested in older adults in the held sources (the RT page
  lists older adults among the strata where the target is unresolved), and the held evidence does not
  settle whether older trainees need more or less than that.
  [[Anabolic Resistance]] is PROT-AGE's reason for setting its floors above the RDA; it is not trial
  evidence that a training older adult needs \~1.6. The older-stratum object is a floor, not a point
  optimum -> [[The Underivable Optimum]].

## Per-meal distribution — even spread vs pulse feeding (open)

[@bauer2013]
Because anabolic resistance raises the *per-meal* trigger, PROT-AGE frames the target per meal as well as
per day (whether even spread beats pulse feeding is open, below). PROT-AGE: «The per-meal anabolic threshold ... is higher in older individuals
(ie, 25 to 30 g protein per meal, containing about 2.5 to 2.8 g leucine)» — the leucine figure is the
leucine content of that dose. On timing and quality PROT-AGE itself holds back: «Protein quality, timing
of ingestion, and intake of other nutritional supplements may be relevant, but evidence is not yet
sufﬁcient to support speciﬁc recommendations.» Moore's breakpoint (0.40
g/kg body mass per meal in older vs 0.24 in younger men — «\~68%» greater) is consistent with the same
advice, though the body-mass difference was not significant («0.40 ± 0.19 and 0.24 ± 0.06
g/kg body mass (p = .055)»); only the lean-body-mass breakpoint differed significantly («0.60 ± 0.29
and 0.25 ± 0.13 g/kg lean body mass (p < .01)») (corrected 2026-10-22: p-values added). Moore came after
PROT-AGE and cites it (its ref 4): «presuming a maximal MPS response at each of the traditional
three meals of a day (ie, breakfast, lunch, and dinner) would help maintain muscle mass with age, our data
lend some support to recent recommendations based on a similar premise of maximizing MPS that optimal
protein intakes for older persons could be higher than the current U.S.-Canadian recommended dietary
allowance of 0.8 g/ kg/d»
[@moore2014]. So Moore lends some support to a higher
*daily total*, on the premise of a maximal response at each of three meals; it does not test the per-meal
spread itself (corrected 2026-10-22: was *is the metabolic basis for the same advice*; amended same day
to extend the quote, which had stopped at *similar premise*).
The default this page takes: **spread protein across breakfast, lunch and dinner so each meal clears the
breakpoint**, rather than the «typically unbalanced daily distribution of dietary protein that is common
in older adults in Western societies» Moore describes (which on the MPS-threshold premise may under-use
the sub-threshold meals) [@moore2014].
 The spread rationale comes from acute MPS: Moore
cites three balanced meals as optimal in *younger men* and conditions its own reading on it («Assuming
this balanced feeding pattern is most favorable for muscle protein anabolism»)
[@moore2014]. The two older-adult
pattern comparisons Bauer tabulates both favoured *pulse feeding* (most protein at midday): «A protein
pulse-feeding pattern (most protein at midday) was more efﬁcient than a spread-feeding pattern in

(midday) had a positive and greater effect on lean mass in malnourished and at-risk hospitalized elderly
patients than a protein spread-feeding pattern» (Bouillane 2013) — surrogate endpoints (whole-body
protein retention; lean mass in malnourished inpatients). Bauer leans toward even spread but leaves it
open: «This evidence suggests beneﬁts to even distribution of protein at breakfast, lunch, and supper;
however, recent studies have also shown anabolic beneﬁts from pulse feeding (ie, a main high- protein
meal, usually at midday).56,57 Additional clinical studies are needed to determine whether both feeding
patterns are effective or whether one is clearly favored over the other.»
[@bauer2013]. So even distribution is a cheap
default that PROT-AGE leans toward, not a proven optimum, and the two held pattern comparisons (both on
surrogates) favoured pulse feeding. (corrected 2026-10-22: was *the typical Western pattern that wastes the

## Renal safety — the healthy-kidney vs impaired-kidney split

[@bauer2013]
The routine worry — that higher protein harms aging kidneys — is not supported for **healthy** kidneys:
«reviews of research studies reveal little or no evidence that high-protein diets cause kidney damage in
healthy individuals, including those who are older», and 1.0-1.2 g/kg/d «is expected to help maintain
nitrogen balance without affecting renal function, especially until results of additional studies are
available». For healthy kidneys PROT-AGE adds no caution at its recommended intake; the
caution is **graded by CKD stage**, per Bauer's Table 6 (chunk 02): at **severe** kidney disease (eGFR
<30, not on dialysis) these individuals «may need to limit protein intake» (abstract) or «need to limit
protein intake» (key-recommendations box, chunk 04) — to 0.8 g/kg/d, the RDA (route (c) contraindication;
for diabetic kidney disease ADA 2019 may disagree, though ADA's sentences give no eGFR band or age group, so whether this reaches severe CKD in over-65s is open; *Diabetes with kidney disease*, above); at **moderate** CKD (GFR 30-60) «Protein >0.8 g/kg BWy/d is safe, but GFR
should be monitored 2x/year»; at mild CKD (GFR >60) intake rises per patient needs. Bauer also reports
two separate cohorts at the mild end. In one (ref 110; women mostly >60, none >79) older women «with normal
or slightly impaired kidney function» on \~1.1 g/kg/d «did not experience a reduction in renal function».
In the other, the Nurses' Health Study (ref 111; mean age 56.0 +/- 6.6 at start, none older than 68; Bauer calls them «older
women», the wiki reads the cohort as mostly middle-aged), protein was not associated with GFR decline at normal renal function, but
«among women with mild kidney
insufﬁciency at the start of the study, high protein intake (particularly nondairy animal protein) was
associated with more rapid GFR decline than expected» [@bauer2013].
 For anyone over 65 whose eGFR is 30-60, the operative word is *monitor*, not *no caution*;
the held sources give no prevalence figure for that band. (corrected 2026-10-22: two cohorts (refs
110, 111) had been read as one *mixed cohort signal*, and an unsupported *eGFR 30-60 is common over 65*
was dropped. Earlier wording was *a genuine stratum flip, not a general caution: severe
kidney disease ... is where protein should be limited*; the moderate-CKD monitoring tier, the 0.8 g/kg
limit, and the mild-insufficiency GFR-decline signal were missing; *should be limited* softened to
Bauer's *may need to limit*. Amended 2026-11-02: the key box's firmer *need to limit* restored beside it.)

Bauer's statement is a consensus-review direction; the **pooled RCT evidence is consistent with it in
mostly middle-aged adults** -> [[Protein Intake and Kidney Function]] (Devries 2018 SR-MA, 28 RCTs): in

## The chronic-outcome evidence — mass yes (in the deficit stratum), strength no (Komar)

[@komar2015]
PROT-AGE and the per-meal breakpoint rest on consensus + acute surrogates. Komar 2015 (gold SR+MA,
16 RCTs, 999 subjects aged >=65, leucine-rich protein supplements for >=10 days; «Additional amount of
leucine varied between 2 g/d and 7.8 g/d and was supplied either via amino acid formulas (BCAA, essential
amino acids, n=6 studies), whey protein (n=4 studies), casein protein (n=2 studies), ricotta cheese (n= 1
study) or commercially available protein-enriched energy drinks (n=3 studies).» — a **different research
lineage** from the Phillips-coauthored (Bauer, Moore) and Wolfe-lab (Katsanos) studies) is the closest
chronic RCT test held for this stratum, on lean mass (a surrogate) and strength. It tests supplements, not
the daily-intake target, and it both **supports and bounds** the target:

- **Supports the mass rationale — where there is a deficit.** Leucine-rich protein raised lean body
  mass +0.99 kg [95% CI 0.43, 1.55; p=0.0005] and body weight +1.02 kg [0.19, 1.85], but in subgroup
  analysis the effect was significant only in the **sarcopenic** stratum (LBM +1.14 kg [0.55, 1.74] in
  sarcopenia vs -0.05 kg [-1.55, 1.46] in healthy elderly). The healthy-subgroup estimate is
  **imprecise, not a shown null** — its CI (about ±1.5 kg) includes the sarcopenic point estimate — and
  no test for subgroup difference was found in the held chunk [searched: interaction / subgroup
  differences / test for subgroup across Komar chunks 01-02], so this is not route-(b) effect-modification
  evidence. So *raise protein to protect muscle* is cashed at the mass endpoint mainly for those *already
  losing* it — a repletion reading; enhancement in the replete is **not shown** (not disproven; Tagawa,
  below, did find LBM gains in a population without serious health conditions)
  [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]]. (corrected 2026-10-22: was *subgroup analysis confines the
  effect to the sarcopenic stratum ... vs null* and *not enhancement in the replete*.)
- **Bounds it — strength did not move.** Grip WMD +0.23 [-0.26, 0.73; p=0.36; I2=65%] and knee
  extension +0.07 Nm/kg [-0.26, 0.40; p=0.68] were null — the mass gain did not read out as measured
  strength ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). Komar itself flags this as likely under-powered («a small number
  of trials potentially insufficient to yield significant results»; broader-inclusion SRs did find grip
  gains), so it is *insufficient evidence* on strength, not proof of no effect — but it withholds any
  strength claim from this stratum's protein target. **A different lineage also shows no grip gain:**
  Coelho-Junior's observational MA (below) found no grip or lower-limb-strength advantage for higher
  habitual intake. So two independent routes (an RCT pool of leucine-rich supplements; observational
  cohorts of habitual g/kg intake) both **fail to show a strength gain** — but each is underpowered
  (Coelho grip CI -1.15 to 0.44), the exposures differ, and an observational contrast cannot speak to
  *raising* protein, so the joint reading is **insufficient evidence on strength**, not a shown null.
  (corrected 2026-10-22: previously read as two routes agreeing that raising protein *does not move*
  strength; `[E-independent]` dropped — two inconclusive nulls on different exposures are not a
  convergent finding.) `Corroborated by [@coelhojunior2018protein]
- **The exposure is protein+energy, not isolated leucine or protein-per-se.** Komar cannot separate
  leucine from the co-ingested protein/AA matrix, and «an increase in total energy consumption might be
  a prerequisite for the success of these supplementations with respect to parameters such as LBM» — a
  reminder that in frail/undernourished elderly the *energy* the supplement carries may be doing much of
  the work, so the lever is *enough protein and energy*, not a leucine trick.
- **Pool-overlap caveat (no independence lift).** Komar's elderly-protein RCTs (Tieland, Chalé,
  Leenders, ...) plausibly overlap Tagawa's protein-supplement pool below, so their agreement that
  protein raises LBM in older adults is partly shared-evidence, not a second independent route.

## The direct-function outcome test — observational, outcome-specific, no knee (Coelho-Junior)

[@coelhojunior2018protein]
Every source above sets the target against a *surrogate* (nitrogen balance, LBM, acute MPS) or, in
Komar, a chronic mass endpoint. Coelho-Junior 2018 (gold SR+MA, 7 observational studies, 8754
community-dwelling adults >=60 across 6 countries) is the closest thing held to a **direct
physical-function** test of the intake bins — one level up the outcome ladder, but bought at the cost of
design: it pooled cohorts/cross-sectional/case-control studies and **excluded all RCTs and
interventions**, so it estimates *association*, not effect. It both **partly supports** the ladder and
**bounds it hard**.

- **Intake below the RDA tracks worse function — every significant contrast is against the <0.8 bin.**
  Very-high (>=1.2 g/kg/d) vs low (<0.8) reached a small significant SMD **0.18 [95% CI 0.01 to 0.35],
  p=0.04** on pooled lower-limb function, and high (>=1.0) vs low reached significance on walking speed
  (ES 0.06 [0.02, 0.11], p=0.003). Whether that 0.06 is standardized is unresolved: the methods say «SMD
  was used in the comparisons between High protein intake and Very high protein intake versus Low protein
  intake in relation to Mobility and Lower limb physical functioning, respectively, since the
  investigations assessed the same outcome, but using different tools.», while Figure 3 is titled «Mean
  differences in Mobility according to protein intake», and the result sentence states no unit.
 Read as an SMD, 0.06 is trivial. (corrected 2026-10-22: heading was *but only at the widest contrast*; amended
  2026-11-02: the 2026-10-22 *MD \~0.06 m/s* reading dropped, since the source is internally inconsistent;
  heading was *Supports raising above the RDA*.) **Both significant

- **Relative protein (g/kg) is confounded by body weight.** The low-intake bin had the *highest* mean

  discriminate the two. (corrected 2026-10-22: previously framed as running against the expectation

## The bone-outcome facet — higher protein tracks higher BMD and lower vertebral fracture (one cohort, observational)

[@weaver2021protein]
Every outcome above is muscle or function; **bone** is a separate channel of the same exposure. The
closest direct test held is Weaver 2021 — a **single prospective observational cohort** (Health ABC,
n=2160 community-dwelling White and Black adults, mean 73.5 y, 51.5% women, 5-y follow-up), **not** an
SR/MA. Protein was measured as **% of total energy intake** (108-item FFQ; tertiles <13% / 13-15% /
&gt;=15% TEI; mean ± SD 0.8 ± 0.3 / 0.9 ± 0.3 / 1.1 ± 0.4 g/kg/d), so the upper-vs-lower contrast (\~1.1 vs
\~0.8 g/kg) is close on average to the **PROT-AGE-floor vs RDA** contrast this page centres, though with
those SDs the %-energy bins overlap heavily on the g/kg scale. It gives a directional bone **signal** at
that contrast, but a weak one.

- **Cross-sectional BMD — positive, small.** Upper vs lower tertile: «mean baseline BMD in
  participants in the upper protein tertile was 1.8% higher for the total hip, and 6.0% (trabecular),
  2.7% (cortical), and 3.3% (integral) higher for the L3 vertebra» (full-model p<.05, positive linear
  trends). **Middle vs lower did not differ** — only the widest contrast separated, so no knee at the
  13-15% band is located -> [[The Underivable Optimum]].
- **BMD *change* — null.** «Protein intake did not affect change in BMD at any site over the follow-up
  period» (4-y DXA, 5-y QCT). The cross-sectional advantage did **not** read out as slower bone loss:
  it is a level difference (lifetime accumulation, or the healthy-eater profile), not a demonstrated
  effect on the trajectory. This weakens the causal reading of the fracture signal below.
- **Fracture — vertebral only, overall-null.** «Total protein intake was not associated with 5-year
  overall fragility fracture risk (Table 4), although reduced risk of clinical vertebral fracture was
  observed among participants in the upper protein tertile compared to those in the lower protein
  tertile (hazard ratio: 0.36 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.14, 0.97], p = .04)». The HR 0.36 rests
  on **8 vs 14 vertebral events**, CI upper bound 0.97 — barely significant, and **adjustment-dependent**:
  the base model was null (HR 0.52 [0.21, 1.26]); significance appears only in the full model (Table 4)
  (corrected 2026-10-22: base-model HR added). **Hip fracture was NOT reduced** — «Protein intake did
  not decrease hip fracture risk in our cohort ... the <1% incidence of hip fracture in our cohort limited
  the study's power»; the full-model hip point estimate actually ran the other way, HR 1.82 [0.54, 6.11]
  on 8 (upper) vs 5 (lower) cases — uninformative either way (insufficient evidence on hip, not no effect)
  (corrected 2026-10-22: hip HR added).

[inferred from @weaver2021protein]

- **Residual confounding is the load-bearing caveat.** The upper-protein tertile is the textbook
  healthy-eater profile: «Participants in the upper protein tertile were more likely to be female,
  White, nonsmokers, nonsedentary, have lower energy intake ... higher dietary calcium and vitamin D
  intakes, be on calcium, vitamin D, or estrogen supplementation, and have higher cognitive function».
  Full-model adjustment covered these plus BMI, physical activity, osteoporosis meds and diagnosis —
  but a single cohort cannot isolate protein from the covarying better-nutrition / higher-SES /
  less-frail bundle. The authors concede «the observational nature of this study does not allow for
  evaluation of a causal association between dietary protein intake and changes in BMD and fracture
  risk» -> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].
- **Exposure = % TEI, self-reported FFQ.** Protein-as-%-energy indexes a dietary *pattern* (the upper
  tertile ate less total energy and lower fat/carb %), and FFQ carries the field's binding measurement
  error -> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]] (attenuation toward null).
- **The animal-vs-source signal is likely total-protein, not source.** Animal protein tracked some BMD
  sites while vegetable protein did not, but Weaver reads this as confounded by dose: «the positive
  effects on BMD with increased animal protein intake observed in our study may stem from a higher
  total protein intake, rather than the specific protein source» (and an RCT MA comparing equal
  animal-vs-vegetable protein found no BMD difference). So this adds no source-choice bone decision
  -> [[Dietary Protein and Mortality]].

## Decision relevance

[inferred from @bauer2013; @moore2014; @evert2019]

- **For a healthy older adult, eat at least 1.0-1.2 g/kg/d (at least 1.2 if exercising or otherwise
  active, resistance training included), with meals of \~25-30 g protein** — \~25-30 g is PROT-AGE's
  per-meal threshold. Reading the floor as 1.0 if inactive and 1.2 if active is the wiki's reading of
  PROT-AGE's two boxes (*Floors, not ceilings*, above). A higher figure for a training older adult, such
  as Morton's \~1.6, is untested in older adults and is not a floor here (*The parameter table*, above).
- **For a light person the two per-meal figures diverge.** Bauer's absolute threshold at three meals
  takes 75-90 g a day at 50 kg (1.5-1.8 g/kg); Moore's per-kg average gives 20 g a meal (1.2 g/kg/d). The
  held sources do not reconcile them and neither tests a light person (*Low body weight*, above). Both
  figures assume three even meals; PROT-AGE leans toward even distribution but leaves even versus pulse
  feeding open (*Per-meal distribution*, above).
- **That intake is a low-cost default with a bounded harm record, not a tested requirement.** The held
  renal trials detect no harm near \~1.8 g/kg on a kidney-function surrogate in mostly middle-aged adults;
  PROT-AGE's renal reassurance is general for healthy people including older ones, with its 1.0-1.2
  recommendation stated as safe provisionally; over-65 trial data at that dose are thin. The two reviews
  are not separate confirmations of PROT-AGE: Devries shares an author (Phillips) with it, and Van Elswyk,
  a different author group, cites PROT-AGE, tabulates the same Walrand trial as Devries, and states that «additional research is needed to
  determine if healthy older adults are at increased risk for kidney disease in response to dietary
  protein intake.» [@vanelswyk2018] (*How far above the
  floor*, above).
- **With diabetes, protein advice is individual.** PROT-AGE says sarcopenic obesity may benefit from more
  protein, and the ADA's 2019 consensus sets no ideal protein share (the >20%-of-energy caution PROT-AGE
  relays is the ADA's 2002 statement; *Floors, not ceilings*, above). For diabetic kidney disease the two
  held sources may disagree on whether to restrict (ADA's sentences give no eGFR band or age group, so whether this reaches severe CKD in over-65s is open; *Diabetes with kidney disease*, above). The renal reviews'
  diabetes data are on [[Protein Intake and Kidney Function]].
- **The move is raising the daily total**; spreading it across meals is a cheap default PROT-AGE leans
  toward, not a settled requirement. PROT-AGE notes that «older adults often consume less protein than do
  young adults» [@bauer2013], so the failure to
  guard against is undershoot rather than excess.
- **Bias the target UP within a stratum when the cost of undershoot is high** — during illness,
  weight loss, or an energy deficit (protect lean mass), the illness row (1.2-1.5) or the active row
  applies; PROT-AGE sets no upper limit for healthy kidneys and the held renal trials (pooled by Devries,
  who shares an author with PROT-AGE, above) detect no harm near \~1.8 g/kg on a kidney-function surrogate in mostly middle-aged
  adults (the one over-65 trial arm, 9 people over 10 days, ended with a non-significantly lower GFR; a
  cross-sectional comparison in elderly people found slightly higher GFR at the top intake quartile, 1.63
  g/kg), so a modest overshoot is low-harm on the kidney axis -> [[The Estimate-to-Action Gap]].
  - **The weight-loss case is directly evidenced in adults >=50** [@kim2016]**:** a gold MA of 20 energy-restricted
    RCTs found higher vs normal protein spared lean mass — WMD +0.45 kg [0.20, 0.71] with HP defined as
    >=25% of energy, +0.83 kg [0.47, 1.19] with HP defined as >=1.0 g/kg/d — and increased fat loss
    significantly only under the %-energy definition (WMD 0.57 kg), with only «a trend» under the g/kg
    definition (corrected 2026-10-22: the two WMDs were conflated under one g/kg definition). Kim compares
    these with earlier analyses: the %-energy result «preserved about the same amount of lean mass» (0.45 vs
    0.43 kg in an analysis of adults over 18), and on the g/kg definition retention «was also comparable for older adults» (0.83 kg vs 0.60
    kg in a younger-adult analysis at a 1.05 g/kg cut) -> the deficit-specific target and dose-response
    live on [[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]].
- **The one flip down is severe renal disease** — PROT-AGE limits it to 0.8 g/kg/d of ideal body weight,
  the RDA; at moderate CKD (eGFR 30-60) >0.8 g/kg/d of ideal body weight stays but with GFR monitoring
  2x/year (Bauer Table 6, above; Bauer's lower-intake CKD 3-4 evidence and its caveats are under
  *Lower-intake evidence in CKD*, above). For **diabetic** kidney disease the held sources may disagree: ADA
  2019 says people with DKD do not need to restrict below average intake, so PROT-AGE's limit may be contested
  in that stratum (ADA's sentences give no eGFR band or age group, so whether this reaches severe CKD in over-65s is open; *Diabetes with kidney disease*, above). (corrected 2026-10-22: was *The one hard flip
  down is severe renal disease*.)
- **Protein quality and timing: PROT-AGE says the evidence does not yet support a recommendation.** Its
  abstract: «Protein quality, timing of ingestion, and intake of other nutritional supplements may be
  relevant, but evidence is not yet sufﬁcient to support speciﬁc recommendations.»; its key box: «Protein
  quality, timing of intake, and amino acid supplementation may be considered so as to achieve the
  greatest beneﬁts from protein intake, but further studies are needed to make explicit
  recommendations.» The 2.5-2.8 g figure is the leucine content of the 25-30 g dose («25 to 30 g protein
  per meal, containing about 2.5 to 2.8 g leucine»), and Bauer's Table 1 adds «It is not known whether
  high leucine intake can limit aging-related loss of muscle protein.»
  [@bauer2013] Moore's breakpoint was
  measured with «high quality, rapidly digested ani- mal-based protein», and he raises lower-leucine
  protein only as speculation («We specu- late that physiological and/or dietary factors could affect the
  acute protein requirements to maximally stimulate»): «In contrast, muscle disuse (5,38,39), disease
  status (29), and/or lower quality protein (with lower leucine content (36,40)) would likely increase
  (ie, induce a “rightward shift”) relative pro- tein requirements, regardless of age.»
  [@moore2014] So someone eating
  mostly lower-leucine plant protein may need more grams for the same per-meal stimulus — a direction
  from Moore's speculation, not a tested amount -> [[Protein Quality and the DIAAS Score]]. This is a
  distinct decision from the source-substitution (mortality) question on [[Dietary Protein and Mortality]].
- **Bone is a third weak outcome channel favouring adequate protein — a benefit *signal*, not
  established benefit.** [inferred from @weaver2021protein] One
  observational cohort links the PROT-AGE-target intake (>=15% TEI, \~1.1 g/kg) to higher BMD and lower
  *vertebral* fracture vs the RDA-level intake — but the bone-loss *trajectory* was null, hip fracture
  was underpowered, and residual confounding is unresolved, so grade this leg **low-confidence**. It
  corroborates the *adequate protein in older adults* case at a new outcome without settling it; the
  muscle/function rationale stays the primary warrant. Some pooled protein MAs report an 11-16%
  hip-fracture reduction (others null) that this single cohort was too small to test — its own hip
  point estimate was above 1 (HR 1.82 [0.54, 6.11]), uninformative (corrected 2026-10-22: was *too small
  to see*) — not a joined
  tension, a power gap; the held *dairy* MAs were themselves null on hip fracture
  -> [[Dairy and Bone Health]], [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]. Protein's bone effect may run
  partly through the muscle->bone loading pathway, so this channel is not independent of the muscle
  target -> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]. **The named gap:** no RCT
  tests protein -> fracture in this stratum.
- **The daily amount and the source are two decisions, not one.** This page sets the *amount* and
  *distribution* for the older stratum; the animal-vs-plant *source* choice (mortality) and the
  *hypertrophy* target (RT) are separate -> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]].
- **Protein may raise muscle even where resistance training is NOT on the table — a plausibility pointer,
  not a stratum-specific proof.** For the frail, the dysphagic, or those who cannot train: PROT-AGE pairs
  its protein advice with exercise («In combination with increased protein intake, exercise is recommended
  at individualized levels that are safe and tolerated.» [@bauer2013]).
  Tagawa's MA (a *different*
  research lineage — Miyachi lab, no PROT-AGE overlap
) «demon- strates for the first time that protein
  supplementation is significantly effective without resistance training in a di- verse population
  without specific serious health condi- tions» (corrected 2026-10-22: quote re-lifted to the sentence
  end), and
  even «less than 0.3 g/kg BW/d (0.17 g/kg BW/d, on average) was suffi- cient to significantly increase
  LBM» — «Daily addition of a high-protein food item such as an egg (6-8 g protein) or 1 cup (200 mL) of
  milk (6.8 g protein)» is Tagawa's named lever for «the elderly or people with dys- phagia, as well as in
  low-income, food-insufficient populations». **But the finding is in a *diverse* population, not
  specifically the anabolic-resistant old** — and [[Anabolic Resistance]] predicts a *blunted* fed-state
  response at low doses in exactly this stratum, so the without-RT effect may attenuate where it is most
  wanted. So: protein-by-diet-alone is a reasonable move when training is infeasible (small, distributed
  top-ups, not loading), and it is a *separate lineage* from PROT-AGE (partly answering the *shared
  lineage* limit below) — a point about authorship, **not sponsor independence**: «R.T., K.I., K.U., K.N.,
  and C.S. are employees of Meiji Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan.» (see *Sponsor exposure* below)
  (corrected 2026-10-22: industry authorship added) — so treat it as a directional pointer to test, not
  as elderly-specific corroboration; it sets no age-specific dose of its own.
  [@tagawa2020]

## Limits — consensus on surrogates, shared lineage, sponsor exposure

[inferred from @bauer2013; @moore2014; @katsanos2006]

- **Surrogate, not patient-important, endpoints.** The targets are set against nitrogen balance, lean
  body mass, and acute MPS — surrogates. No fracture, disability, or mortality *trial* shows that
  hitting 1.0-1.2 vs 0.8 g/kg/d changes what the person experiences; muscle *function* and sarcopenia
  are on the outcome menu, but the causal link from *raising protein* to *better function* is asserted,
  not proven -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. The loop is open. Two patient-important endpoints are touched,
  both observationally: **physical function** (Coelho-Junior's walking speed and lower-limb function,
  above — unadjusted associations, significant only against the <0.8 bin) and **vertebral fracture**
  (Weaver, above — a single cohort with an underpowered, barely-significant signal and unresolved
  confounding). They move the function and bone legs from *surrogate-only* to *weak observational
  signals*, not to established benefit.
- **Consensus + small studies, partly shared lineage — partly widened by Komar.** Bauer is a Delphi
  consensus (`recommendation` tier — cite its underlying reviews for any effect/certainty claim); Moore
  and Katsanos are small, acute metabolic studies (corrected 2026-10-22: not both male-only — see below).
  Bauer and Moore share an author (Phillips); Katsanos (Wolfe group) shares no author with either and is
  linked to them by citation (both cite it). So their agreement is partly shared lineage, not
  corroboration from separate groups -> [[Anabolic Resistance]]. Moore is male-only; Katsanos is a mixed-sex
  parallel-group study. Komar 2015 (gold SR+MA, Vienna group) adds **different-lineage chronic RCT**
  evidence on lean mass (a surrogate) for leucine-rich supplements, in sarcopenic elderly — not a test of
  the daily-intake target — but it still *cites* Katsanos and leaves *strength* and hard outcomes
  unproven, so it upgrades the mass rationale without closing the surrogate loop. (amended 2026-11-02:
  was *coherence within one research school* and a *confirmation of the mass claim*.)
- **Sponsor exposure.** PROT-AGE was funded by a Nestlé Nutrition grant and most authors disclose
  medical-nutrition-industry ties (Nestlé, Nutricia, Abbott); Moore and Katsanos are partly
  industry-funded alongside public grants (Moore: a Canadian research council, the U.S. Dairy Research
  Institute, GlaxoSmithKline; Katsanos: an NIH/NIAMS grant plus AminoScience Laboratories, Ajinomoto)
  (corrected 2026-10-22: was *dairy / amino-acid funded*). Tagawa reports «No external funds supported
  this work», but five of its authors are Meiji Co, Ltd employees and Meiji researchers gave «support in
  data extraction» [@tagawa2020];
  the held text does not describe Meiji's business, so its product interest is not stated here
  (corrected 2026-10-22: Tagawa was missing from this audit). Katsanos's second author, Kobayashi, is
  listed at «AminoScience Laboratories, Ajinomoto Company, Incorporated»
  [@katsanos2006]. The two renal reviews
  behind the *low-harm overshoot* reading carry the same direction of interest: Devries's senior author
  reports research funding, travel support «and honoraria from the US National Dairy Council, Dairy
  Farmers of Canada, and the US National Beef Cattlemen’s Association.»
  [@devries2018], and a Van Elswyk
  author «is currently employed by the National Cattlemen’s Beef Association, a contractor to the Beef
  Checkoff, as the Executive Director of Human Nutrition Research.»
  [@vanelswyk2018] (amended 2026-11-02: renal reviews and
  Katsanos's co-author added). Under symmetric standards this is a
  directional-bias flag on the *eat more protein /
  supplement / leucine* conclusion — held loosely, not dismissed, since the underlying tracer physiology
  is hard to confound.
- **Sex, and the >65 label.** Moore's per-meal dose-response is men only (Katsanos included women, so the
  leucine-threshold finding is not male-restricted); the female per-meal *protein* breakpoint is only
  partly evidenced. "Older adult" is treated as >65, but anabolic resistance is a gradient, not a switch
  at a birthday.
