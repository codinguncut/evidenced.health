## Less salt lowers pressure a little; only a potassium salt substitute has cut strokes and deaths in trials

This guide is for an adult deciding how much salt to eat and whether to add potassium, from food or from
a salt substitute. It does not cover choosing a blood-pressure drug, managing chronic kidney disease, or
sodium limits in heart failure. Those are treatment decisions that need a clinician and the person's own
test results.

**Eating less sodium lowers blood pressure in almost everyone.** The fall is a few mmHg of systolic
pressure, roughly two to three times larger in people who already have high blood pressure. Feeding
trials tested intakes down to about 1.5 g of sodium a day (about 3.75 g of salt). Over that range each
step down lowered pressure further. **No trial among the sources this guide draws on found a point where
the benefit stops.** [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]] [inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]
[inferred from @graudal2020sodium]

**For sodium reduction on its own, the trials that counted heart attacks, strokes and deaths are few and
small, and the one pooled estimate WHO graded is underpowered and inconclusive.** WHO pooled two RCTs,
which gave RR 0.84 (0.57-1.23), and infers the event benefit from the blood-pressure fall instead.
[@who2012] [@who2012]
He reports a re-analysis of six long-term salt-reduction trials with about 20% fewer cardiovascular events
(P<0.05) [@he2013].
That re-analysis is ungraded and comes from the He/MacGregor research group, so it does not raise
certainty (the Events section below).

Drug trials show that lower pressure means fewer events. No trial has tested whether pressure lowered by
eating less salt protects in the same way; that is assumed ->
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]. This guide calls the assumption *the transfer*:
reading fewer heart attacks and strokes off a sodium-driven fall in pressure, because drug-driven falls
delivered them.

**The one large dietary trial that showed fewer strokes and deaths tested a potassium salt substitute, not
sodium alone.**
SSaSS switched about 21,000 high-risk adults in rural Chinese villages, most of whom had already had a
stroke, to a salt that was one-quarter potassium chloride. Over almost five years they had about an eighth
fewer strokes, heart events and deaths. The substitute cut urinary sodium and raised urinary potassium at
the same time. [@neal2021] So **the trial
shows what the swap does, not what less sodium does**.

**No source this guide draws on has settled whether very low intake does harm.** Large cohorts such as
PURE find more deaths and heart events at the lowest intakes. No study among those sources has run the
check that would separate a real effect from a measurement or confounding artifact, so the right floor
for sodium intake is unsettled. For someone with normal pressure and low risk, **the gain from eating
less salt is small whichever side is right**. [@mente2016pure]
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

**Potassium lowers blood pressure only when the pressure is high.** In people with hypertension, more
potassium lowered pressure; in people without, three short trials showed no meaningful effect.
[@aburto2013] For people like those in SSaSS,
this guide plainly recommends switching to a potassium salt substitute once kidneys and medicines are
checked. For everyone else, the case rests on the blood-pressure fall. **Anyone with kidney disease, or
taking potassium-sparing drugs or potassium supplements, should not use one**: their kidneys may not
clear the extra potassium, and the trials left them out. [@neal2021]
 [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

## Blood pressure: a sodium cut lowers systolic pressure 1 to 6 mmHg, with no plateau down to 1.5 g a day

Every camp agrees on one link: less sodium means lower blood pressure. WHO pooled 36 RCTs and found
resting systolic pressure 3.39 mmHg lower (95% CI 2.46-4.31), rated high certainty, for an average cut of
about 1.74 g sodium a day (about 4.4 g salt). Six studies that used 24-hour ambulatory monitoring found a
larger fall, 5.51 mmHg (3.16-7.87).
[@who2012]
[@who2012]
Trials report sodium as urinary mmol a day: 100 mmol is about 2.3 g sodium, or 6 g salt, and about 43
mmol is 1 g sodium.

### The fall is about 4 to 6 mmHg with high blood pressure and 1 to 2.4 mmHg without

He and colleagues pooled 34 RCTs lasting at least four weeks (3,230 people), with an achieved cut of 75
mmol a day. Systolic pressure fell 4.18 mmHg overall (3.18-5.18), 5.39 mmHg in people with hypertension
(4.15-6.62; 22 trials) and 2.42 mmHg in people without (1.29-3.56; 12 trials).
[@he2013]
WHO's split runs the same way: 4.06 mmHg (2.96-5.15; 24 studies, high certainty) with hypertension and
1.38 mmHg (0.02-2.74; 6 studies, moderate certainty) without.
[@who2012]

Graudal's Cochrane review comes from the camp that disputes the low-intake target. It pooled 195 included
trial reports lasting 3 to 1,100 days at a deeper cut, from about 203 to 65 mmol a day.
[@graudal2020sodium]
White people with hypertension fell 5.71 mmHg (4.74-6.67) and white people without fell 1.14 mmHg
(0.63-1.65), both high certainty. The Black and Asian estimates rest on 5 to 8 trials each and are graded
low to moderate.
[@graudal2020sodium]

**For someone with hypertension both camps agree on direction and rough size**: Graudal concludes that
sodium reduction may serve as a supplementary treatment in white people with raised pressure.
[@graudal2020sodium]
The two reviews share many trials (DASH-Sodium, TOHP II and others), so they agree partly because they
pooled the same trials. They are not two independent sources. [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

**The fall is about two to three times larger with hypertension, but the three ratios are not three
independent confirmations.** He's figures give a ratio of 2.23, WHO's 2.94 and ESC's 3.05. ESC takes its
figures from He and MacGregor, so those two ratios are one research lineage's finding. WHO's commissioned
review pools an overlapping set of trials, so it agrees partly because it shares those trials; it is not
a separate lineage.
[inferred from @he2013]
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

He is a member of, and MacGregor chairs, the salt-reduction charities CASH and WASH
[@he2013]. Graudal's group
holds the opposite position in print. This guide takes the pooled numbers from both sides at face value,
but not their interpretations.

For people without hypertension, three reviews give 1.14 to 2.42 mmHg. They do not measure the same
quantity, so this guide gives a range in words (about 1 to 2.4 mmHg) and never an average:

| Review | SBP fall, mmHg (95% CI) | Sodium cut | Trial length | People |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WHO 2012 | 1.38 (0.02-2.74), 6 studies | not reported for this group (\~76 mmol overall) | >=4 weeks | all; baseline BP in the upper half (Graudal's reading) |
| He 2013 | 2.42 (1.29-3.56), 12 trials | 75 mmol | >=4 weeks | all; median 127/77 |
| Graudal 2020 | 1.14 (0.63-1.65), 95 trials | \~138 mmol | any | white only; mean \~119/71 |

Do the three figures measure the same thing? **Partly**: the outcome is the same, but the trials, the
size of the cut, the duration, the ethnic mix and the starting pressure all differ.
[@who2012] [@he2013] [@graudal2020sodium]
Per 100 mmol of reduction, that works out to roughly 3.2 mmHg in He's normotensives and 0.8 in Graudal's
. No source this guide draws on says which of the five differences
explains the gap.

### Starting blood pressure, not starting intake, sets the size of the fall

**The higher systolic pressure starts, the more a sodium cut lowers it, and the effect is still present
below 120 mmHg.** Huang's review (133 RCTs, 12,197 people, the He lineage's larger successor) found the
continuous gradient by baseline systolic pressure significant (P trend 0.01). The yes/no split into
hypertensive and normotensive was not (P trend 0.08), and Huang calls that dividing line arbitrary.
Starting sodium intake did not change the effect (P trend 0.20).
[@huang2020]

Graudal, from the other camp, explains its gap with WHO the same way: WHO's normotensives had higher
starting pressures. The two reviews share trials, so Graudal's explanation is not independent backing.
It does narrow the dispute to people near 120 mmHg. Both camps see a small fall there; they disagree on
whether it is worth having. [@graudal2020sodium]
[inferred from @graudal2020sodium; @huang2020]

Comparing trials, Huang also keeps older age and non-white ethnicity as effect modifiers
[@huang2020]. Graudal reads the
racial differences as confounding by starting pressure, age and the size of the cut. **This guide does
not use ethnicity to tailor advice**; it tailors by baseline risk instead, which needs no subgroup claim.
[@graudal2020sodium]
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

### No trial finds a plateau down to about 1.5 g a day

DASH-Sodium fed people either a typical US control diet or the DASH diet. The DASH diet emphasises
fruit, vegetables and low-fat dairy. Against the typical diet, it carries less total and saturated fat
and cholesterol and more potassium, calcium, magnesium, fibre and protein
[@sacks2001dashsodium]. The DASH diet
changes all of these at once. So no DASH result in this guide, including the smaller sodium effect on
DASH below, can be pinned on potassium or any other single nutrient.

**No trial or review this guide draws on has found a point where further cuts stop lowering pressure.**
That holds over achieved intakes of about 64 to 144 mmol a day across both DASH-Sodium diets (about 1.5
to 3.3 g sodium) in 30-day feeding periods. It also holds down to a mean of about 65 mmol a day in the
low-sodium arms of Graudal's trials, many of them short. Nothing is measured below about 64 mmol a day.
Over the studied range the curve shows *no plateau*. That does not make it *linear*, and it does not
mean *lower is always better* ([[The Underivable Optimum]]).
[inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]
[inferred from @graudal2020sodium]

WHO also has a direct test of going under 2 g. Two trials (820 people) randomised people to several
sodium levels. The arm below 2 g a day (average 1.4 g) had systolic pressure 3.47 mmHg lower
(0.76-6.18) than the arm cut to just above 2 g (average 2.29 g), rated high quality.
[@who2012] [@who2012]

He and Huang each estimated how much pressure falls per unit of sodium cut, from different sets of
trials. He compared trials of at least four weeks with modest cuts and found 5.8 mmHg less per 100 mmol
(2.5-9.2). He labels this between-trial estimate exploratory and open to confounding.
[@he2013]

Huang, across all trial lengths, found no raw association between the size of the cut and the fall. After
adjusting for duration, age, sex, ethnicity and starting pressure, each 50 mmol bought 1.10 mmHg
(0.66-1.54), about 2.2 per 100. Restricted to He's window (trials over 14 days, cuts up to 100 mmol),
Huang reproduces about 5.8. **Both slopes support the direction, a larger cut bringing a larger fall, but not a precise figure
per gram.**
[@huang2020]
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

DASH-Sodium tested three sodium levels inside one trial, each fed for 30 days in random order:

| Step on the typical US control diet (achieved urinary sodium) | SBP change, mmHg (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| high to intermediate (\~141 to \~106 mmol) | -2.1 (-3.4 to -0.8) |
| intermediate to low (\~106 to \~64 mmol) | -4.6 (-5.9 to -3.2) |
| high to low | -6.7 (-8.0 to -5.4) |

[@sacks2001dashsodium]
[@sacks2001dashsodium]

**On the control diet the lower step lowered pressure more than the upper one** (P 0.03 for systolic).
The steps were 35 and 42 mmol (Sacks calls each about 40 mmol), so step size does not explain the
difference. On the DASH diet the two steps gave -1.3 and -1.7 mmHg; Sacks reports no test comparing
them.
[@sacks2001dashsodium]

WHO (P 0.17) and Huang (P 0.20) tested whether starting intake changes the effect and found no sign
that it does. But they compared different trials, while DASH-Sodium compared steps within the same
people, so both results can hold. WHO's own point estimates rose across intake bands, from 1.79 to 5.74 mmHg. Its null test therefore says as much about statistical power as about biology. [inferred from @who2012]
[inferred from @huang2020; @sacks2001dashsodium]

Comparing the reviews hints the other way from DASH-Sodium's steeper lower step: Graudal's deeper cut
bought less per mmol in white normotensives (the normotensive table above). Neither result settles
whether the curve bends below about 100 mmol a day.
[inferred from @graudal2020sodium]

**For a decision: over the studied range, every step down from high intake lowers blood pressure
further.** The Low intake section below asks whether it also pays on events below about 3 g a day.
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

### A DASH-style diet halves the sodium effect, and doing both lowers pressure less than the two effects added

**On a DASH-style diet the same sodium cut lowers pressure about half as much.** DASH-Sodium enrolled 412
adults with systolic pressure 120-159 mmHg (41% with hypertension, 56-57% Black in each diet group). It
excluded people on BP drugs, provided all food and held weight constant. Cutting from high to low sodium
lowered systolic pressure 6.7 mmHg on the control diet and 3.0 mmHg (1.7-4.3) on DASH (interaction
P<0.001). The interaction was randomised inside one trial, and that kind of evidence justifies tailoring
advice to what else a person eats. The sodium cut still lowered pressure on DASH. [@sacks2001dashsodium]
[inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]

**Doing both lowers pressure more than either alone, but by less than the two effects added together.**
DASH plus low sodium, against the control diet with high sodium, lowered systolic pressure 8.9 mmHg
(6.7-11.1). The single effects from the same start, DASH 5.9 and sodium 6.7, would add to about 12.6.
So take a combined estimate from the trial's combined arm; never add the two numbers.
[@sacks2001dashsodium]
[inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]

Siervo pooled 20 trial articles (19 on blood pressure) and found DASH's own fall of 5.2 mmHg (3.4-7.0).
In Siervo's meta-regression, sodium did not explain that fall: the sodium difference between arms did
not predict it (P 0.67), a null Siervo itself calls unanticipated and open to cautious interpretation.
Never add DASH's figure to a sodium-reduction figure.
[@siervo2015]
[[DASH Diet and Blood Pressure]]
DASH-Sodium ran 30-day periods, measured blood pressure only, and did not test free-living DASH-like
diets. [@sacks2001dashsodium]

### Trial length and the way pressure is measured change the size of the fall

Huang found each 50 mmol cut bought 1.05 mmHg (0.40-1.70) in trials of 14 days or less and 2.13 mmHg
(0.85-3.40) in longer ones, and 57% of its data points came from the short trials. Huang reads this as a
real lag of several weeks. **But its main duration analyses were null** (P trend 0.87), and its six trials
with repeated measurements showed no gradient either, so the doubling is a possibility, not a finding.
[@huang2020]
[inferred from @huang2020]

**A blood-pressure number depends on how it was measured.** WHO's ambulatory fall (5.51 mmHg) is larger
than its resting fall (3.39). But it comes from 6 of the trials rather than all 36, so the gap may
reflect the trials as much as the method. ESC reports white-coat hypertension, pressure raised in the
office but normal on ambulatory or home readings, in up to 30-40% of patients.
[@esc2021]
[inferred from @esc2021; @who2012]

Compare a personal reading with a threshold only when both use the same method. WHO calls its pooled
figure resting pressure, but the WHO documents this guide draws on do not describe the protocol behind
it (office, home or device). [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

## Events: for sodium alone, fewer events are inferred from the pressure fall; no graded trial shows them

A few mmHg matters only if it means fewer heart attacks and strokes. Drug trials show that lowering blood
pressure prevents events. WHO reached its moderate grade for fewer events from cutting sodium alone
another way: it treated blood pressure as a stand-in for cardiovascular risk. WHO cites the observational
relationship between higher pressure and more events, then two papers on biomarkers (one on blood
pressure as a surrogate, one a general review), and cites no drug trial directly. Its own wording:
«Recognizing the limitations of any biomarker, it was nonetheless determined that blood pressure could be
a suitable proxy indicator for risk of cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease and stroke.»
[@who2012, p.13].

Its direct event estimates did not earn that grade for fewer events: the cohort ratios were rated low to
very low, and the two-trial pool, though rated moderate, was inconclusive (the table below) ->
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]] [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### WHO rated the pressure evidence high and most of the direct event evidence very low

WHO's 2012 review graded the blood-pressure fall (the Blood pressure section above) at high certainty.
For events it had mostly cohorts, which compare people who eat more sodium with people who eat less, so a
ratio above 1 means more events at higher intake. It also had two small trials of reducing sodium, where
a ratio below 1 means fewer events on the reduced arm.

| Outcome | Design | Ratio (95% CI) | WHO certainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-cause death | 7 cohorts, 21,515 people | RR 1.06 (0.94-1.20), inconclusive | very low |
| Stroke | 10 cohorts, 72,878 people | RR 1.24 (1.08-1.43) | very low |
| Fatal stroke | cohorts | RR 1.63 (1.27-2.10) | low |
| Fatal coronary heart disease | cohorts | RR 1.32 (1.13-1.53) | very low |
| Cardiovascular disease | 9 cohorts, 46,483 people | RR 1.12 (0.93-1.34), inconclusive | very low |
| Coronary heart disease | 6 cohorts, 37,343 people | RR 1.04 (0.86-1.24), inconclusive | very low |
| Cardiovascular disease | **2 RCTs**, 720 people | RR 0.84 (0.57-1.23), inconclusive | moderate |

[@who2012]
[@who2012]

**Higher intake went with more strokes, fatal strokes and fatal coronary events, and with no clear change
in all-cause death or overall cardiovascular disease.** Only one randomised estimate of events from
sodium reduction alone carries a grade: WHO's two-trial pool. Its interval runs from 43% fewer events to
23% more, so it cannot tell benefit from no effect. WHO called it «inconclusive regarding the effect of decreased sodium on
risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke and coronary heart disease»
[@who2012]. WHO graded the pressure evidence high and the cohort
event evidence as low as very low, a certainty gap of up to three levels
[[The Certainty-Importance Inversion]].

**WHO closed the gap by moving the pressure evidence down one level, not by measuring events.** Its
Annex 8 reads: «Because of the well-established relationship between blood pressure and cardiovascular
disease outcomes, the evidence of an effect of sodium on blood pressure was also considered
moderate-quality evidence that reduced sodium is beneficial for reducing risk of cardiovascular disease,
stroke and coronary heart disease.» [@who2012] WHO used the pressure
evidence in addition to the cohort evidence, which it read as supporting fewer strokes. But it never
pooled the two into one rating [@who2012].
The guideline gives no single overall certainty rating. **WHO's moderate grade for fewer events therefore
rests on blood pressure standing in for events (a surrogate step), not on a direct finding** [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

### Drug trials measured whether lower pressure means fewer events, and for drugs it does

The Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration pooled individual data on 344,716 people
from 48 trials [@bplttc2021].
**Each 5 mmHg fall in systolic pressure cut major cardiovascular events by about a tenth:** HR 0.91
(0.89-0.94) in people without prior cardiovascular disease and 0.89 (0.86-0.92) in those with it. Per 5
mmHg, stroke fell 13%, heart failure 13%, ischaemic heart disease 8% and cardiovascular death 5%. The
trials showed no reliable heterogeneity across baseline systolic pressure from below 120 to 170 mmHg and
above
[@bplttc2021]
[@bplttc2021].

These participants were older and at higher risk than a typical adult: mean age 65, and 31.9 major events
per 1,000 person-years in the comparator arm of those without prior disease
[@bplttc2021].

For some ways of lowering pressure, trials measured the link to fewer events; for others it is only
assumed:

| How pressure was lowered | Pressure -> events link | What stands behind it |
|---|---|---|
| A drug | **measured** | 48 trials, individual data, with and without prior disease |
| A potassium salt substitute (sodium down, potassium up) | **measured once** | one large trial in high-risk rural Chinese adults (SSaSS) and pools it dominates; the Salt substitutes section below |
| Cutting sodium alone | **assumed** | WHO's graded two-trial pool, inconclusive; an ungraded He/MacGregor re-analysis (the one-lineage subsection below) |

 — per-route reading from [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], *Decision relevance*.
[[Surrogate Outcomes]] sets out the general test for when a marker can stand in for an outcome.

**Researchers dispute whether a diet-induced fall protects like a drug-induced one.** Graudal's Cochrane
review points to beta-blockers, which lower pressure in hypertensives without lowering mortality, and
concludes:
«Such studies indicate that it is not possible to extend the general association of BP with mortality
(Collins 1990) to the eﬀect of a BP-reducing intervention on mortality.»
[@graudal2020sodium] This guide reaches the same limit from
the other side. The drug-trial event figures apply to drug-induced lowering; carrying them over to a
lifestyle route is a second, untested step [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]. No trial
this guide draws on tests the transfer for sodium reduction alone.

### If the transfer holds: about 2-4.5% fewer events for a normotensive, about 10% for a hypertensive

Assume a sodium-lowered pressure protects like a drug-lowered one, and apply BPLTTC's slope. **A
normotensive's fall of about 1 to 2.4 mmHg works out to about 2-4.5% fewer major cardiovascular events,
relative.** At a low baseline risk that is a small absolute gain, and nothing this guide draws on puts a
per-1,000 figure on it
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]
[inferred from @graudal2020sodium; @he2013; @who2012; @bplttc2021].

A hypertensive's fall is larger. He's pooled trials found −5.39 mmHg systolic in hypertensives at a cut
of about 75 mmol a day (about 1.7 g sodium, 4.4 g salt)
[@he2013]. **On BPLTTC's slope that is
roughly 10% fewer events, relative.** The trials that started a drug in untreated stage-1 hypertension
supply the nearest baseline: 25 cardiovascular events per 1,000 over 2-5 years in their control arms
[@wang2025mildhtn].
On that baseline the sodium cut buys about 2-3 fewer events per 1,000 over the same 2-5 years
[[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]].

Both figures hold only if **lowering pressure protects the same way whatever lowers it, which Graudal
disputes, and the trial baseline fits the person.** The sodium figure is carried over twice: once
from the pressure surrogate to events, and once from drug-induced to sodium-induced lowering. A drug
started in the same stage-1 stratum has direct trials, but they are low-certainty and imprecise: total
cardiovascular events −2 per 1,000 (−8 to +6) over 2-5 years. That drug figure is direct and the sodium
one is carried over, so they are not the same quantity, and this guide does not rank them;
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]] makes the comparison
[inferred from @wang2025mildhtn; @bplttc2021; @he2013; @graudal2020sodium].

### Two larger event claims come from one research lineage and do not raise certainty

He's 2013 review reports a He/MacGregor re-analysis of six long-term trials in people with and without
hypertension, combining their participants: **20% fewer cardiovascular events** (P<0.05) and a
non-significant 5-7% fall in all-cause death
[@he2013]. He's re-analysis carries no certainty
rating. ESC's 2021 guideline gives a similar figure: a cut of 2.5 g salt a day (about 1 g sodium) went
with 20% fewer atherosclerotic cardiovascular events, RR 0.80, citing a He/MacGregor review
[@esc2021].

ESC grades its recommendation «It is recommended to reduce salt intake to lower BP and risk of CVD.»
Class I, Level A, citing the same He/MacGregor review
[@esc2021] [@esc2021].
Class I, Level A is ESC's grade for a guideline recommendation, not the certainty of a pooled estimate,
and here it rests on the same lineage's analysis. **ESC's grade and He's figure are one lineage's reading, not
independent confirmation.** So the graded *trial* record for sodium alone stays where WHO left it: one
inconclusive two-trial pool
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]
[inferred from @esc2021; @who2012].

### Kidneys: sodium is tied to kidney stones by a route that does not run through blood pressure

Sodium also acts on a second outcome, kidney stones, by a route that does not run through blood
pressure. In people who form calcium stones, high intake raises urinary calcium and lowers urinary
citrate. **The European Association of Urology advises restricting salt when urinary sodium is high** (level of
evidence 1b, strong), to about 4-5 g salt a day (roughly 1.6-2 g sodium). It notes that no prospective
trial has tested sodium restriction on its own [@eau2026uro]
[[Kidney Stone Recurrence Prevention]].

For first stones, Lin's pooled observational studies put high against low sodium intake at RR 1.38
(1.21-1.56). That is a highest-versus-lowest contrast with no
dose-response, so it shows a direction, not a threshold
[@lin2020]. The EAU adds that the link between
sodium and first-time stones «has been confirmed only in women»
[@eau2026uro].

For chronic kidney disease, Kelly's review of observational studies found RR 1.21 (1.06-1.38) for high
against low sodium intake, moderate certainty. The high arms started at 172-283 mmol a day across studies
(about 4-6.5 g sodium; Kelly gives 9.88-16.27 g salt). About 92% of the review's studies used
self-reported exposure, and **Kelly declined to set any target or threshold**
[@kelly2020ckd]
[[Chronic Kidney Disease and Modifiable Exposures]].

**Controlling blood pressure with a drug does not by itself deal with stone risk.** One drug class
touches the stone route: EAU lists thiazides for hypercalciuria in calcium stone formers and states
«Thiazides reduce calciuria». Whether that prevents recurring stones is less settled. EAU reports one
trial in which hydrochlorothiazide did not differ substantially from placebo, then adds «However, the
study was not powered to show any difference of HCTZ over placebo» and notes that the trial's main aim, a
dose-response on recurrence across three doses, was shown, in people whose calciuria sat below EAU's
treatment threshold
[@eau2026uro].

Substitution is outcome-specific: a drug stands in for a sodium cut only on the outcomes the drug moves.
A person whose pressure a drug controls still has stone risk as a reason to cut
sodium (even a thiazide addresses calciuria, not the lower urinary citrate that high sodium also brings;
EAU lists both effects of sodium), and for a stone former with high pressure the same cut serves both [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]
[inferred from @eau2026uro; @lin2020].

## Low intake: cohorts see more events below 3 g a day, and no source this guide draws on has tested whether that is real

The event estimates above, borrowed from drug trials, assume that less sodium is better all the way down.
Large cohorts dispute that below about 3 g of sodium a day (about 7.5 g of salt). **Cohorts have
observed the low-intake signal, but no source this guide draws on has settled it.** None has run a test
that could tell a true harm apart from a measurement or confounding effect. So the right floor for sodium
intake is unsettled. This guide carries both readings below and leads with neither. ->
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]], [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]]

> **Scope:** WHO's review set aside people with illnesses or treatments that can lower blood sodium or
> cause acute fluid build-up, or who need physician-supervised diets, naming heart failure and type 1
> diabetes. Nothing in this guide addresses sodium targets for them; in those conditions sodium is part
> of disease management.
> [@who2012]

### PURE found more events at both ends of intake, and the low-intake excess appeared in people without hypertension too

Mente's PURE analysis pooled **133,118 people** from 49 countries in three cohorts (PURE,
ONTARGET/TRANSCEND, EPIDREAM), with more than 10,000 events over a median 4.2 years. The outcome was
death or a major cardiovascular event. **People eating 4-5 g of sodium a day (10-12.5 g of salt) had the
lowest risk**, so that band is the reference for the hazard ratios below.
[@mente2016pure]

| Stratum | Below 3 g/day | Above 7 g/day |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertensive (63,559) | HR 1.34 (1.23-1.47) | HR 1.23 (1.11-1.37) |
| Non-hypertensive (69,559) | HR 1.26 (1.10-1.45) | HR 0.90 (0.76-1.08), not significant |

[@mente2016pure]

The two ends behave differently. The association differed by hypertension status (heterogeneity P =
0.0342), and excess risk at high intake appears only in hypertensives. **Excess risk at low intake
appears in both groups and survives adjustment for blood pressure** (HR 1.35, 1.23-1.49, in
hypertensives; still P = 0.0011 in non-hypertensives). Mente therefore suggests mechanisms unrelated to
blood pressure may be at work.
[@mente2016pure]

**Mente reads the split as a case for targeting sodium reduction.** The excess risk at high intake was
confined to hypertensives excreting more than 7 g a day: 7,060 people, 11.1% of the hypertensive group
(63,559) and about 5% of all 133,118 participants. Mente concludes that lowering sodium is best targeted
at people with hypertension who also eat a lot of sodium. It argues against a population-wide approach
in most countries, except where mean intake is very high
[@mente2016pure].

Mente's own summary calls the 7,060 «11% of the population studied», but the count is 11% of the
hypertensive group only [inferred from @mente2016pure].
WHO and ESC instead set one line for everyone;
[[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]] weighs the two policies.

These are hazard ratios across PURE's bands, from under 3 to over 7 g a day. This guide has no event rate
for each band, so it cannot turn them into events per 1,000.

Graudal's Cochrane review adds a pooled table of adjusted cohorts, using the healthiest subgroups. Usual
intake carried lower all-cause mortality than low intake, RR or OR 0.84 (0.76-0.93) or 0.87 (0.76-0.98),
with *low* cut at under 114 or under 130 mmol a day (roughly 2.6-3 g). **That pool includes PURE itself
(RR 0.62), so it does not independently confirm** what cohorts see in their lowest-intake group (the
*low arm*). [@graudal2020sodium]

### PURE estimated intake from a single morning urine sample

PURE did not collect 24-hour urine. It estimated each person's intake from **one fasting morning urine
sample**, run through the Kawasaki formula. Checked against a 24-hour collection, that estimate reaches
an intraclass correlation of about 0.70. Mente corrected for regression dilution using repeat samples
taken 30-90 days later in 448 people.
[@mente2016pure]
Even a full 24-hour urine fixes calibration, not day-to-day scatter: one day is a poor guide to habitual
intake. [inferred from @willett] -> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]

**Error that differs between people who go on to have events and people who do not can bend a flat
relationship into a U with no bias and no confounder.** Willett states the mechanism for diet recall:
«For example, greater random error for cases than controls would create a U-shaped relationship if there
was no true overall relationship.» [@willett]
No source this guide draws on has tested whether PURE's spot-urine estimate carries such unequal
precision between groups, so it is unmeasured. PURE's own checks would not catch it, because dropping
early events or sick people tests for reverse causation and confounding.
-> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]

Both camps argue over the measurement objection. Mente writes that correcting for day-to-day variation
and for the imperfect match with 24-hour urine «would “steepen” all the associations», at both the low
and the high end
[@mente2016pure]. Graudal
argues that a single estimate and repeated 24-hour collections gave almost identical outcomes. It cites a
2018 analysis by He and colleagues that this guide does not draw on
[@graudal2020sodium]. **No source this guide
draws on has tested either claim.**

Two other explanations stay open: sick people may eat less salt (reverse causation), and frailer people
may differ in ways adjustment does not remove (confounding by frailty). ESC makes the same point:
«Underlying illness and malnutrition may explain both low food and salt intakes as well as increased
ASCVD.» [@esc2021]
[inferred from @mente2016pure] -> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]

Four tests could settle whether the low-arm excess is real: a referent correction, a
Mendelian-randomization instrument for sodium intake, repeated 24-hour urine collections per person, or a
large long-term trial that randomises people to different sodium *levels* rather than to a reduction.
**No source this guide draws on has run any of them.** Each camp has argued its reading; none has tested
it in a source this guide draws on.

### Trials and cohorts point to different best intakes

GBD needed one optimal sodium intake to compute disease burden. It chose 3 g a day and, to carry the
disagreement, set an uncertainty range of 1-5 g: **under 2.3 g a day gives the lowest blood pressure in
randomised trials, and 4-5 g a day gives the lowest cardiovascular risk in observational studies.**
[@afshin2019]
WHO's line sits under 2 g (5 g of salt); PURE's lowest-risk band sits at 4-5 g. The 1-5 g range carries
both readings without choosing between them.
-> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]]

The two numbers come from different exposures and different evidence. The trials randomise a
*reduction* and measure blood pressure; PURE estimates habitual intake from one spot urine and counts
deaths and events. Trial low arms do reach below 3 g. They sit around 78-87 mmol a day in He's trials of
four weeks or more (usual median intake minus the pooled 75 mmol cut)
[inferred from @he2013], and at about 65 mmol in Graudal's pool
[@graudal2020sodium].

He's two trials longer than a year cut salt by only about 2.4 g a day, half the 4.8 g cut in its other
normotensive trials
[@he2013]. The deep cuts therefore
ran mostly for weeks and were measured on blood pressure and blood markers. **No trial this guide draws
on tests low-arm intakes against deaths or events, or runs at those intakes for years.**
-> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]]

Mente also modelled risk on the assumption that sodium acts only through systolic pressure. That model
predicts lower risk as sodium falls below 3 g; the observed hazard ratios rise instead. **The study
cannot separate two explanations for the gap:** sodium's effect does not run wholly through blood
pressure, or measurement error or confounding contaminates the low arm.
[inferred from @mente2016pure] -> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

The short trials that reach low intake show the body pushing back. **Both Cochrane lineages find renin,
aldosterone and noradrenaline rising when sodium falls.** Graudal also finds total cholesterol and
triglycerides rising; LDL does not change significantly in either review.

## Potassium: more of it lowers high blood pressure, not normal pressure, and the fall flattens from about 90 mmol excreted

Sodium and potassium push blood pressure in opposite directions: less sodium lowers it, and more
potassium can too. **Extra potassium lowers systolic pressure by about 5 mmHg in people with hypertension and by
nothing measurable in people without it.** [@aburto2013]
On Filippini's fitted curve, the fall is flat from about 90 to 150 mmol a day of potassium in a 24-hour
urine, with one small further step (0.9 mmHg) at 120 against 90.
[@filippini2020potassium]

Potassium is reported two ways, as what people ate (intake) or what their urine carried (excretion), and
each figure below says which. The trials measured excretion. Aburto's review converted it to intake by
multiplying by 1.3, so 90 mmol a day excreted is about 117 mmol eaten.
[@aburto2013]
In grams, 90 mmol of potassium is about 3.5 g and 120 mmol about 4.7 g. arithmetic,
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

### Potassium lowers pressure only where it is already high

Aburto's WHO-commissioned review pooled 21 trials (1,892 people) on systolic pressure. Raising potassium
lowered it by 3.49 mmHg (95% CI 1.82 to 5.15), and the review graded that result high certainty.
[@aburto2013] **That average blends two
different groups.** In 16 trials of people with hypertension, systolic pressure fell 5.32 mmHg (3.43 to
7.20). In the three short trials of people without hypertension it changed by 0.09 mmHg (−0.77 to 0.95).
[@aburto2013] Once one outlier was removed,
baseline hypertension status explained most of the spread between trials. The normotensive figure is a
precise zero: potassium has no meaningful effect on lowering a pressure that is already normal. Aburto
adds that these trials did not test whether potassium prevents a later rise, so that question stays
open.
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

**Cutting sodium lowers pressure in both groups; adding potassium lowers it in only one.** The sodium
fall is larger in hypertensives (the Blood pressure section above). For a person with normal pressure,
the trials do not show that more potassium lowers blood pressure; the stroke cohorts below give the
reason to eat more of it. Filippini's dose-response review describes a small fall in normotensives up to
a 20-30 mmol a day excretion difference. But it shows that fall only in a figure (with confidence bands,
no numeric estimates in the text), drawn from five trials compared with each other. This guide therefore
rests the asymmetry on Aburto's tabulated estimate, and treats a normotensive dose-response as a named
gap (the Gaps section below).
[@filippini2020potassium]
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

### Higher potassium goes with fewer strokes, only in cohorts; no other hard outcome reaches significance

Higher potassium intake may lower stroke risk. Across nine cohort comparisons (97,152 people), people with
the highest intakes had a stroke risk ratio of 0.79 (0.68 to 0.93) against the lowest. Aburto graded that
low in its GRADE table because it comes from cohorts; its abstract calls the stroke association moderate
quality. Aburto's text gives 0.76 (0.66 to 0.89) for the same nine comparisons and does not explain the
difference; this guide uses the table row. **No other hard outcome reaches significance.**
Cardiovascular disease (RR 0.88, 0.70 to 1.11; 4 cohorts), coronary heart disease (0.97, 0.77 to 1.24; 3
cohorts) and all-cause mortality (1.08, 0.91 to 1.29; one cohort of 1,766 people) all rest on very low
certainty. For those three the evidence is insufficient, which is not the same as a shown null.
[@aburto2013]

Aburto required 24-hour urine from its trials but not from its cohorts, so the cohorts' intake figures
lack the recovery biomarker the trials used ([[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]). Aburto also
counts the change in systolic pressure as indirect evidence on stroke and heart disease, downgraded once
for indirectness (its sentence names sodium, apparently a carry-over from the companion sodium review).
[@aburto2013] Aburto
thus makes the same surrogate step from blood pressure to events that WHO makes, and that step likely
explains the abstract's moderate grade.
 The cohorts report no baseline stroke risk, so the risk ratio cannot be turned into strokes
per 1,000. A person at higher baseline stroke risk gains more in absolute terms from the same relative
effect.
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

The drug trials predict a smaller stroke benefit than the cohorts show. In the BPLTTC trials, 5 mmHg
lower systolic pressure cut strokes by about 13%, so potassium's 5.3 mmHg fall in hypertensives
predicts a risk ratio near 0.86, against the cohorts' 0.79. The two are not statistically
inconsistent: 0.86 sits inside both of Aburto's intervals.

Three reasons could each explain the excess, and none is established. Potassium might act on stroke by a
route other than pressure. The cohorts might carry residual confounding. And the exposures do not match: a cohort compares the highest with the
lowest eaters, while the trials gave a modest supplement dose. **The drug-trial arithmetic supports the
direction of the stroke benefit, not its size**, and only where potassium moves pressure, in
hypertensives. For normotensives it gives no trial-based reason to expect fewer hard outcomes.
 [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]],
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]

### The fall flattens from about 90 mmol a day excreted (about 117 eaten)

Two reviews of largely the same supplement trials locate where extra potassium stops lowering pressure.
They read it on different axes, so their numbers do not line up one to one.

| | Aburto (WHO review) | Filippini (fitted curve) |
|---|---|---|
| Axis | intake (urine x1.3), in bands | excretion at trial end |
| Best or flat region | 90-120 mmol/day intake: SBP −7.16 (−12.41 to −1.91) vs control | flat 90-150 mmol/day excreted (\~117-195 intake), vs 90 |
| Shape test | bands not significantly different | spline only, no non-linearity test |
| Trials | 21 RCTs on SBP, mostly supplements | 32 RCTs, 1,764 people, all supplements |

[@aburto2013]
[@filippini2020potassium]

Aburto found the largest fall when the potassium arm ate 90-120 mmol a day, without a dose gradient,
and saw no added benefit above 120 mmol a day intake; the review did not identify a precise optimum.
[@aburto2013]

Filippini's trials ran at least 4 weeks (median 4), 27 of them in hypertensives.
Against 90 mmol a day excreted, systolic pressure ran 9.1 mmHg higher (4.6 to 13.5) at 30 and 3.9
higher (2.1 to 5.8) at 60, then 0.9 lower (0.2 to 1.6) at 120, 0.2 lower (−1.8 to 2.2) at 150
and 0.7 higher (−2.9 to 4.2) at 180: «The SBP and DBP change remained constant
in the range of 90 to 150 mmol/d of achieved potassium excretion.» They describe the point above 150 as
a weak rise. [@filippini2020potassium] The supplemented
arms excreted 57-225 mmol a day and the control arms 34-94. So the 30-mmol point lies below every control
arm, while the 60-mmol point rests on many trials.
[@filippini2020potassium]

**Put both reviews on one axis and the plateau starts near 117 mmol a day eaten, at the top of Aburto's
best band rather than its bottom.** The WHO and EFSA figure of 90 mmol a day intake works out to about 69
mmol excreted, on the steep part of Filippini's curve. For a hypertensive with normal kidney handling, 90
mmol a day intake is therefore a floor, not the top of the benefit. Intakes in many countries (below
70-80 mmol a day intake) sit lower still. About 120 mmol a day eaten reaches the flat region. Beyond the
start of that region, Filippini shows only one gain: the small further step at 120 mmol excreted.
 [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

This plateau is a region, not a target: it comes from comparisons between trials, mostly in
hypertensives, with no certainty rating. At least 17 of Filippini's 32 trials are reports Aburto also
pooled, and one Filippini author co-wrote two included trials. So the curve adds shape but does not
independently confirm Aburto. [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

### Bigger supplement doses show no further fall, and the rise in pressure at the top doses is unproven

Filippini also read pressure against the gap in 24-hour urinary potassium between the two arms (the
excretion difference, studied from 17 to 131 mmol a day). The largest fall came at a 30 mmol a day
difference, 3.3 mmHg (1.6 to 4.9), then 2.0 (0.5 to 3.4) at 60. At 90 and 120 the estimates turned to
rises of 1.1 (−2.9 to 4.7) and 4.2 (−2.3 to 10.6) mmHg, both intervals including zero.
[@filippini2020potassium] **Push a
potassium supplement past a 30-60 mmol a day excretion difference, and no extra fall has been
demonstrated.** The urinary difference runs below the prescribed dose, so the dose swallowed is higher.
 [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

Filippini call the curve U-shaped, with pressure rising above a difference of about 80 mmol a day.
[@filippini2020potassium] That rise rests on six
trials with 109 participants, all crossover, all in untreated hypertensives or normotensives, three
from one German group. Filippini found the rise by comparing those trials with others, so randomisation
does not protect it, and every reported interval at the top includes zero. The authors concede wide
intervals from few studies at high intakes. [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]], [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]

**No trial has settled whether the rising arm is real: it is no reason to escalate supplements for
blood pressure, and no evidence of harm either.** Filippini advises that treated hypertensives with high
intakes should not exceed the optimum. That advice is a subgroup hypothesis drawn between trials. A
within-trial comparison of three or more supplement doses above 60 mmol a day would settle it (the Gaps
section below).
[@filippini2020potassium]

All of this assumes the kidneys clear potassium normally. In trials of such adults, extra potassium
showed no adverse effect on kidney function, blood lipids or catecholamines, which Aburto calls high
quality evidence. But people with impaired urinary potassium excretion risk hyperkalaemia, and the
trials left them out. The kidney screen in the Salt substitutes section below draws the same line.
[@aburto2013]

### The 1:1 sodium-to-potassium ratio is untested, and the two electrolytes may interact

Aburto notes that 90 mmol a day of potassium intake plus under 2 g of sodium (5 g salt) gives a molar
ratio near one to one, which a 2003 WHO/FAO report called beneficial. The review did not grade that
claim, and the evidence on changing both at once is two studies, not a meta-analysis. **No graded
evidence tests the ratio as a target.**
[@aburto2013]
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

Potassium did lower pressure more in trials where people ate over 4 g of sodium (10 g salt) a day:
systolic pressure fell 6.91 mmHg (2.29 to 11.53), against 1.97 (0.52 to 3.41) at 2-4 g. That
difference was not significant and comes from comparing different trials, so it is a hypothesis.
[@aburto2013] Filippini's curve leans the same way on
largely the same trials, so it does not independently confirm the pattern. If the pattern is real,
cutting sodium and adding potassium partly substitute for each other on pressure: doing both still beats either, but their effects should not be added, and potassium advice
should not be tailored to a person's salt intake on this basis.
 [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

## Salt substitutes: one large Chinese trial cut strokes and deaths, and the pooled reviews mostly count that trial again

A salt substitute replaces part of the sodium chloride in household salt with potassium chloride, so it
lowers sodium and raises potassium at the same time. **Among sodium-related levers, only this one has had
its effect on strokes and deaths counted in a large randomised trial:** SSaSS. Two later reviews pooled
SSaSS with smaller trials. But SSaSS carries most of the weight in every hard-outcome pool they report,
so they restate its answer rather than confirm it from a second source. [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]

### SSaSS: about 5 fewer deaths per 1,000 people per year, in a very high-risk group

SSaSS randomised 600 villages in rural China, 20,995 people in all, to a substitute of 75% sodium chloride
and 25% potassium chloride or to regular salt, and followed them for 4.74 years. Entrants had a prior
stroke, or were aged 60 or over with high blood pressure: 72.6% had had a stroke, mean BP was 154/89 and
mean age was 65. Assessors judged events blind to assignment.
[@neal2021]

| Outcome | Substitute vs regular salt, per 1,000 people per year | Rate ratio (95% CI) | Difference, per 1,000 people per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stroke (primary outcome) | 29.14 vs 33.65 | 0.86 (0.77 to 0.96) | 4.5 fewer |
| Major cardiovascular events | 49.09 vs 56.29 | 0.87 (0.80 to 0.94) | 7.2 fewer |
| Death from any cause | 39.28 vs 44.61 | 0.88 (0.82 to 0.95) | 5.3 fewer |

[@neal2021]

**The absolute gains are large because the participants were at very high risk.** The same 12-14%
relative reduction would prevent far fewer events per 1,000 in a lower-risk group (route a,
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]). The substitute lowered 24-hour urinary
sodium by 15.2 mmol (350 mg sodium, about 0.9 g salt) and raised urinary potassium excretion by 20.6 mmol
(803 mg). Systolic pressure fell 3.34 mmHg (2.18 to 4.51). Australia's National Health and Medical
Research Council funded the trial, and a company, Jiangsu Sinokone Technology, donated the substitute for
years 3 and 4. [@neal2021]

### The benefit belongs to the substitute, not to sodium alone

**SSaSS moved both electrolytes at once (−15.2 mmol sodium, +20.6 mmol potassium in the urine), so its
event results cannot be credited to sodium reduction alone.** The authors state that the two electrolytes
lower blood pressure independently and act synergistically. They read the trial as «reassurance about
the efficacy and safety of sodium-intake reduction» for preventing cardiovascular events and death. The
trial tested one formulation of a salt that changes both electrolytes. It does not separate the two, and
it says nothing about the best sodium-to-potassium ratio ([[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]).
[@neal2021] [inferred from @neal2021]

**Strokes fell slightly more than the blood-pressure drop predicts, but within the trial's error.** Drug
trials show about 13% fewer strokes per 5 mmHg of systolic fall, so 3.34 mmHg predicts a stroke rate ratio
near 0.91. SSaSS observed 0.86 (0.77 to 0.96), an interval that contains 0.91
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Neal's authors call the stroke effect «in keeping
with our power estimates at trial inception» and consistent with «a mechanism of action mediated through
blood-pressure reduction». [@neal2021]

If there is an excess, three explanations fit, and the trial establishes none. Potassium may act through
a route other than blood pressure. The measured 3.34 mmHg may understate the long-run fall, because
incomplete adherence and some substitute use in the control group probably attenuated the effects, as
Neal says. Or blood pressure, measured yearly in a targeted subgroup of villages, may be a noisy
estimate. The potassium stroke cohorts show a similar gap, also within error (the Potassium section
above).
[@neal2021] [inferred from @neal2021; @bplttc2021]

**SSaSS does not test very low sodium intake.** Its participants' mean 24-hour urinary sodium was 4.3 g
(187 mmol; about 11 g salt). On its face that falls in the 4-5 g band where the PURE cohorts saw the lowest
risk. But PURE estimated intake from a spot urine, not a 24-hour collection, so the two figures are not
the same quantity, and SSaSS's place on PURE's curve is undetermined.
[@neal2021] [@mente2016pure] -> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]]

Lai pooled the 10 trials that measured urinary sodium: a potassium salt lowered it by only 19.33 mmol a

### Lai's network meta-analysis: the mortality benefit rests on SSaSS again, and the result says little beyond Chinese-style diets

(25-40% potassium chloride) and its low-sodium node (60-79% sodium chloride). **Lai's absolute effects are
modelled, not observed:** it applied the pooled ratios to control-arm baselines taken from statin trials,
set at 5-10% (intermediate) and 20-30% (high) five-year cardiovascular risk.

**SSaSS supplies about 87% of the participants in Lai's mortality pool** (20,995 of 24,106 people). The

## Guidelines: WHO's 2 g and the Nordic 2.3 g are policy lines on one curve, not measured optima

A guideline body cannot ask about one person's risk or kidneys, so it writes one number for everyone. The
three guidance families this guide draws on set their sodium lines close together (sodium in g a day,
salt in parentheses):

| Body | Line | What kind of number | What the body's text attaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| WHO 2012 | <2 g sodium a day (5 g salt) | chronic-disease reduction target | *strong*; Annex 8 calls the <2 g evidence high and moderate quality |
| NNR 2023 | <=2.3 g sodium a day (about 5.75 g salt) | chronic-disease risk-reduction limit, adapted from NASEM | BP falls with sodium down to below 2 g; cohorts link higher intake to stroke and CV events; trials show reduction below 2 g is safe (citing Jula 2023, which this guide does not draw on) |
| NNR 2023 | 1.5 g sodium a day (3.75 g salt) | adequate intake, a deficiency-side reference | a different construct, not a reduction target |
| ESC 2021 | at most 5 g **salt** a day | population maximum | Class I, Level A (He/MacGregor-sourced); optimal intake «might be as low as 3 g/day» of salt; set against Western intakes of 9-10 g salt |

[@who2012] [@who2012]
[@nnr2023]
[@esc2021] [@esc2021]

**WHO makes its under-2 g line a strong recommendation, and grades the evidence behind it at more than
one certainty level.** WHO writes: «WHO
recommends a reduction to <2 g/day sodium (5 g/day salt) in adults (strong recommendation).»
[@who2012] Its Annex 8 summary reads: «High and moderate-quality
evidence that consuming <2 g sodium/ day compared with consuming ≥2 g sodium/day is beneficial for reducing
blood pressure and risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke and coronary heart disease in adults.»
[@who2012] The blood-pressure half rests on direct trials,
including the 3.47 mmHg comparison rated high (the Blood pressure section above). The event half rests on
the surrogate step from blood pressure to events described in the Events section above.
-> [[Certainty of Evidence vs Strength of Recommendation]]

**WHO's evidence table held no direct test of the 2 g threshold against events.** It found none for
cardiovascular disease, stroke, coronary heart disease or death. A footnote adds 5 cohorts that compared
intake below 2 g with intake at or above it. Stroke risk was higher in the higher group, RR 1.30
(1.03-1.64), and the other outcomes did not differ significantly
[@who2012].

**WHO chose one line for everyone on a mainly public-health ground.** It measured a roughly threefold
smaller blood-pressure fall in people with normal pressure (the Blood pressure section above) and wrote:
«Although the reduction in blood pressure was statistically significantly less in individuals without
hypertension, it was still evident.» It then cited the high global prevalence of hypertension and the
breadth of benefit, and declined to stratify
[@who2012]. That is a fair stance for a
body advising a whole population. It says nothing about how much the line buys a low-risk individual
 -> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**The Nordic 2.3 g and WHO's 2 g sit on the same curve; the two bodies drew their lines 0.3 g apart.**
NNR sets its chronic-disease limit at 2.3 g, adapting the reasoning of the US National Academies
(NASEM). It states that blood pressure falls in a linear dose-response down to below 2 g a day
[@nnr2023]. Both bodies read the same slope and
picked different cutpoints. NNR's evidence base overlaps WHO's, so it confirms the direction rather than
adding independent confirmation
[inferred from @nnr2023].

**None of these numbers is a measured optimum.** Start from the hypothesis that any cutpoint marks where
the evidence thins; 2 g fits that reading. In He's meta-analysis of trials lasting at least four weeks,
the typical low arm sat at about 78-87 mmol a day, roughly 1.8-2.0 g sodium
. The few trials longer than four weeks that measured side effects
cut sodium only to levels above 87 mmol a day
[@graudal2020sodium]. Shorter trials go lower
without showing a plateau (the No plateau subsection above). Whether events rise below about 3 g is
unsettled (the Low intake section above) ->
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**ESC asks for a population-wide halving.** It sets a maximum of 5 g salt a day, WHO's line in salt
units, against Western intakes of 9-10 g salt [@esc2021].
Whether people can reach a line is a separate question from where the curve sits. Achievability bears on
any population target without being a health finding; this guide names that non-health load and does
not weigh it -> [[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]]. Salt substitutes and population salt
policy also carry cost, taste and equity loads, which this guide likewise names and does not weigh
against the health findings.

**GBD's ranking counts deaths across a whole population, a different sum from what one person gains.**
GBD 2017 attributed about 3 million deaths (95% UI 1-5 million) and 70 million DALYs (34-118) worldwide to
high sodium, level with low whole grains as the largest dietary risk by deaths
[@afshin2019]. Sodium's exposure data are
thin: a representativeness index of 26.2% against 94.9% for most foods, so GBD modelled it from about a
quarter of countries [@afshin2019].

GBD's 2019 cycle also cut the whole diet burden by about 30% (as a share of DALYs) after revising its
methods and adding new systematic reviews [@gbd2020rf].
**GBD multiplies a per-person risk across everyone exposed; this guide's per-person estimates show what
one person can expect** from cutting back -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

## What to do: cut high salt, eat potassium-rich food for high pressure, switch salt after a kidney check

Three levers matter for one person; which of them apply depends on blood pressure, kidney function and
where the salt in the diet comes from. **The table and subsections give each lever an action, a dose
where the evidence holds one, and its basis**; the uncertainty follows the advice. Where no trial pins a
dose, the guide offers a *labelled default*: a starting point this guide proposes, with its basis stated beside it.
Do not add the blood-pressure figures across levers. They come from different trials against different
comparators, and DASH-Sodium found the combined effect smaller than the sum (the DASH subsection above)
[@sacks2001dashsodium]
-> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

| Who | Do this | Dose | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| High BP, high sodium intake | Cut sodium | toward 2-2.3 g sodium a day (5-5.75 g salt) | BP trials, high certainty; events inferred from drug trials (the transfer) |
| Normal BP, high CV risk | Cut sodium where intake is high | toward 2-2.3 g | BP trials; absolute benefit grows with baseline risk (route a); events inferred from drug trials (the transfer) |
| Normal BP, low risk | Cutting is optional; it gains little either way | none shown | BP trials; evidence on net effect insufficient |
| High BP, healthy kidneys | Eat more potassium-rich food | toward \~120 mmol a day intake (\~4.7 g); \~90 mmol is a floor | BP trials in hypertensives; food dose a labelled default, not trial-tested as food |
| Normal BP, healthy kidneys | Eat more potassium-rich food (fruit, vegetables, pulses) | no graded dose; \~90-120 mmol a day intake (\~3.5-4.7 g) as a labelled default | stroke cohorts (low certainty) and Filippini's ungraded BP optimum |
| High intake, salt added at home, high BP or high risk | Switch home salt to a \~25% KCl substitute after the kidney screen | the salt you add yourself | one Chinese trial for events |

### With high blood pressure and a high intake, cut sodium

People with hypertension who eat a lot of salt should eat less of it. **Both camps in the low-intake
dispute agree on this direction**, including the review most skeptical of population-wide cuts: «In
white people with elevated BP, sodium reduction decreases BP by about 3.5%, indicating that sodium
reduction may be used as a supplementary treatment for hypertension.»
[@graudal2020sodium]. The agreement
stops at direction. The same review adds that hypertensives may also suffer harms from the side
effects: the hormone and lipid rises above, which it found similar with and without hypertension
[@graudal2020sodium]. Expect systolic
pressure to fall by about 4-6 mmHg (the Blood pressure section above)
-> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]].

**ESC points to processed food as the place to cut:** «Salt reduction can be achieved
by dietary choices (fewer processed foods) and the reformulation of foods by lowering their salt
content» [@esc2021].

**No trial pins a target, so the dose is a labelled default**: WHO's under 2 g sodium a day (5 g salt)
or the Nordic 2.3 g (about 5.75 g salt). Both are policy lines drawn on the blood-pressure curve, not a
measured optimum [@who2012]
[@nnr2023].

**Over the range trials covered, each step down from a high intake lowered pressure further**, and no
plateau showed down to about 1.5 g sodium a day (the No plateau subsection above). Below about 3 g a day
the event effect is unsettled, and both guideline lines sit inside that range (the Low intake section
above) [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].
[@graudal2020sodium]


### With normal blood pressure, the gain is small at low risk and larger at high risk

If intake is high or moderate (3-6 g a day), cutting back lowers systolic pressure by about 1 to 2.4
mmHg [@graudal2020sodium]
[@he2013]. **That is a small lever whichever side of the low-intake dispute turns out right**, and it stays
small even if the hormone and lipid rises fade completely. The evidence on the net health effect is
insufficient. The blood-pressure fall comes with marker rises that no source this guide draws on
translates into outcomes, and none of them has settled whether going below about 3 g a day helps or harms.

A low-risk normotensive can take this as licence to stop optimizing sodium: the remaining gain is small
because the fall in pressure is small and the baseline risk is low
 -> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]],
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**At high cardiovascular risk, the same fall is worth more.** The drug trials found the same relative
benefit per 5 mmHg below 120 mmHg as above it (the Events section above). The same relative benefit
avoids more events when baseline risk is high. A normotensive at high risk who eats a lot of salt has a
reason to cut toward 2-2.3 g. The event benefit still rests on the transfer from drug trials; it is not
shown for sodium alone -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

### With high blood pressure and healthy kidneys, eat more potassium-rich food

For a hypertensive with normal kidney function who takes no potassium-sparing drug, **raise food
potassium from fresh fruit, vegetables and pulses toward about 120 mmol a day of intake (about 4.7 g)**,
where Filippini's plateau begins. About 90 mmol is a floor on the steep part of the curve (the Potassium
section above). Food works as the vehicle: «Increasing potassium intake through dietary
supplements and through dietary changes both reduced systolic blood pressure, suggesting that the
vehicle of increased potassium intake can be food and does not have to be a supplement.»
[@aburto2013].

Aburto adds: «Food processing reduces the potassium content of food, and a diet high in processed foods
and low in fresh fruits and vegetables is often lacking in potassium.»
[@aburto2013]
Cut processed food, then, and you move both levers: less sodium and, through the fresh food that
replaces it, more potassium.

Basis: the blood-pressure effect in hypertensives is high certainty (the Potassium section above); the
food dose is a labelled default. The dose curve comes from supplement trials, mostly in hypertensives,
that ran a median of 4 weeks, and its shape comes from comparing trials with each other. So the food
route rests on three advice trials and the stroke cohorts
[inferred from @filippini2020potassium]
-> [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**Do not escalate potassium supplements on blood-pressure grounds.** The fall did not grow past a
30-60 mmol a day rise in urinary potassium excreted
[@filippini2020potassium].
With normal blood pressure, raising potassium had no meaningful effect on lowering it. So the stroke
cohorts' direction supplies the reason to eat more potassium-rich food; Aburto's GRADE table rates that
direction low and its abstract rates it moderate
[@aburto2013].

**With normal blood pressure and healthy kidneys, eat more fruit, vegetables and pulses.** That is a
low-harm default. No source this guide draws on grades a dose for this group, but two ungraded figures
point to one. Aburto's stroke cohorts did best when the comparison group ate 90-120 mmol a day (RR 0.70,
0.56-0.88), though not significantly better than below 90 mmol (0.82, 0.71-0.93); the cohorts were not
split by blood pressure [@aburto2013].
Filippini's discussion finds normotensives' small blood-pressure fall largest at about 4,500 mg of total
intake, a between-trial point estimate with no interval [@filippini2020potassium].
So about 90-120 mmol a day eaten (about 3.5-4.7 g) serves as a labelled default
 -> [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

With kidney disease or a potassium-sparing drug, the mechanism predicts a hyperkalaemia risk from the
same exposure; the trials excluded those people
[inferred from @aburto2013].

### In a high-salt, home-cooking setting, switch to a potassium salt after the kidney check

For an adult who eats a lot of salt, adds most of it at home, and has hypertension or high
cardiovascular risk, **switch the salt added at home to a \~25% KCl substitute**. Do it after the screen
in the Before switching callout above
[inferred from @lai2026salt; @neal2021]
-> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

At intermediate risk (5-10% five-year cardiovascular-event risk), Lai estimates **about 7 fewer deaths
per 1,000 over 5 years (between 3 and 11 fewer), moderate certainty**, on modelled baselines borrowed
from statin-trial control arms
[@lai2026salt]
[@lai2026salt].
The relative effect is mostly SSaSS's, so the figure applies one Chinese trial's result to a modelled
baseline. The absolute gain is larger at high risk (the Salt substitutes section above).
Basis: one Chinese trial for hard outcomes.

**Choose the \~25% KCl formulation.** It is the one with hard-outcome evidence, and a higher-KCl product has not been shown to add blood-pressure benefit
[inferred from @neal2021; @lai2026salt; @brand2022lsss].

**Outside that setting, this guide offers the switch as a labelled default.** For a screened adult, a
potassium-enriched substitute for the salt they add themselves is still a low-harm blood-pressure lever.
Expect less where most sodium arrives in processed or restaurant food, and expect nothing proven on
deaths: no trial outside China measured mortality

-> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]. For someone with normal blood pressure, the evidence on the
substitute's blood-pressure effect is insufficient, which is not a null
[@brand2022lsss].

### Kidney-stone formers with high urinary sodium: keep salt at 4-5 g a day at most

Dose and basis are in the Kidneys subsection above. A stone former whose pressure a drug controls still

### Measuring intake and pressure: repeat the 24-hour urine, and compare pressures only when measured the same way

**A 24-hour urine, if a clinician orders it, measures absolute sodium intake**, so it is one of the few
ways to check a person against a guideline cutpoint such as 2 g. One collection covers one day, a poor
estimate of usual intake, so repeat collections are needed: «multiple 24-hour collections are needed due
to the large within-person variability»
[@willett]
[@willett]
[inferred from @willett; @who2012].
Office and ambulatory readings are not interchangeable: compare a reading only with a threshold measured
by the same method [@esc2021]

### Sea and Himalayan salt carry the same sodium, and switching from iodized salt can cut iodine

Gram for gram, sea and Himalayan salt deliver the same sodium as table salt
[inferred from @who2012].
**Switching away from iodized table salt can lower iodine intake** unless the replacement is iodized
. WHO treats the two policies as one: «These recommendations recognize that salt reduction
and salt iodization are compatible.» [@who2012]
-> [[Excess Iodine Intake and Thyroid Disease]].

### In low-risk mild hypertension, a drug and these levers both buy little

For a low-risk stage-1 hypertensive, a drug and these levers both buy little in absolute terms;
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]] makes the comparison.

## Gaps: what the evidence cannot yet tell you

The evidence this guide draws on leaves a few holes under the actions above. Two of them shape the whole
guide. The rest bound narrower choices: who may switch to a salt substitute, how far the substitute's
results apply beyond China, and how much potassium to add.

**Only two small RCTs make up the one graded event pool for sodium reduction alone, and that pool was
inconclusive** (RR 0.84, 0.57-1.23, about 720 people) [@who2012].
The He/MacGregor re-analysis of six trials (20% fewer cardiovascular events) is ungraded and comes from
one lineage [@he2013]. Every graded trial result
showing fewer events comes from a potassium salt substitute (SSaSS, and the Lai and Brand reviews that
re-pool it). A substitute moves both electrolytes at once
[@neal2021] (the Events section above;
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]).

**What would close it:** an RCT, or a meta-analysis of RCTs, of sodium reduction alone that is powered
for events. An SSaSS analysis this guide does not yet draw on,, apportions the trial's blood-pressure
fall between the two electrolytes ([[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]). It would partly separate
the two electrolytes' effects on blood pressure; it would not measure sodium's share of the events.

**No source this guide draws on has tested whether the extra events seen below about 3 g of sodium a day
(about 7.5 g salt) are real.** Neither camp has run a strong check (the Low intake section above;
[[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]).

**What would close it:** any one of a referent-corrected analysis, repeated 24-hour urine collections
per person, a Mendelian-randomization instrument on the low-intake arm, or a large long-term RCT of
sodium *level* rather than reduction ([[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]]).
