Of the cardiometabolic interventions the wiki holds, **BP-lowering is the one whose hard-outcome benefit
is proven to reach primary prevention** (in BPLTTC's older, \~3%/yr-risk primary-prevention population;
transported, not directly shown, in low-risk untreated stage 1 — *The low-risk tail* below) — the partial exception to
[[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]. The largest randomised
evidence base (BPLTTC 2021, «344 716 participants from 48 randomised clinical trials») largely settles two long-
contested questions: the benefit does **not** require pre-existing heart disease, and it does **not**
vanish at lower baseline blood pressure. [@bplttc2021]
The second is held less firmly in one corner, people without CVD in the lower two thirds of one trial's baseline SBP (143.5 mmHg or less at a baseline visit; the authors' \~140 tracks the upper third's mean usual SBP, not a converted boundary), where one
prespecified within-trial test points the other way (*The intermediate-risk modern-drug trial* below)
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3].

</div>

<div class="recent-update" data-last-updated="2026-10-26">

## The effect, and why its independence from baseline is the point

> «a 5 mm Hg reduction of systolic blood pressure reduced the risk of major cardiovascular events by
> about 10%, irrespective of previous diagnoses of cardiovascular disease, and even at normal or
> high–normal blood pressure values».

Per 5 mmHg SBP reduction, the hazard ratio for a major CV event was **0.91 (0.89-0.94) in people without
prior CVD** and **0.89 (0.86-0.92) in those with it** [@bplttc2021]. Across seven baseline-SBP strata from <120 to >=170
mmHg there was «no reliable evidence of heterogeneity», and «in no subgroup was the HR above 1·0».
Component reductions per 5 mmHg: stroke 13%, heart failure 13%, ischaemic heart disease 8%, CV death 5%
(«13%, 13%, 8%, and 5%, respectively»). [@bplttc2021]

**This is what distinguishes BP-lowering from the other cardiometabolic levers.** Lifestyle weight loss
was null on hard events even where it was achieved ([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]);
an obesity drug's CV benefit is unproven outside established disease
([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]); a statin's is real but decays to below the
action threshold at low risk ([[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]). BP-lowering
alone has a **directly proven relative benefit in primary prevention**, at an IPD-meta-analytic warrant.
*Scoped 2026-10-26 (Wang):* proven in BPLTTC's primary-prevention population (mean age 65, comparator
31.9 events per 1000 person-years); in the younger, untreated, low-risk stage-1 tail the direct trials are
low-certainty and underpowered, so there the benefit is a transport of the relative effect, not a direct
finding -> *The low-risk tail* below. [inferred from @wang2025mildhtn; @bplttc2021]

</div>

## But the ceiling's mechanism survives — treat on absolute risk, not a BP number

The paper does **not** conclude *lower everyone's BP*. Its explicit decision rule is the
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]] applied to blood pressure:

> physicians should «emphasise its importance on reducing cardiovascular risk rather than focusing on
> blood pressure reduction itself»; the finding «calls for consideration of blood pressure-lowering
> treatment for any individual who has a sufficiently high absolute risk of cardiovascular disease».

And the guard against over-reading, in the authors' own words: the uniform *relative* effect «does not
necessarily mean that it is worthwhile treating every patient» — decisions «will require consideration
of... an individual's overall risk of future cardiovascular events, potential risk of adverse effects,
the cost of treatment, and patient preferences». They even «caution against using the reported absolute
risk differences from clinical trials for making policy decisions» — the trials' populations were
higher-risk (absolute rates «31·9... in the comparator group and 25·9... in the intervention group» per
1000 person-years without prior CVD, i.e. a \~3.2%/yr comparator-group baseline, far above a low-risk primary-prevention
person). [@bplttc2021]

**So BP-lowering refines, not refutes, the ceiling.** Its *relative* benefit is proven where weight-loss
and GLP-1 CV benefit are not — but the *absolute* benefit still scales with baseline risk, exactly the
ceiling's mechanism, and the paper legislates the same rule (treat on absolute risk). A constant \~10% per
5 mmHg on a low absolute risk is a small absolute gain; on a high one it is large. That scaling is an
inference from a constant relative effect applied to different baseline risks, not a between-stratum
finding of this paper.

**BPLTTC's own absolute-scale result does not show it across its strata.** The paper states «The lack of
heterogeneity of treatment effects was observed on the relative as well as absolute risk scales», and its
crude event rates per 1000 person-years show a *smaller* gap with prior CVD (39·7 comparator vs 36·0
intervention) than without (31·9 vs 25·9). [@bplttc2021]
Those crude rates pool trials with different achieved BP reductions and follow-up, so they are not
per-5-mmHg absolute effects; what the paper supports is that prior-CVD status and baseline SBP did not
sort people into larger and smaller absolute gains in these trials, consistent with its point that
single risk factors of modest strength discriminate risk poorly. The constant-relative-effect arithmetic
still applies across the much wider risk range between a trial population and a low-risk
primary-prevention person, which these strata do not span; that is the paper's own
prescription: «Measures of absolute risk are better obtained from representative populations, which can
then be combined with relative effects from our randomised comparisons to model population-specific
absolute risk reductions». [@bplttc2021]

</div>

## What it does not settle

- **Not the target.** BPLTTC standardises to a *fixed 5 mmHg reduction* and shows a consistent *relative*
  effect; it «is unable to directly address» the optimal magnitude or a target BP. How low to go is a
  separate question — *now partly held via SPRINT below*.
- **Not the drug/adverse-effect trade-off** for an individual — adverse effects, cost and preferences are
  named as decision inputs but not quantified — *SPRINT supplies the harm side, below*.

[@bplttc2021]

## SPRINT — the intensive-target trial that operationalises *treat on absolute risk*

BPLTTC is the meta-analysis and carries the per-mmHg claim; **SPRINT is one landmark RCT**, read *inside*
that framing as the worked target-trial. It randomised 9,361 **high-CV-risk, non-diabetic** hypertensives
to a systolic target «less than 120 mm Hg (intensive)» vs «less than 140 mm Hg (standard)» (achieved
\~121 vs \~136), and stopped early (median 3.26 y):

- **Intensive won on hard outcomes:** primary composite (MI/ACS/stroke/HF/CV-death) «hazard ratio ...
  0.75; 95% CI 0.64 to 0.89»; and **all-cause mortality «hazard ratio, 0.73; 95% CI 0.60 to 0.90»** — a
  25% and 27% relative reduction, «number needed to treat ... to prevent one primary outcome event was
  61» over 3.26 years. [@sprint2015]
- **It confirms BPLTTC's own logic rather than competing with it.** SPRINT selected a *high-risk*
  population — exactly where BPLTTC says the *absolute* benefit of a given relative effect is largest —
  and the \~15 mmHg achieved gap, at BPLTTC's \~0.91 per 5 mmHg, predicts a composite HR near 0.75, which
  is what SPRINT observed.
- **The harm side BPLTTC did not quantify:** intensive treatment raised serious «hypotension, syncope,
  electrolyte abnormalities, and acute kidney injury or failure, but not ... injurious falls».
  [@sprint2015] So a lower target buys real
  events in the high-risk but at a real adverse-event cost — the target decision is a net-effect
  judgement, not *lower is always better*.

</div>

## A refutation of the observational J-curve

The intro records the standing controversy: registries show a «J-shaped association» between BP and
events (lowest risk \~130/75, implying harm below), which had been read as a reason not to lower BP far.
The randomised evidence shows benefit «down to less than 120 mm Hg» with no rising HR in any low-BP
stratum — so the observational J-curve is not a treatment effect. [@bplttc2021] A worked instance of
[[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]: the protective-looking lower arm of an observational curve does
not survive the randomised (here, interventional) check.

**A randomised counter-signal in one corner `[2026-10-27, Lonn]`.** HOPE-3, which is not in the BPLTTC
pools (published after their 1972-2013 data window), found a nominally significant trend across thirds of baseline SBP, with a lowest-third HR of 1.16
(0.82-1.63) at a mean SBP of 122, and its authors read this as support for «a J-curve phenomenon» for
major CV events other than stroke [@lonn2016hope3].
This is a different object from the observational J-curve above: a within-trial gradient in the
treatment effect, not a risk curve of untreated BP. It is also not the only report of its kind. BPLTTC's
own introduction calls the tabular meta-analyses on this question conflicting, one of them reporting that
treatment «does not reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease in people without previous cardiovascular
disease when systolic blood pressure is less than 140 mm Hg.» [@bplttc2021]
Lonn cites diabetes meta-analyses showing «a potential for harm» below 140 [@lonn2016hope3].
Neither analysis is held, the first may well include HOPE-3, and BPLTTC argues that tabular
meta-analyses «are limited in cross-stratification of people, which is at the core of this controversy»
[@bplttc2021],
so these are not independent replications. HOPE-3 is the one held within-trial signal in largely
non-diabetic primary prevention. Its lowest-third interval spans benefit and harm and stroke showed no
significant trend, so it does not overturn BPLTTC's pooled no-modification finding; but the no-CVD corner
of HOPE-3's lower two SBP thirds (143.5 mmHg or less at baseline, a tertile cut rather than a located
threshold) is contested, not cleanly refuted. Detail and the parameter table: *The
intermediate-risk modern-drug trial* below.

## A dietary BP-lowering route that reached hard outcomes — SSaSS `[2026-08-04, Neal]`

The page's caution below is that a **lifestyle-induced** BP drop transmitting to events is *an assumption,
not the same evidence* as the pharmacological trials here. SSaSS (Neal 2021) is the closest test the
corpus holds: a **potassium-enriched salt substitute** (a dietary intervention) cut stroke (rate ratio 0.86),
major CV events (0.87) and death (0.88) in 20,995 high-risk adults, with an achieved SBP fall of
**−3.34 mmHg**. [@neal2021] So a
diet-route BP reduction *can* reduce hard events — but two caveats keep it from being a clean confirmation
of the sodium→events chain: (1) the salt substitute moves **sodium and potassium together** (a joint
lever, not sodium alone — [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]); (2) the **event benefit outruns the
BP-channel prediction** — this page's \~13% stroke reduction per 5 mmHg predicts RR \~0.91 from −3.34 mmHg,
while SSaSS observed 0.86, consistent with a BP-independent electrolyte effect *or* the measured BP fall
understating the sustained one (adherence dilution). The BPLTTC transmission model is corroborated in
*direction*; SSaSS does not let the diet-route magnitude be read straight off the BP fall.
[inferred from @neal2021]

**Across Lai's 34-trial NMA (7 reporting all-cause mortality), the hard-outcome warrant is still mostly SSaSS's
`[2026-10-26, Lai]`.** Lai's network meta-analysis puts the SSaSS-band substitute (25-40% KCl) at SBP
**-4.64 mmHg (-6.05 to -3.24; 18 trials, moderate certainty)** and all-cause mortality **7 fewer per 1000
over 5 years at intermediate risk (3 to 11; moderate) and 16 fewer at high risk (7 to 24)** (Lai's abstract grades these figures *moderate to high* across nodes;
the held tables do not separate this node's own grade), the per-1000 figures being pooled RRs applied to statin-trial (CTT) control-arm baselines, with
intermediate = 5-10% and high = 20-30% five-year CV-event risk. These are transports: the relative effect
is mostly SSaSS's, measured at a control death rate of 44.61 per 1000 person-years (roughly 200
per 1000 over 5 years [inferred from @neal2021]; 72.6% with prior stroke) [@neal2021],
above both of Lai's all-cause control baselines (62 and 143 per 1000 over 5 years) [@lai2026salt], and applied to them on the
assumption that the relative effect holds. Without SSaSS, all-cause mortality lost
significance (point estimates 4 and 10 fewer per 1000), while CV mortality stayed significant (9 and 29
fewer — larger than with SSaSS, from the one or two small Chinese trials left, which reads as fragility or
small-study inflation rather than robustness), and no non-Chinese trial reported mortality.
[@lai2026salt]
So the diet route's *measured* endpoint rests on Chinese trials, mainly SSaSS, now with a GRADE label that
Lai itself scopes to «the populations actually studied» (detail and the Lai-vs-Neal parameter table:
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]). On the drug side, the measured endpoint is a 344,716-person IPD
base, including secondary prevention. Salt substitution has both transport problems: setting (where the
salt comes from) and baseline risk (Lai's per-1000 figures assume 5-10% or 20-30% five-year CV risk, above
the low-risk tail). Drugs have the baseline-risk one (*The low-risk tail* below). Not a ranking.
[inferred from @lai2026salt; @bplttc2021]
**Harms, as far as they can be set beside the drug harms below.** Lai found no significant increase in
adverse events overall (moderate certainty for most formulations, on 5 trials with estimable AEs); for the
25-40% KCl node, adverse events 7 more per 1000 (-11 to 38; 2 trials) and withdrawals for any reason 18
more per 1000 (-14 to 59; 15 trials), neither significant. Withdrawals due to adverse events (10 in 3,311
participants) were too few to pool, so no figure here matches Wang's +87 per 1000 (not the same
quantity). Lai did not analyse hyperkalaemia, and says «Clinicians should first rule out underlying kidney problems and
hyperkalaemia risk before recommending salt substitutes», naming potassium-sparing diuretics and diabetes
as risk groups. [@lai2026salt]
SSaSS excluded persons if they or a household member had a contraindication, where «contraindications
included use of a potassium-sparing diuretic, use of a potassium supplement» or known serious kidney
disease, with no kidney-function test; 22.8% of participants were on an ACE inhibitor or ARB. Its harm
estimate: serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalaemia 3.35 vs 3.30 per 1000 person-years, rate
ratio 1.04 (0.80 to 1.37) — but with no routine serum potassium measurement, only 2 definite or probable
events, and possible events added by a post hoc decision.
[@neal2021] Route (c) detail:
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**The Cochrane review's harm rows, held to the same standard `[2026-10-26, Brand]`.** Brand 2022 (26 RCTs,
WHO-commissioned) pools what Lai leaves out. Its verdict on hyperkalaemia: «In adult participants,
meta‐analyses showed that LSSS likely result in little to no difference in hyperkalaemia.» The row is RR
1.04 (0.46 to 2.38), 88 vs 91 per 100,000 (4 more, 47 fewer to 121 more), 5 trials, 22,849 people; blood potassium +0.12 mmol/L (0.07 to
0.18), 6 trials, 784; both moderate certainty, downgraded once for risk of bias. Other adverse events were
25/1094 (2.3%) vs 14/1015 (1.4%) across 8 trials, too diverse to pool, very low certainty; hypokalaemia was
reported by one 22-person trial in people on potassium-depleting diuretics (zero events, not estimable,
very low certainty); no trial reported
hyponatraemia. [@brand2022lsss]
Brand adds that the interval «included important benefit and important harm» and that all of the
information «came from two trials in participants judged to be at possible and unclear risk of
hyperkalaemia» [@brand2022lsss]. Read with the
page's expectancy test, as for Wang below, this page reads the hyperkalaemia row as closer to
insufficient evidence than a shown null: about 92% of its participants are SSaSS's (20,995 of 22,849), a trial in which «Routine serum
potassium measurements were not performed» [@neal2021], so the narrow absolute interval comes from SSaSS's denominator, while most of the
events come from one high-risk-of-bias nursing-home trial with no reported exclusions; the relative
interval crosses both of Brand's importance thresholds, and Brand did not downgrade it for imprecision. Brand did not downgrade the benefit rows for imprecision
either, though their intervals reach no effect (CV mortality 0.60 to 1.00); unlike the hyperkalaemia
row's \~20 events they clear Brand's stated imprecision trigger of «300 events» for dichotomous outcomes
[@brand2022lsss], and they do not span important harm (event arithmetic on
[[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]).
Its RR 1.04 is not SSaSS's rate ratio 1.04 above: a different event definition and about 20 events against
315 (the parameter table is on [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]). Set beside the drug rows (SPRINT's
serious adverse events above; Wang's withdrawals below, low certainty and mostly 1980s regimens), the harm
quantities differ on every side (incremental-target serious adverse events, tolerability withdrawals,
biochemical hyperkalaemia) and so does ascertainment, and both sets of trials screened their entrants. The
one roughly parallel quantity, the substitute's crude other-adverse-event split, leans the same way as the
drug excess, at very low certainty and unpooled. So neither *the substitute is safer* nor *less is known,
so it is riskier* is shown by these numbers; for the screened-out strata (kidney disease,
potassium-sparing drugs, potassium supplements) the substitute is contraindicated, as SSaSS itself listed
them [@brand2022lsss], on the mechanism that people with impaired
urinary potassium excretion «can be at risk of hyperkalaemia» [@aburto2013].
The trial evidence there is insufficient, not shown safe and not measured as harm.
[inferred from @brand2022lsss]

## Another lifestyle BP route — alcohol reduction, and the modelled-events gap made explicit `[2026-08-20, Roerecke]`

Roerecke's 2017 SR+MA of **36 alcohol-reduction RCTs (2865 participants)**, GRADE-high, is a second
behavioural BP lever — and it makes this page's *lifestyle-BP-drop-to-events-is-an-assumption* caveat
concrete. The BP effect is real and dose-dependent but **shown only in heavier drinkers**: no significant
BP change at 2 drinks/day or fewer, then dose-dependent above (SBP -1.18 at 3 drinks/day rising to
-5.50 [-6.70 to -4.30] at >=6 drinks/day; overall -3.13 [-3.93 to -2.32]).
[@roerecke2017alcohol]
*Qualified 2026-10-22:* the <=2 drinks/day band (SBP -0.18, -1.02 to 0.66) shows no fall, not a zero; it
pools five trials, by weight mostly Mori 2015 (a 4-week crossover).
[@roerecke2017alcohol]
A dose-response meta-analysis of seven cohorts finds SBP rose 1.25 mmHg (0.49 to 2.01) more over 4-12 years
of follow-up at a baseline intake of 12 g/day than with no alcohol.
[@difederico2023alcbp]
The trials measure BP level after weeks to months of changed intake, the cohorts the difference in BP change
over 4-12 years by baseline intake; matched on quantity the two do not clash, unless the cohort difference is
read as a level effect that stopping would reverse within weeks, which neither source shows; even then
the SBP intervals overlap and a clash appears only on overall DBP -> [[Does Light Drinking Raise Blood Pressure]]. The magnitude is «similar to that
of other health behaviour changes, such as physical activity» or weight-loss diets.
[@roerecke2017alcohol]

Crucially, Roerecke did **not measure** CV events — it **modelled** them: its «more than 7000 inpatient
hospitalisations and 678 cardiovascular deaths» projected for the UK rest on «standard methods for
comparative risk assessment analyses (eg, Global Burden of Disease studies)», run «assuming persistence
of lower alcohol consumption within 1 year, and no lag time of eﬀ ects on CVD outcomes».
[@roerecke2017alcohol] So the
alcohol-reduction lever is exactly the case this page flags — a lifestyle BP drop whose event payoff is
*assumed through a risk model*, not observed — unlike SSaSS, the one lifestyle route that reached
*measured* hard endpoints. Read against BPLTTC's \~0.91 per 5 mmHg, a -5.50 mmHg fall in a heavy drinker
predicts a \~10% CV-event reduction *if* the pharmacological transmission transfers. Full dose-response
-> [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]].
[inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol]

## A third lifestyle BP route — structured exercise, and the drug head-to-head that does not exist `[2026-08-20, Naci]`

Naci's 2019 network meta-analysis (Br J Sports Med, gold-tier) is the drug-as-realistic-alternative
comparator the telos calls for: it places **structured exercise** and **antihypertensive drugs** in
one network on SBP-lowering. It pooled «391 RCTs, 197 of which evaluated exercise interventions
(10 461 participants) and 194 evaluated antihypertensive medications (29 281 participants)» — but the
head-to-head does not exist: «No RCTs compared directly exercise against medications»
[@naci2019exercise]. **Every exercise-vs-drug number
below is INDIRECT** (network-inferred across disjoint trials), and the authors call the linking
assumption «tenuous» (below).

**The naive comparison is confounded by baseline SBP — and the confound is the finding.** Across all
populations, drugs beat exercise: medications «−8.80 mmHg, 95% CrI −9.58 to −8.02» vs exercise «−4.84,
95% CrI −5.55 to −4.13» from baseline vs control, a «−3.96, 95% CrI −5.02 to −2.91» advantage to drugs
[@naci2019exercise]. But the two arms were measured in
**different populations**: «the mean SBP at baseline was 132 mmHg for participants in the RCTs of
exercise interventions, it was consistently over 150 mmHg in medication RCTs»
[@naci2019exercise]. SBP-lowering scales with baseline
SBP, so a drop measured at \~132 mmHg (mostly normotensive) is **not the same quantity** as one measured
at \~157 mmHg (all hypertensive) — the parameter table makes this explicit:

| Naci comparison | Effect vs control (mmHg, 95% CrI) | Population / baseline SBP | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| All exercise | −4.84 (−5.55 to −4.13) | exercise trials, mean baseline 132 | reference |
| All medications | −8.80 (−9.58 to −8.02) | drug trials, baseline >150 | NO — different baseline SBP |
| Meds vs exercise (all pops) | −3.96 (−5.02 to −2.91), drugs superior | mixed (normotensive vs hypertensive) | NO — the confounded, indirect contrast |
| Exercise, restricted to hypertensive >=140 | −8.96 (−10.27 to −7.64) | 56 trials, 3508 people, baseline >=140 | YES — baseline now matched to drugs |
| Meds vs exercise, hypertensive >=140 | 0.18 (−1.35 to 1.68), no difference | both hypertensive >=140 | YES — matched; the honest "similar" claim |

Restricting exercise to the hypertensive stratum nearly **doubles** its estimated effect (−4.84 ->
−8.96) and erases the drug advantage: «We did not observe a difference between the SBP-lowering effects
of medications and exercise (0.18, 95% CrI −1.35 to 1.68)»
[@naci2019exercise]. Naci names the mechanism himself
in the limitations (the counter-passage): «Transitivity may be tenuous given the typically different
levels of SBP in medication versus exercise trials. However, when we tried to match trial populations
more closely, the gap between medication and exercise in efficacy diminished»
[@naci2019exercise]. This is a worked instance of
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]] on a *continuous* outcome — the absolute SBP reduction
is a function of baseline SBP, so an unmatched cross-intervention comparison misleads.
[inferred from @naci2019exercise]

**The equivalence is real but doubly hedged — do not read it as "exercise = drugs" plainly.** The
authors state it only «Assuming equally reliable estimates»
[@naci2019exercise], and the estimates are not equally
reliable: the exercise evidence is far sparser in the matched stratum (56 trials / 3508 people vs 194
drug trials / 29 281), higher risk of bias (17/20 sampled exercise RCTs at high risk of performance and
detection bias from lack of blinding, vs 1/20 drug RCTs), and — since food/behaviour cannot be blinded —
this weakness is structural, not fixable. The conclusion carries the hedge: the exercise effect «appear
similar to that of commonly used antihypertensive medications, but this is tempered by the observed
differences in study population characteristics»
[@naci2019exercise]. One bias runs the other way, and
Naci names it: the authors say they may have underestimated exercise's SBP effect, because «We excluded
trials of medications and dosages that were considered to be ineffective or suboptimal by the BNF, even
though we did not exclude any exercise trials on this basis»
[@naci2019exercise]. The drug arm was screened for
effective doses and the exercise arm was not, so the matched-stratum parity is not obviously flattering
to exercise.

**Which type.** The single types lower SBP about equally; combined endurance plus resistance training
beats dynamic resistance alone (−2.98, 95% CrI −5.04 to −0.93), and in the >=140 mmHg sensitivity
analysis it beats endurance, resistance and isometric training alike [@naci2019exercise], reaching −13.51 (−16.55 to −10.45) vs control
[@naci2019exercise]. Naci
notes that only a small subset of trials tested the combination and their frequency and duration were not
consistently reported [@naci2019exercise], so the
edge may partly be more total training. The type groups
differ in baseline SBP (125 mmHg resistance, 135 combination; Table 1), so part of the all-populations
edge may be baseline; the edge persisting at >=140 mmHg is the better test. The decision reading and its
caveats are on [[Is the Exercise Modality Doing Any Work]].

**Decision relevance.** For a **hypertensive** person, structured exercise (endurance or dynamic
resistance) is a credible-magnitude SBP lever — indirectly comparable to a first-line drug in the
matched stratum — so it is a defensible *adjunct*, and a candidate *alternative* where a drug is
declined or not yet indicated. Two guards: (1) the equivalence holds only at hypertensive baselines; at
a **normotensive** baseline the exercise SBP effect is smaller (−4.84) and the drug comparison was never
the point; (2) SBP here is a **surrogate** — Naci measured no CV events. The event payoff runs through
this page's BPLTTC channel (\~10% per 5 mmHg), which is evidenced for *pharmacological* lowering; whether
an exercise-induced SBP drop transmits at the same rate is the same assumption flagged for the sodium,
alcohol and salt-substitute levers above. Exercise also carries large off-target benefits (fitness,
glycaemia, weight) that a BP-only comparison ignores -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]],
[[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]. The head-to-head RCT that would settle the comparison
directly does not exist — a standing gap. [inferred from @naci2019exercise]

## Second outcome — antihypertensive treatment also lowers dementia risk

Hypertension is one of the 14 modifiable dementia risk factors, and the treatment lever carries over
-> [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]]. The Commission holds this observationally: «those
with untreated hypertension had a 42% higher risk of dementia than healthy controls (HR 1.42; 1.15-1.76),
but this risk was attenuated or lost with treatment» (HR 1.13, 0.99-1.28).
[@livingston2024]

**The interventional effect, de-secondhanded first-hand (Peters 2022, F-refinement `[2026-09-04]`).** The
IPD OR the Commission borrows is now held at source: Peters 2022, an IPD MA of **five double-blind
placebo-controlled** antihypertensive RCTs (HYVET, SYST-EUR, PROGRESS, ADVANCE, SHEP; «28 008 individuals
recruited from 20 countries», 861 dementia cases) [@peters2022bp]. «Multilevel logistic regression reported an adjusted odds ratio 0.87 (95% confidence
interval: 0.75, 0.99) in favour of antihypertensive treatment reducing risk of incident dementia with a
mean BP lowering of 10/4 mmHg» [@peters2022bp] —
«Class I evidence» [@peters2022bp]. In absolute terms
dementia occurred in «403 (2.9%) and 458 (3.3%)» of active vs placebo (>=2y follow-up)
[@peters2022bp]. That is an ARD of \~0.4 percentage
points, i.e. an NNT of \~250 over the median follow-up [inferred from @peters2022bp]; and it is conservative
because the trials stopped early on the CV endpoint (dementia accrues slower). This is **NOT an `[E-independent]` corroboration** of
this page's CV-events evidence: Peters' five trials sit inside BPLTTC's 48-trial base and the author lists
overlap (Chalmers, Woodward, Anderson — George Institute), so the shared RCT substrate defeats independence
on the BP-lowering *mechanism* — what is new is the OUTCOME (dementia), a new-endpoint extension. Full
facets (mediation 53%, no effect-modification, cognitive-decline null) on
[[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]].
[inferred from @peters2022bp; @bplttc2021]

**The observational U survives no better here than for CV events.** Peters' abstract records the same
J/U-curve puzzle — «Observational studies indicate U-shaped associations of blood pressure (BP) and
incident dementia in older age, but rando-mized controlled trials of BP-lowering treatment show mixed
results on this outcome in hypertensive patients» [@peters2022bp] —
and randomization erases it: «no evidence of a U-shaped re-lation of the effect at any age», with a
monotone «linear relationship between lower risk of dementia and lower BP, down to at least 100 mmHg
systolic and 70 mmHg diastolic» [@peters2022bp]. The 2024 Lancet Commission's target — «maintain systolic BP of 130 mm Hg or less from age 40
years» — is a *dementia*-prevention framing of the same midlife-BP lever. As with CV events, MR studies
suggesting BP is protective are discounted as survival-bias artifacts, consistent with this page's
treat-on-absolute-risk conclusion -> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

**preDIVA — no cognitive harm detected from BP-lowering in the elderly, and the dementia benefit is stratum-specific
`[2026-08-07]`.** preDIVA (Moll van Charante 2016), a 6.7-yr nurse-led multidomain vascular-care cluster-RCT
in 3526 *unselected* elderly (70-78), lowered systolic BP by -2.06 mm Hg but did **not** reduce all-cause
dementia (HR 0.92, 0.71-1.19) [@mollvancharante2016]. Two decision-relevant reads: (i) **it eases a standing worry** — «our results mitigate fears that
antihypertensive treatment in older age groups evokes cognitive decline ... they show that such an
intervention is safe, which is in accordance with findings from the recent SPRINT trial»
[@mollvancharante2016], so one trial detected
no cognitive-harm penalty from modest BP-lowering (-2.06 mm Hg) in the over-70s; the authors' «safe» is a
single-trial null, not shown safety; observational concerns about low BP and cognitive decline in older
people are, in Rahimi's reading, «likely to be due to reverse causation» and «have not been substantiated in
a randomised trial thus far» [@rahimi2021bpage]
(-> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]); (ii) **any dementia benefit is
concentrated where BP is genuinely untreated** — the adherent-untreated-hypertension subgroup reached
HR 0.54 (0.32-0.92) [@mollvancharante2016],
a route-(b) effect-modification *hypothesis* (post-hoc, adherence-selected), consistent with this page's
treat-on-absolute-risk logic: the lever pays where the deficit is real, not in an already-well-managed
population. Full multidomain-trial context -> [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]].

## A fourth lifestyle BP route — vegan dietary pattern (observational, very-low COE) `[2026-08-29, Landry]`

Landry's 2024 umbrella adds a diet-pattern BP lever: pooling 16 observational studies (Benatar),
«vegan diets were associated with lower systolic BP [MD (95 % CI): −2.56 mmHg (−4.66, −0.45); I2=83 %]»,
non-significant for diastolic, and «largely driven by participants from non-Eastern Asian countries»
[@landry2024vegetarian]. This is the
**weakest-warranted** of the four lifestyle routes on this page: observational only (the
controlled-trial BP evidence was set aside because «19 of these studies targeted adults with diagnosed
diseases»), GRADE **very low**, I2=83%, and healthy-adherer confounded — vegans differ on many mortality
levers at once -> [[Vegetarian Dietary Patterns and Mortality]]. Read through this page's
\~10%-per-5-mmHg channel, −2.56 mmHg predicts only **\~5%** CV-event reduction *if* the pharmacological
transmission transfers — and the vegan diet simultaneously moves LDL, BMI and CRP, so the BP channel is
not separable as the cause. A dietary-pattern BP association, not a titratable lever; the SBP number is
a surrogate here as for the sodium/alcohol routes above. [inferred from @landry2024vegetarian]

## A fifth lifestyle BP route — yoga, the least-warranted of them `[2026-09-23, Geiger]`

Geiger's 2025 SR+MA (30 RCTs, 2283 prehypertensive-to-hypertensive adults, gold-tier) adds yoga — a
stress-mitigation lever ([[Stress Management and Cardiometabolic Health]]) — as a BP route, and it is the
**weakest-warranted route on this page**, below even the vegan association. The headline against waitlist
is respectable (SBP «MD = -7.95 mmHg», DBP -4.93) but three things collapse it as a titratable events
lever: (1) GRADE **very low**, I2=90%; (2) against an *active* control the SBP effect is **not
significant** (-4.16, 95% CI -10.76 to 2.44, p=0.22); (3) under 24h-ABPM the SBP effect **disappears**
(only DBP survives) [@geiger2025yoga]. Read through this
page's \~10%-per-5-mmHg channel, even the waitlist -7.95 would predict \~16% CV-event reduction *if* the
pharmacological transmission transferred — but that transmission is unproven for any lifestyle route
(the caveat that governs the sodium/alcohol/vegan routes above), the fair-comparator estimate is null,
and the SBP fall is partly a clinic-BP measurement artifact. **Layer-1 sizing: yoga is a small, uncertain
refinement, not a big rock** — its apparent big-rock size (-7.95) is a waitlist/expectancy inflation, and
net of the best realistic substitute (any supervised activity, which the active-control arms already
supply) the yoga-specific SBP increment is not distinguishable from zero. It earns a place only as a
low-cost adjunct for someone who will actually do it, never as a substitute for salt reduction,
medication, or structured exercise.
[inferred from @geiger2025yoga; @bplttc2021]

## A sixth route — garlic, a big clean surrogate mover with no hard-outcome warrant `[2026-09-25, weave: Ried + Ma]`

Garlic supplementation is a **supplement** BP route ([[Garlic and Blood Pressure]]), and placing it beside
the lifestyle routes above exposes a structure they only imply: **among the non-drug BP levers, surrogate
magnitude and hard-endpoint transmission run inverse — a big office-SBP mover need not be a well-warranted
route.** In the hypertensive stratum garlic's office SBP effect is large and unusually clean — -8.38 mm Hg
(-11.13, -5.62), I2=0, placebo-controlled across 4 trials (Ried), replicated at «-8.121, 95% confidence
interval [CI]: -10.95 to -5.28» mm Hg (Ma, 12 trials)
[@ried2008; @ma2025garlic]
— the largest held among placebo-controlled *supplement* levers, and on par with the biggest lifestyle
movers. It is **not** the single largest non-drug lever: structured exercise restricted to the same
hypertensive stratum is larger at -8.96 (Naci, above), but exercise is unblindable, so its estimate
carries an expectancy component garlic's placebo-controlled trials remove — the two are not the same
quantity (the baseline-and-comparator caution the Naci section already makes). Yet garlic is the *least*
validated of the set: office BP only (no ambulatory pool), no cardiovascular endpoint ever trialled, and
publication bias unresolved (Ma's results and limitations sections contradict each other on it). So a
lever with one of the biggest, cleanest surrogate numbers is the one whose transmission to events — the
caveat governing every route on this page — is *most* completely unproven.

strong-on-the-endpoint are different levers. (Urate lowering is the one non-comparable cell: Qu reports

sizing: garlic ranks as a small rock despite the big surrogate number** — net of a mature low-harm BP drug
(which *is* validated on events — in BPLTTC's population; in the low-risk stage-1 tail only by transport, see

missing endpoint and unresolved pub-bias, cannot be assumed nonzero; Ma's «further studies are unnecessary»
claim cannot hold for CV outcomes that were never trialled. Garlic earns a place only as a low-cost adjunct
in the hypertensive stratum for someone who will take it, never as a substitute for a route with a measured
endpoint.

## The low-risk tail — direct trials of starting a drug in untreated mild hypertension `[2026-10-26, Wang]`

BPLTTC's primary-prevention benefit was measured in an older, higher-risk population: «Mean age at
baseline was 65 years, both in participants with and without previous cardiovascular disease (table).»,
and the comparator group without prior CVD had 31.9 major CV events per 1000 person-years
[@bplttc2021]. A Cochrane
review by Wang and colleagues asks the narrower question that a low-risk person actually faces: does
*starting* a drug, against placebo or no treatment, in untreated stage-1 hypertension (SBP 140-159 and/or
DBP 90-99 mmHg) with no CVD reduce hard events? It found 5 trials, 9124 participants, weighted mean age 50,
mean follow-up 4.4 years; most trials excluded diabetes, and only one (PREVEND IT) enrolled people with
kidney disease (microalbuminuria). Every outcome is **low certainty** (risk of bias, imprecision; WDAE also
indirectness) [@wang2025mildhtn]:

| Outcome (2-5 y) | Control per 1000 | Drug per 1000 (95% CI) | RR (95% CI) | Difference per 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-cause mortality | 21 | 18 (13 to 24) | 0.85 (0.64 to 1.14) | -3 (-8 to +3) |
| Total CV events | 25 | 23 (17 to 31) | 0.93 (0.69 to 1.24) | -2 (-8 to +6) |
| Stroke | 7 | 3 (1 to 6) | 0.41 (0.20 to 0.84) | -4 (-6 to -1) |
| Coronary heart disease | 18 | 20 (14 to 28) | 1.12 (0.80 to 1.57) | +2 (-4 to +10) |
| Withdrawals for adverse effects (5 y) | 23 | 110 (95 to 128) | 4.80 (4.14 to 5.57) | +87 (+72 to +105) |

[@wang2025mildhtn] —
the difference column is arithmetic on the per-1000 columns.

**The mortality and CV-event nulls are insufficient evidence, not evidence of no effect.** Total CV events
rest on roughly 180 events (25 per 1000 of 7292 participants), and the interval, 0.69 to 1.24, contains
the effects BPLTTC would predict for any plausible achieved difference (0.91 per 5, \~0.83 per 10, \~0.75
per 15 mmHg). Trials this size could not have detected a BPLTTC-sized effect, and their
result is compatible with one — the expectancy test returns *insufficient*. That is the
wiki's reclassification: Wang's headline is GRADE null-leaning language, «There may be little to no
diﬀerence between antihypertensive monotherapy or step-up therapy, or both, and placebo or no treatment in
the risk of all-cause mortality (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.14; 5 trials, 9124 participants;
low-certainty evidence).» [@wang2025mildhtn], though its Discussion also
says «Our study re- illustrates the existing uncertainty on initiating pharmacotherapy in
people without pre-existing cardiovascular disease.» [@wang2025mildhtn]
The review does not report the achieved BP difference between arms [searched: mmHg / achieved across
chunks 01-02 with srcgrep], so its RRs cannot be put on BPLTTC's per-mmHg scale.

**The one significant benefit, stroke, is fragile.** It rests on 35 events, and Wang states «most of the events occurred
in a trial that recruited people with mi- croalbuminuria», on an ACE inhibitor, a class whose benefit Wang describes as
«partially independent of BP change»; «We therefore cannot determine if this benefit can be extrapolated
to people without microalbuminuria.» [@wang2025mildhtn]
(Update 2026-10-27: if Wang used Sundström's per-trial counts, and VA-NHLBI contributed no strokes, 30 of
the 35 strokes would be in MRC, not the microalbuminuria trial, though the pooled result's significance would still hang on PREVEND IT; pooled, Sundström's MRC and
PREVEND IT counts give an RR of about 0.40, near Wang's 0.41; flagged as
unresolved in *The BPLTTC grade-1 pool* below.)

**The harm is measured (low certainty), but on 1980s drugs and partly the wrong population.** The withdrawal estimate is
the whole MRC trial (17,354 people, more than the review's own pool) because IPD for this outcome was not
accessible, so it includes people with pre-existing CVD: «Downgraded one level due to indirectness with
inclusion of people with pre-existing cardiovascular disease.» The authors list the limits themselves —
«a significant proportion of withdrawals were due to headaches (a controversial adverse eﬀect)», and the
drugs are no longer first-line or no longer prescribed — and then add that the concern stands: «However,
this concern has been reflected in a recent large meta-analysis, which demonstrated a significant
association between antihypertensive pharmacotherapy and severe adverse events including syncope and
acute kidney injury [157].» (ref 157, unheld) [@wang2025mildhtn].
Read the MRC figure as specific to bendrofluazide-10-mg/propranolol-era regimens (one extra withdrawal
per \~11 people treated for five years). For current low-dose agents it is plausibly an overestimate, but
that is the wiki's reading, not a measurement; current-drug tolerability is not measured
here, and Wang itself treats the tolerability concern as standing, citing unheld evidence of serious
adverse events. Because MRC was placebo-controlled, expectancy common to both arms is in principle subtracted
(the artifact described in [[The Nocebo Component of Drug Side-Effects]]); the trial was only single-blind, and
residual unblinding by drug-specific side effects, the drug era and how headaches were attributed remain
open, so how much of the excess is pharmacological is not settled here.

**Parameter table — Wang against BPLTTC, the held collaboration.** Wang names its conflict as being with
a different paper: «This updated study remains in conflict with the results of Sundström and colleagues
[29].» [@wang2025mildhtn] — Sundström's 2015
BPLTTC mild-hypertension IPD analysis (now held; its own parameter table is in the next section). Against

The population, contrast, metric and baseline-risk rows all read NO, so this is **not a filed tension**: the not-joined check (ii) fires — the two

That attribution is route (a) — lower baseline risk means less absolute benefit and less power — not a

exists here.
Wang does not endorse transport, though. Its Background states the opposite method position: «It is
critical that we use data from high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to make decisions around
initiation of pharmacotherapy in people with mild hypertension without pre-existing cardiovascular
disease, rather than relying on extrapolations from indirect data.» [@wang2025mildhtn]
BPLTTC prescribes exactly that extrapolation (combine relative effects with population absolute risk,
quoted above). Wang's interval is compatible with BPLTTC's estimate (the wiki's reading; neither source
comments on the other's number), and the two disagree on *whether to transport at all*; the Layer-1 sizing
below is BPLTTC's method applied by the wiki, which Wang would not license. That method disagreement is
named here and not filed as a tension, because no matched estimate is in conflict; the wiki's labelled, revisable default on it (BPLTTC's transport, stated as a
transport) is recorded, as a method position rather than a verdict, on
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. type-F: Wang bounds this page's *proven in primary prevention* to BPLTTC's population and
leaves the low-risk tail as transport, not direct finding.

**What it changes at Layer 1.** In this stratum the drug's hard-outcome benefit is a transport of BPLTTC's
relative effect to a baseline roughly 5-6x lower. Transported, a 5 mmHg drop buys about 2 fewer CV events
per 1000 over \~4.4 years (25 x 0.09; \~1.5 to 2.8 from BPLTTC's CI, baseline uncertainty not included;
per 10 mmHg about 4; about 4.5 per 5 mmHg, 3 to 6 from the under-55 interval (0.76-0.88), if Rahimi's larger under-55 estimate holds, though that band mixes primary and secondary prevention, *By age* below), against a low-certainty +87 withdrawals per 1000 on old drugs (one single-blind trial that included
people with prior CVD) and a modern-drug cost that
is not measured here. A withdrawal is a reversible tolerability outcome, not commensurate with a CV event,
so the two are not netted here. So the substitution rule's guards (ii) and (iii) bite:
the antecedent *a mature, low-harm drug captures the BP benefit* holds on relative efficacy but is unproven
on direct hard outcomes here, and its tolerability cost is measured on old drugs in a partly
off-stratum trial (with a current-drug figure for an adjacent intermediate-risk stratum, after a run-in: HOPE-3 below); so the drug shrinks the lifestyle BP
rocks **less** here than in a high-risk stratum. It does not make those rocks large: their absolute benefit
is small for the same baseline-risk reason, and a lifestyle-induced drop needs a second transport (from
drug to diet or exercise) that the drug does not. On the BP->CV channel both levers are small in absolute
terms — a ceiling-is-a-finding result — and the choice between them is the person's (Layer 3). Guard (i)
keeps this scoped to that channel: exercise keeps its non-BP value (fitness, glycaemia, weight; the Naci
section above), which a single-channel drug does not substitute, so exercise does not shrink with the BP
rock. The labelled default for this stratum is a three-to-six-month non-drug trial first: structured
exercise (−8.96 mmHg vs control in Naci's hypertensive trials, which were largely unblinded; the comparison
with drugs is indirect; the Naci section), sodium
reduction where intake is high ([[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]) — including switching home-added
salt to a 25-40% KCl substitute after ruling out kidney disease, potassium-sparing diuretics and potassium
supplements in everyone who eats the household's food (SSaSS's exclusions, which it applied to household
members too), and checking kidney function and serum potassium first for anyone in the household with diabetes or on an
ACE inhibitor/ARB (groups Lai or Brand class as possibly at risk, with no in-stratum safety estimate; safety is
also untested in children and the switch untested in pregnancy, see [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]];
hard outcomes are shown only in high-risk
rural Chinese adults with mostly discretionary salt; here it rests on the BP fall, measured at a weighted
mean SBP of 147.6 mmHg (inside this band) and mean age 62.3; Lai's meta-regression (low credibility)
suggests larger falls in older patients and at higher baseline SBP, so in a younger stage-1 adult it is
likely somewhat less; Lai paragraph above) — cutting alcohol for people above 2 drinks/day (the
alcohol section), and weight loss where overweight (a BP lever like the others, though its own
hard-event trials were null, [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]), then reassess BP. Basis: part of guidance as Wang reports it, and that a few months'
delay forgoes very little expected benefit at this baseline while avoiding the drug's tolerability cost;
not trial-tested. The drug remains an individual decision
[inferred from @wang2025mildhtn; @bplttc2021].
That matches the review's own implication — «We suggest consideration of initiation of antihypertensive
pharmacotherapy in patients with mild, untreated hypertension, and no pre-existing cardiovascular disease,
diabetes, or chronic kidney disease on an individual basis.» — and the guideline split it reports: «Some
guidelines suggest a three- to six-month trial of non-pharmacologic intervention prior to initiation of
pharmacotherapy [18], while others suggest non- pharmacotherapeutic intervention only [19, 20].»
[@wang2025mildhtn]
(ESH 2023, Hypertension Canada 2020 and NICE 2019 as Wang cites them; the split is as Wang reports it.)
Wang also characterises the same guidance the other way: «Recent guidelines recommend a low threshold to
initiate antihypertensive pharmacotherapy for primary prevention [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22].»
[@wang2025mildhtn] — refs 16-22 add ACC/AHA 2017, ESC 2024 and WHO 2021 to
the three above. So, as Wang reports it, guidance runs from a low drug threshold, through a non-drug
trial first, to non-drug intervention only in this stratum; where guidance differs on sequencing and on
whether to start a drug at all, the evidence does not settle it, which is what the low-certainty pool
shows.

**Gaps.** No quality-of-life outcome is reported [searched: quality of life across chunks 01-02]; no
achieved-BP difference. The efficacy pool includes two current first-line agents, though barely: chlorthalidone in VA-NHLBI (50-100 mg,

PREVEND IT (212), about 13% of participants [@wang2025mildhtn], but tolerability rests on MRC alone, and no new qualifying trial was found from 2012 to June
2024.

**On the estimates a distinction; on the conclusions a joined issue.** On the pooled estimates, check (ii): the
populations, contrasts and drugs differ, and the pool's benefit comes mostly from people Wang excluded. On
the matched trials there is no clash to test: the two analyse largely the same participants and return near-identical numbers
(Wang RR vs Sundström non-BPLTTC OR: deaths 0.85 vs 0.85, coronary 1.12 vs 1.12, CV events 0.93 vs 0.97;
the trial sets differ by PREVEND IT and SHEP). Check (iii) cuts the other way for the conclusions. Read in
full, Sundström extends its relative effect to grade 1 generally (the «can be gen- eralized» sentence below)
and concludes benefit is «likely» in uncomplicated grade 1; Wang's summary of it is narrower than that
conclusion. Wang does describe Sundström's population accurately: «Moreover, most trials in
our meta-analysis excluded people with diabetes, whereas 96% of participants from the BPLTTC trials had type
2 diabetes.» and closes «Overall, our study reflects the uncertain benefit in people with lower baseline
cardiovascular risk, while Sundström and colleagues' study supports a benefit in those with a higher
cardiovascular risk, including diabetes.» [@wang2025mildhtn]
Sundström names the same population difference first when explaining its disagreement with Wang's earlier
version: «Besides the inclusion of persons with diabetes or prior antihypertensive treatment, the disparity
between the conclusions of this review and the one immediately preceding it (7) is primarily attributable
to statistical power.» [@sundstrom2015mildhtn]
The two attributions (lower baseline risk; population plus power) both fit the held numbers, since the
non-BPLTTC intervals contain the pooled ORs (0.86 inside 0.71-1.33 for CV events, 0.78 inside 0.62-1.15 for
deaths); neither is shown over the other. So the estimates are a distinction (check ii, plus agreement on the matched trials),
while the conclusions join on one issue of method: whether the relative effect may be *assumed* to
transport to the untreated, non-diabetic stratum when that stratum's own trials are inconclusive
(Sundström: yes; Wang: decisions should not rest on extrapolation from indirect data). Neither asserts the relative effect is different there. No not-joined check dissolves it; it is the transport-method disagreement taken up below
.

**What remains is the transport-method disagreement already named for BPLTTC 2021.** Sundström takes the
relative effect as portable and the absolute effect as uncertain: «Therefore, although it is reasonable to assume
that the relative risk reductions in the present study can be gen- eralized to a broader population group
with grade 1 hypertension, there is uncertainty about the wider ap- plicability of the absolute risk
reductions observed.» It comes after the authors' note that the pool's 5-year risk «was not trivial»,
partly because of diabetes, and directly after «Patients in this study may have had subclinical car-
diovascular damage at baseline that was not captured in the BPLTTC data set or the reports of the other
trials.» Two sentences later (past a pointer to Table 2) comes «In each case, the estimates must be interpreted
with caution because the CIs are wide and the point estimates of absolute effect are probably un-
reliable.» The next sentence leans the other way: «Nonetheless, these data highlight the poten- tially
sizeable risk reductions that might be achieved by the treatment of grade 1 hypertension, particularly
among persons with multiple cardiovascular risk fac- tors.» [@sundstrom2015mildhtn] Wang's
Background, quoted above, asks for direct trials «rather than relying on extrapolations from indirect data».
That is a joined issue on method: the same question, with clashing backing (Sundström's non-significant
interactions plus earlier evidence of similar relative effects with and without diabetes, against Wang's
stance against extrapolation from indirect data). Neither side is absolute: Sundström leaves the absolute
effect uncertain and suggests absolute-risk-based decisions «may be the best approach» (below), and Wang does not rule drugs out:
«We suggest consideration of initiation of antihypertensive pharmacotherapy in patients with mild,
untreated hypertension, and no pre-existing cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease on
an individual basis.» [@wang2025mildhtn]. No matched estimate conflicts, so, as for BPLTTC 2021 above, it is named
as a joined method disagreement that neither source's evidence settles, and not filed as a tension. The wiki's
default (carry the relative effect over, labelled as a transport, absent positive evidence of
modification) is its own method position and is recorded with its limits on
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. type-F, two ways: Sundström's data resolve the estimate side of Wang's named conflict into a
difference in who is pooled (population and, with it, events and power), on shared trials that agree (Sundström itself attributes its disparity with Wang's 2012 version to the
diabetic and already-treated inclusion and, beyond that, primarily to power), and it supplies the
within-pool untreated subgroup and the second transport below. The first part restates Wang's own
attribution; the beyond-summary content is the matched-trial concordance and the untreated subgroup. Not
type-E against BPLTTC 2021 or Rahimi (same collaboration, shared authors and trials), and not against Wang
(nested data).

**The transported absolute effect at Wang's baseline.** Table 2 applies the pooled OR to the non-BPLTTC

**What the trial could and could not detect.** It was powered (80%) for a risk «at least 22.5% lower»
assuming 1% a year [@lonn2016hope3]. The
first-coprimary interval, 0.79-1.10, rules out a reduction much larger than \~21% and an increase much
above \~10%. BPLTTC's 0.91 per 5 mmHg, scaled to the 6.0 mmHg achieved here, predicts about 0.89, which
sits inside the interval; at a 4.4% placebo risk that is about 5 fewer events per 1000 over 5.6 years.
So the overall result is **insufficient evidence**, not evidence of no effect: it neither confirms nor
excludes a BPLTTC-sized benefit, and Wang's *demonstrated no benefit* states it more firmly than the
trial's report does [inferred from @lonn2016hope3].
The composites differ, so the comparison is approximate: BPLTTC's counts stroke, MI or ischaemic heart
disease, and heart failure causing death or admission [@bplttc2021],
while HOPE-3's first coprimary counts CV death, non-fatal MI and non-fatal stroke.

**Tolerability, on current drugs at these doses.** Permanent discontinuation did not differ between arms
(24.4% vs 25.2%, P 0.33). Discontinuation was more common on the drug «owing to symptomatic hypotension,
dizziness, or light-headedness (217 participants [3.4%] vs. 130 [2.0%], P<0.001) but not owing to syncope
(7 [0.1%] vs. 4 [0.1%], P = 0.55) or renal dysfunction or abnormalities in the serum potas- sium level (32
[0.5%] vs. 20 [0.3%], P = 0.13)»; there was no difference in cancer, hospitalisation or non-CV death [@lonn2016hope3]. The excess is about 14 per
1000 over \~5.6 years, one discontinuation for hypotension or dizziness per \~70 treated. All
these figures come from people who had already passed a 4-week active run-in: only
those taking >=80% of tablets without «an unacceptable level of adverse events» were randomised (12,705
of 14,682), and side effects, «the most common of which were abnormal laboratory values and hypotension»,
were among the reasons the rest were not [@lonn2016hope3].
The run-in gave «both active treatments (for blood-pressure lowering and for cholesterol lowering)»
[@lonn2016hope3], so some exclusions may reflect rosuvastatin (the run-in checked creatine kinase and
alanine aminotransferase) and some the BP drugs (it also checked creatinine and potassium, and hypotension was a
named reason). The run-in also selected people taking >=80% of tablets, which bears on how far the
benefit transports as well as the harm. Even so, the trial
measures BP-drug tolerability in people who had tolerated a month of active treatment; in an unselected
population the hypotension excess may be larger, by an amount the paper does not report. The other comparisons
(renal, syncope, diabetes) show *no excess detected*, not *no harm*.

In the same paragraph they note the counter-evidence, «Several meta-analyses have shown similar
reductions in relative risk across pretreatment systolic blood-pressure levels ranging from less than 130
mm Hg to more than 180 mm Hg», mostly from trials in people with vascular or renal disease, diabetes, or

contrast does not cleanly separate SBP from risk. Their «above approximately 140» cut
is not the 143.5 tertile boundary; it follows directly on their regression-dilution-corrected *usual* SBP

whether the baseline reading is the pre-enrollment one is not stated. The test is a trend across

**The issue is joined at the level of conclusions.** On the same hypothesis, that baseline SBP modifies
the relative effect in people without CVD, HOPE-3 reports a prespecified trend (P 0.02, 0.009, 0.005 on
three nested composites, not independent replications) and BPLTTC no evidence of it (adjusted P >0.28, no subgroup HR above 1.0). BPLTTC treated
the question as open going in, since it built its IPD analysis to resolve conflicting tabular reports.
What the held material cannot show is a clash of *estimates*: the strata are cut differently, BPLTTC's
per-stratum HRs are image-only, the populations differ about 4x in risk, and every HOPE-3 third's interval
contains BPLTTC's predicted \~0.89, the lowest third's 1.16 included. The two G cells are BPLTTC's own HRs
for people without CVD below 130 mmHg and the make-up of that stratum, which together would show whether
a pooled lowest-stratum estimate sits near 1.0 too. On one side is a nominal within-trial trend. On the
other is a pooled IPD test with many more people in the corresponding stratum (14,928 without CVD below 130
mmHg, against 4202 in HOPE-3's lowest third), but that stratum is drawn from a no-CVD population about 4x higher in risk (part of it BPLTTC's
broader composite), in which CKD was more common than in BPLTTC's own CVD group (its prevalence relative to
HOPE-3 is not held), while the stratum's own risk and make-up are not held; it also differs
in contrast (it pools drug-vs-drug and intensity trials) and scale (standardised per 5 mmHg). Its adjustment
(Hommel's method) guards against false positives, not false negatives, and
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]] accepts a pooled IPD interaction test for an absence as for
a positive but ranks it below a within-trial one.
So the default stays with BPLTTC's constant relative effect on size and precision rather than design, the
low-SBP no-CVD corner is recorded as contested, and no tension page is filed while the matched estimates are unheld. HOPE-3 is not type-E
support for BPLTTC either: its data are independent, but its authors overlap and its result is a trend,
not agreement [inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021].
The conditional that would reconcile both: baseline SBP modifies the relative effect only at intermediate
risk with little comorbidity, which BPLTTC's lower-SBP no-CVD stratum might not show if it is
higher in risk, as its no-CVD population is, or in comorbidity (not held; BPLTTC reports CKD more common than
in its CVD group, not by stratum). A no-CVD, low-SBP, low-comorbidity IPD stratum with its own estimate would confirm or kill it (G)
.

**Route (a) or route (b)?** The SBP trend is the right design class for route (b): within one trial,
prespecified, with a stated hypothesis. But it is a nominal test among three hypothesis-based subgroups,
stroke shows no gradient, the lowest-third interval spans benefit and harm, and a larger pool with
independent data found no modification. Read it as **a hypothesis, not an established modifier**: the
default stays BPLTTC's constant relative effect, with absolute benefit scaling by baseline risk (route
a). The same trial found no significant interaction by thirds of baseline risk, which is what route (a)
predicts for the relative effect and which points to SBP rather than overall risk as the candidate
modifier in this trial. That absence is weakly informative: the trial was powered for its main effects,
its population was selected to be at intermediate risk, and the baseline-risk-third estimates sit in a
supplement the wiki does not hold [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]] [inferred from @lonn2016hope3].
Here SBP serves two roles that should not be confused: as a component of risk it feeds the route-(a)
default; as an effect modifier it is only a hypothesis.

**What it changes at Layer 1.** Two numbers from one trial, on crude event proportions over a median
5.6 years [inferred from @lonn2016hope3]:

- **Upper SBP third (>143.5 at baseline, mean 154), intermediate risk:** 6.5% vs 4.8%, about 17 fewer first-coprimary events
  per 1000 (one per \~57 treated; about 4 to 29 fewer from its interval), nominally significant and from a subgroup. Under the route-(a) default
  (BPLTTC's \~0.89 at this BP difference on a 6.5% placebo risk) the expected figure is about 7 per 1000
  (one per \~140); the subgroup estimate is the larger, less certain number. This band overlaps Wang's
  stage-1 range, but the third includes some people already on a BP drug (the per-third share is not
  reported), and the trial population is older (mean 65.7) than Wang's (weighted mean 50), so it is a direct estimate for an
  adjacent stratum, not for Wang's.
- **Lower two SBP thirds (143.5 or less at baseline):** no benefit shown in either lower third. The nominal subgroup estimates are
  about 4 and 3 *more* events per 1000 (70/2080 vs 62/2122; 87/2120 vs 81/2141), with intervals spanning benefit and
  harm. If BPLTTC's constant relative effect holds there, the expected benefit at a 2.9-3.8% placebo risk
  is about 3-4 fewer events per 1000. Against either sit \~14 per 1000 more discontinuing for hypotension or
  dizziness (measured, after a run-in). The figures are not commensurate and are not netted.

The authors summarise the lower thirds as «no benefit was observed in partici- pants who had a baseline
systolic blood pressure of 143.5 mm Hg or less and a suggestion of harm for those in the lower-third
subgroup» [@lonn2016hope3]. Their «approximately 140»
tracks the upper third's mean usual SBP (140.9), not a boundary; this section's decision text does not use
either number as a cut.

For an intermediate-risk person without CVD, the expected benefit under the route-(a) default follows
absolute risk: about 3-4 fewer events per 1000 over 5.6 years at the lower thirds' placebo risk (2.9-3.8%)
and about 7 at the upper third's (6.5%), so a person's place is set by their absolute risk, not by SBP alone.
Read literally, the subgroup estimates would sharpen this into an SBP split: no benefit shown and
harm-leaning point estimates in the lower thirds, about 17 per 1000 in the upper. That split is a
hypothesis, and its cut-point is not located. Against either reading sit \~14 per 1000 more discontinuing
for hypotension or dizziness (trial-wide, after a run-in; not reported by third in the main text);
renal or potassium discontinuations showed no excess detected (0.5% vs 0.3%) and total discontinuation
did not differ between arms, so the cost side is mainly the hypotension excess, not general intolerance.
Under route (a) alone the two ends differ only about twofold (3-4 vs \~7 per 1000), so a sharper split
by SBP would draw on the subgroup estimates. In HOPE-3 risk and SBP travelled together, so the two readings
agree only where they still do. At the low end of this risk range (crude CV death, MI or stroke roughly
0.5-0.7% a year, as in the lower thirds' placebo arms; route (a) about 3-4 per 1000), no hard-outcome
benefit was shown in the held overall or lower-SBP-third results (the risk-third estimates are not held),
and the transported one is small, so a non-drug route first, or no BP drug, is a reasonable default, and
the decision is the person's (Layer 3). That is a labelled default: on the BP channel a lifestyle drop would face the
same or a less certain transported benefit (lifestyle-to-events is itself a transport), and if the SBP
gradient is real it binds BP lowering by any route; so the preference for the non-drug route rests on
harm, cost and non-BP value, not on a measured advantage. (Trial-wide, at about 0.8% a
year, route (a) gives \~5 per 1000.) A person at that low end of risk whose baseline SBP is above the trial's
upper cut is where the readings part: the subgroup reading
favours treatment, route (a) gives only the low-end figure, and the choice turns on how much weight the
person gives the unconfirmed gradient.

Toward about 1.2% a year (as in the upper third's
placebo arm), route (a) gives about 7 fewer per 1000 and starting a drug is reasonable to consider. The
upper-third subgroup estimate supports this only where the higher risk comes with a baseline SBP above the
trial's upper cut (mean 154), and there it is larger than treating on risk predicts but direct, nominal and
imprecise. Where the risk comes from other factors and SBP is in the 130s to low 140s, the subgroup reading
does not settle it: on the baseline-visit scale that range is mostly the middle third (1.08,
0.80-1.46, no benefit shown), but on a multi-visit average the low 140s sit at the upper third's mean usual
SBP (140.9) and 130-140 cannot be placed in a third. So the case rests on route (a) alone and on how much weight
the person gives an unconfirmed SBP gradient
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021].

## By age — 0.82 under 55, 0.91 from 55 to 84, 0.99 at 85+, one borderline interaction `[2026-10-27, Rahimi]`

Rahimi and colleagues re-cut the BPLTTC dataset by age at entry: 51 trials, 358,707 people aged 21-105
(median 65), with previous heart failure excluded. Bands: <55 n=42,960 (12.0%), 55-64 n=128,437, 65-74
n=128,506, 75-84 n=54,016 (15.1%), **>=85 n=4788 (1.3%)**; about 22,000 were aged 80 or more, a count the
paper gives without a separate estimate [@rahimi2021bpage].
The headline: «The hazard ratios for the risk of major cardiovascular events per 5 mm Hg reduction in
systolic blood pressure for each age group were 0·82 (95% CI 0·76--0·88) in individuals younger than 55
years, 0·91 (0·88--0·95) in those aged 55--64 years, 0·91 (0·88--0·95) in those aged 65--74 years, 0·91
(0·87--0·96) in those aged 75--84 years, and 0·99 (0·87--1·12) in those aged 85 years and older (adjusted
p-interaction=0·050).» [@rahimi2021bpage]
This is the same collaboration and lead authors as BPLTTC 2021 above, on an overlapping dataset (51 vs 48 trials), so it refines that estimate
by stratum (type-F); it is not independent corroboration. The age bands pool people with and without prior
CVD (prior heart failure excluded) and include drug-vs-drug and more-vs-less-intensive trials, so they are
not primary-prevention estimates.

**Both ends' point estimates depart from the middle; only the <55 interval excludes 0.91, and the one test
that covers both ends is borderline.** Under 55 the estimate is larger, 0.82 (0.76-0.88); from 55 to 84 it is 0.91 in every band; at 85 and over
it is 0.99 (0.87-1.12). The single adjusted p of 0.050 covers both ends. The authors' own framing: «Although
we found evidence for diminishing relative risk reductions with increasing age, absolute risk reductions did
not follow the same pattern and appeared to be largest in the oldest age groups.» They keep two readings of
the relative trend open. One is chance: «With a p-interaction of 0·05 for the primary outcome and the absence of any
meaningful interaction by age for ischaemic heart disease and heart failure, a chance finding cannot be
ruled out. Indeed, an alternative interpretation of our age-stratified results could be that relative risk
reductions for most participants included in the analyses are consistent.» The other is a real
attenuation: «On the other hand, statistical tests for interaction are notoriously conservative and our
results in the context of large-scale epidemiological studies» (citations omitted) «which have also shown a
pattern of diminishing relative effects with increasing age, invites consideration of different
explanations.» Their
candidate reasons are shorter treatment («the average treatment duration in the oldest age group was only
about half of that in the youngest age group»), longer lifetime exposure to raised BP, and more clustered
risk factors [@rahimi2021bpage].

**Which estimate applies at 85 and over is a route-(b) question.** The authors' uncertainty statement is
about the band *in isolation*: «With only about 1000 major cardiovascular events accrued over a median
follow-up duration of 2·8 years in the group of participants aged 85 years or older at randomisation, the
treatment effects in this highest age category in isolation were uncertain.» [@rahimi2021bpage]
The age subgroups were prespecified and tested within the pooled IPD («To test whether treatment effects varied
across prespecified subgroups of age categories and systolic and diastolic blood pressure at baseline, we
used likelihood-ratio tests for interactions.» [@rahimi2021bpage]),
the design the route-(b) rule accepts for its within-trial part; what mainly keeps the modification from
clearing the bar is its strength (adjusted p=0.050, the authors' chance caveat, no meaningful interaction
for ischaemic heart disease or heart failure). For the oldest band the comparison with younger bands is partly
between trials (elderly-only trials such as HYVET, very likely in the pool though not confirmed, see below, may
supply much of it). The authors checked one part of the trial mix: an unstandardised sensitivity analysis of the
age-stratified effects, also stratified by the three trial designs, was «broadly similar to the main results» [@rahimi2021bpage]. By-trial
composition of the bands is not reported, so between-trial differences could still push the 85+ contrast
in either direction. So the default at 85+ stays the common \~0.91, applied with wider
uncertainty;
the band's own 0.99 (0.87-1.12) is compatible with it and can neither confirm nor overturn it, since its
interval runs from 0.87, a larger benefit than the 55-84 point estimates, to 1.12, a possible harm. The 85+ data
cannot confirm the benefit by themselves, and they do not show it is absent: *insufficient evidence* in
the four-state sense for the band alone, not *no benefit*. The authors' descriptive point, from the cumulative-incidence curves (figure 1, image-only, not held): «In all
age groups, event rates were lower in the intervention than in the comparator group (figure 1). However,
the confidence limit was widest in the group of individuals aged 85 years or older at baseline, reflecting
the smaller number of participants and events in this group (figure 1).»
[@rahimi2021bpage]

**What it changes for the young: the low-risk-tail transport may understate.** The <55 interval excludes
0.91, but the same borderline interaction covers it, so a larger effect in the young is also not
established. If it is real, the transport in *The low-risk tail* above (weighted mean age 50) would be
about 4.5 fewer CV events per 1000 per 5 mmHg over \~4.4 years (25 x 0.18; about 3 to 6 from the 0.82
interval) rather than about 2, still small in absolute terms at that baseline. The <55 band is not a
primary-prevention estimate: 30.8% had ischaemic heart disease at entry
[@rahimi2021bpage]. That is a scope
note, not a reason to expect the 0.82 to be inflated: in BPLTTC 2021 the relative effect barely differs by
prior CVD (0.91 without, 0.89 with; the effect section at the top).

**Absolute benefit grew with age, but the paper's numbers are not for direct use.** «Absolute risk
reductions for major cardiovascular events varied by age and were larger in older groups (adjusted
p-interaction=0·024).» The figures are in an image-only forest plot not held here, and the authors limit
them: «Thus, our analyses of absolute risk differences are only useful for internal comparisons of effect
sizes across strata.» [@rahimi2021bpage]
For a decision, they say to combine the relative effect with absolute risk from a current registry; that
is the same method as *decide on absolute CV risk* below, so for a 75-84-year-old the page's existing
approach holds with 0.91 per 5 mmHg and an age-appropriate baseline.

**No evidence of a baseline-BP threshold inside an age band.** Within each band the authors found no
evidence that the relative effect varied across baseline SBP from <120 to >=170 mmHg («all adjusted
p-interaction\>0·070»; for the oldest band «adjusted p-interaction=1·00»), and none by DBP, down to <70
mmHg [@rahimi2021bpage]. This extends the pooled *no J-curve
in randomised data* finding above to age bands up to 84 (the within-trial HOPE-3 counter-signal in the no-CVD corner is noted in the J-curve section). At 85 and over it is close to uninformative: only
121 people in that band started below 120 mmHg [@rahimi2021bpage],
and where the band's overall estimate is near 1, *no variation* cannot show benefit at low BP either
.

**All-cause death and harms are hedged, not settled.** «We found that the proportional risk reduction for
all-cause death is marginally larger in younger than in older age, with no obvious effect in people older
than 75 years.» The authors caution that all-cause effects are sensitive to the share of non-CV deaths, so a subgroup
with more competing non-CV death would show diluted proportional effects, and they read the
absence of excess deaths as «the lack of excess mortality risk in older groups suggests that harmful fatal
effects of the treatment are unlikely in any age group.» [@rahimi2021bpage]
That is a *suggests unlikely* about *fatal* harm, and it is no estimate of non-fatal harm. Falls, syncope
and tolerability are not pooled by age in this paper [searched: adverse / syncope / falls across chunks
01-02]. The authors write that «Health-related quality of life and prevention of harms might be of equal
or even greater importance to older people than prevention of fatal or non-fatal cardiovascular events.»,
and that, «to our knowledge, no randomised comparisons exist to suggest that a fixed level of blood pressure
reduction in older individuals causes more harm than benefit in older people»; they support this with a SPRINT subgroup
and with cited, unheld analyses: «Several other age-stratified analyses of RCTs and their meta-analyses
have also shown no worsening in functional status, physical wellbeing, or quality of life in older
people.» None of this is pooled here [@rahimi2021bpage].
Wang's measured withdrawal excess above is from a different trial population and drug era and does not
fill this cell.

**Who the old trial participants were not.** «Relatedly, because of the typically restricted eligibility
criteria of RCTs, other groups such as those with a high multimorbidity and polypharmacy burden, frail
individuals, and individuals living in institutions have been under-represented.» and «The generalisability
of the findings to these highly relevant and growing patient groups remains uncertain.» [@rahimi2021bpage]
So the evidence describes trial-eligible older people; for a frail, multimorbid or care-home resident it
is transport (see [[Frailty]]).

**HYVET** is named as «the only large-scale trial that has exclusively recruited patients aged 80 years and
older», 3845 people with SBP 160 or higher, with 30% fewer strokes and 23% fewer CV deaths as Rahimi
reports it [@rahimi2021bpage]. Its principal
investigator is on the BPLTTC roster of both papers, so its data are very likely inside this pool and it
would not be an independent check; the trial list (appendix) is not held to confirm.

**What the authors conclude.** «In conclusion, we found no evidence to substantiate the common approach of
withholding antihypertensive treatment for older adults, in particular when their blood pressure is not
highly elevated. Although the findings for people aged 85 years or more at study entry were less
compelling, the overall patterns were consistent and suggestive of worthwhile reductions in cardiovascular
outcomes across all age groups. Although clinical decision making for initiation and continuation of
pharmacological blood-pressure-lowering will continue to be based on harm-benefit trade-offs for any
individual, our study does not support the common belief that such trade-offs justify the overemphasis of
several clinical practice guidelines on an individual's age or starting blood pressure. Therefore,
pharmacological blood pressure reduction should be considered as an important treatment option for the
prevention of cardiovascular events even in those aged 80 years or older and guidelines should be
simplified to remove any differing blood pressure thresholds by age.» For people already treated: «However, in the absence of any strong evidence for excess harms from randomised
studies, we believe that it is appropriate for patients who are on blood-pressure-lowering pharmacotherapy
to continue receiving such treatment if well-tolerated and when prevention of fatal and non-fatal
cardiovascular events remains of importance.» [@rahimi2021bpage]
They describe ESC/ESH as using a higher drug threshold (160/90) from age 80 and NICE 2019 as not treating
over-80s below 150/90 [@rahimi2021bpage]; the held ESC 2021 prevention guideline's own age provisions are not compared here.

**What the page carries as decisions.** From 55 to 84, age alone does not shrink the relative benefit, so
the absolute-risk route sizes it. For a *trial-eligible* person aged 85 or over the same route applies on
the carried \~0.91. If \~0.91 holds, the absolute benefit would be at least as large as at younger ages
. Separately, from the observed data the authors report absolute reductions that «appeared to
be largest in the oldest age groups», a finding they limit to internal comparison [@rahimi2021bpage];
the phrase is plural and the held text does not resolve it by band (the per-band figures are in an
image-only plot). If the age
attenuation they also report is real (the band's own estimate is 0.99, 0.87-1.12), the relative benefit
over the \~3 years of follow-up in that band may be small or absent, and the absolute benefit could then be
small despite the higher risk; their shorter-treatment explanation would make the attenuated figure the relevant one for
someone with a short horizon. The band cannot tell these apart, so starting is reasonable to consider and
is held less firmly than at 55-84; neither reading is established. For *frail, multimorbid or
institutionalised* people at any old age, the relative effect is transported to a group the trials
under-represented, so starting is an individual trade-off (Layer 3). The authors' continuation default is
stated in exactly that context: right after naming the under-represented groups and ATEMPT they write
«We acknowledge that clinical decisions cannot be deferred until such evidence emerges.» and then advise
continuing a well-tolerated drug (quoted above) [@rahimi2021bpage].
For a frail person already on a well-tolerated drug, the page carries the authors' default (continue if
well-tolerated and while CV prevention remains of importance). The wiki reads *well-tolerated* as including
the absence of falls, orthostatic symptoms or syncope, and would review if these appear or priorities change
. That is the authors' default extrapolated to a group the trials under-represented,
and the person's ongoing tolerability is individual harm evidence that a decision to start lacks; a poorly
tolerated drug falls outside «if well-tolerated» either way. On trial efficacy evidence,
continuing is no better supported than starting: the trials compared starting, intensifying or drug-vs-drug treatment, and no
held trial tests stopping at 85+. Unpooled non-fatal harms are not an 85+-specific reason, since they are
unpooled at every age. The authors go further, without restricting it to any subgroup: starting should be considered even at 80 or older, with BP
thresholds that do not differ by age; they also frame initiation and continuation as individual harm-benefit trade-offs and call
transport to the under-represented groups uncertain. The wiki agrees for the trial-eligible, with the
85+ uncertainty stated in both directions, and for the frail it keeps the authors' continuation default
while leaving starting as an individual trade-off: a narrower reading than the authors' unrestricted
*should be considered*, compatible with their individual-trade-off framing, made because the effect is
transported
[inferred from @rahimi2021bpage; @bplttc2021].
Peters' dementia meta-analysis (above) is a different outcome and is not compared on this scale.

## Decision relevance



- **BP-lowering is the cardiometabolic lever with the best hard-outcome warrant, and it applies in
  primary prevention** — for a hypertensive person at BPLTTC-like risk it is the one place a proven
  CV-event reduction is on the table (for low-risk untreated stage 1, see the paragraph after this list).
- **But decide on absolute CV risk, not the BP number.** A \~10%-per-5-mmHg relative effect buys little
  absolute benefit at low risk and a lot at high risk; combine the relative effect with a stratum-
  specific baseline ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]) rather than treating to a
  threshold. This also means the exact BP measurement matters less than the overall risk. (HOPE-3's
  SBP-third gradient, *The intermediate-risk modern-drug trial* above, would make baseline SBP matter as an effect modifier too; it is a
  hypothesis, so SBP enters here as a component of risk.)
- **Lifestyle BP reduction (e.g. sodium, weight, alcohol reduction in >2 drinks/day drinkers) plugs into
  the same channel.** [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]] holds BP as a *surrogate* with hard outcomes
  graded very low; BPLTTC supplies the missing link — *pharmacological* BP reduction does reduce hard
  events — but the transmission of a *lifestyle-induced* BP drop to events is an assumption, not the same
  evidence (a different intervention, unmeasured here — Roerecke's alcohol-reduction CV benefit is
  *modelled*, SSaSS's is *measured*).
- **Where the levers sit in the ranking.** BP is a big rock only conditionally, by baseline risk
  -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]; the lever ordering above is a surrogate ordering,
  read with the transmission criterion in [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. That table leaves out sodium reduction, DASH and
  potassium, which each have a home page: [[DASH Diet and Blood Pressure]], [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]],
  [[Dietary Magnesium and Cardiometabolic Outcomes]], and, for whether sodium reduction should target the
  salt-sensitive or everyone, [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]]. Weight loss as
  an events route -> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].
- **Age 55-84 is a baseline-risk question, not a reason to withhold.** The relative effect on major CV
  events per 5 mmHg is 0.91 in each band from 55 to 84 (Rahimi), so older age raises the absolute benefit
  through higher risk; for all-cause death the authors report no obvious effect above 75, and they
  caution generally that all-cause effects would be expected to be diluted in a subgroup where non-CV
  deaths make up more of the deaths
  [@rahimi2021bpage]. At 85 and
  over the default relative effect is
  \~0.91, carried with wider uncertainty because the band's own estimate is imprecise (HR 0.99,
  0.87-1.12) and is compatible with the same benefit, a smaller one or none (its interval also reaches a
  larger benefit and a possible harm): for the trial-eligible,
  starting is reasonable to consider, held less firmly; for the frail, multimorbid or institutionalised,
  starting is an individual trade-off, and continuing a well-tolerated drug defaults to the authors'
  continue-if-well-tolerated, with the effect extrapolated (the review triggers are the wiki's reading,
  *By age*). The authors also frame initiation and continuation as
  individual harm-benefit trade-offs and call transport to these groups uncertain (*By age* above).

**The first bullet does not hold in the low-risk stage-1 tail: there the benefit is transported, not
proven.** For someone
untreated at 140-159 / 90-99 with no CVD, diabetes or CKD, the direct trials are low-certainty and too
small to confirm or exclude the benefit; transported from BPLTTC it is about 2 CV events per 1000 per
5 mmHg over \~4.4 years (about 4 per 1000 at the \~9 mmHg drugs achieved in Naci's trials, whose
baselines of 156-160 mmHg sit above this band, so likely less here; the Naci section), or about 4.5 (3 to 6)
per 5 mmHg if Rahimi's <55 estimate applies, though that band mixes primary and secondary prevention
(*By age*), plus a low-certainty direct stroke signal (35 events; Wang places most in the microalbuminuria trial,
which Sundström's per-trial counts appear to contradict; unresolved, *The BPLTTC grade-1 pool* above). On the
other side is a low-certainty withdrawal cost (+87 per 1000 over 5 years, from one single-blind 1980s
trial that included people with prior CVD). The two are not netted. On the BP->CV channel drug and
lifestyle levers are both small here; a non-drug trial first is the labelled default (practice and
guidance basis, not trial-tested), and exercise keeps its non-BP value either way (*The low-risk tail*
above). Sundström's BPLTTC grade-1 pool does not change this reading. Its statistically significant,
wide-interval benefits on deaths and stroke come mostly from the 96%-diabetic half (498 of 665 deaths,
196 of 226 strokes). In the subgroup on no BP drug at baseline, CV events were 0.84 (0.68-1.05), deaths
0.83 (0.67-1.03) and stroke 0.66 (0.45-0.99), but 201 of that subgroup's 366 CV events are in untreated
BPLTTC participants, almost all diabetic; the
non-BPLTTC (diabetes-excluding) trials alone gave 0.97 (0.71-1.33) for CV events (from MRC and VA-NHLBI;
ANBP did not report CV events) and 0.50 (0.24-1.08) for stroke (MRC only, 30 events). Its own transport to a
non-diabetic baseline gives about 3 CV events (0 to 6) and 4 deaths (2 to 7) fewer per 1000 over 5 years,
the same order as above and not independent of it. On the non-BPLTTC trials' own ORs the same baselines
give about 1 fewer CV event (about 7 fewer to 8 more) and about 3 fewer deaths (about 8 fewer to 3 more)
. With diabetes, treated or not, the closer
reference class is the BPLTTC half (the already-treated subgroup's own estimates are mixed, and for them
the decision is adding a drug; a non-diabetic person already on treatment is nearly absent from the pool, at most about 25 of 3505): about 10 (−1 to 19) fewer CV events and 14 (5 to 22) fewer deaths per
1000 over 5 years at 7.4% 5-year CV risk, hedged by the authors as unreliable point estimates (*The
BPLTTC grade-1 pool* above). Diabetes marks where these were measured: a non-diabetic person at a similar
5-year CV risk on that four-part composite (stroke, coronary events, heart failure, CV death), and with a similar BP reduction, gets a similar CV-event figure only if the relative effect transports, which the non-diabetic trials'
own OR (0.97, 0.71-1.33) neither confirms nor excludes
[inferred from @sundstrom2015mildhtn; @wang2025mildhtn].

**Intermediate risk, no CVD, SBP around 130-145: the one direct modern-drug trial showed no significant
benefit overall, and the benefit expected under the route-(a) default is small.** HOPE-3's overall interval
(0.79-1.10) is compatible with a BPLTTC-sized effect of about 5 fewer events per 1000 over 5.6 years
trial-wide, so the null is insufficient evidence rather than proof of no effect. Under route (a) the
expected benefit follows absolute risk: about 3-4 per 1000 at the lower thirds' placebo risk (roughly 2-5 from BPLTTC's interval) and
about 7 at the upper third's (roughly 5-9). The lower thirds' own subgroup intervals run from about 5-8 fewer to about
17-18 more events per 1000. Neither lower SBP
third showed benefit (nominal estimates slightly worse on the drug, intervals spanning both directions), and
the upper third showed a nominal, subgroup-level benefit of about 17 per 1000 (about 4 to 29 from its
interval). That SBP gradient is a
hypothesis, not an established modifier, and its cut-point is not located (the 143.5 boundary is a tertile
cut on baseline readings). About 14 per 1000 more stopped the drug for hypotension or dizziness than stopped
placebo, measured in people who had passed an active run-in, while total discontinuation did not differ.
So decide on absolute risk: little for the drug at about 0.5-0.7% a year (crude CV death, MI or stroke),
reasonable to consider toward about 1.2% a year (very roughly 5-7% and 12% over 10 years if the rate held
constant, which understates the rise of risk with age; a 10-year calculator's composite may differ); at SBP 130-145 that rests on route (a), since the
SBP-subgroup reading cannot place such a person reliably (the thirds were cut on a single baseline visit's BP,
and the upper third's usual SBP averaged 140.9) (*The intermediate-risk modern-drug trial* above)
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021].

## Limits

- Trials 1972-2013 — older drug regimens and co-treatment; relative (not absolute) effects are the
  transportable quantity, by the authors' own caution.
- One (very large) collaboration's IPD; the target-BP and adverse-effect questions are out of its design.
- The direct trials of *starting* a drug in untreated low-risk stage-1 hypertension (Wang) are five,
  mostly 1970s-80s, low certainty, and too small (\~180 CV events) to confirm or exclude a BPLTTC-sized
  effect, so they cannot test the transport of its relative effect to that stratum; they also partly
  share trial data with BPLTTC (SHEP, PREVEND IT; ANBP possibly), so they are not fully independent of it.
- Wang's pool is nested in Sundström's BPLTTC grade-1 analysis (9117 of 9124 participants), so the two are
  one data set on the untreated, non-diabetic question, not two. Sundström's pooled benefit comes mostly
  from a 96%-diabetic half of which 55-61% were already treated (55% if two trials' listed zeros are
  real, 61% as printed). Prior treatment is a contrast
  mismatch, adding vs starting, but the subgroup split does not show it driving the benefit: untreated vs
  already-treated ORs were 0.84 vs 0.92 for CV events and 0.66 vs 1.01 for stroke, but 0.83 vs 0.71 for
  deaths, all interactions non-significant. Its data-source interaction test is non-significant on few
  events and confounded with diabetes, prior treatment and drug class, and its baseline-risk test was run
  only inside the diabetic BPLTTC half, so neither shows nor excludes a different relative effect in the
  non-diabetic, untreated stratum
  [inferred from @sundstrom2015mildhtn; @wang2025mildhtn].
- The >=85 relative effect rests on about 1000 events over a median 2.8 years and is not settled either way; frail,
  multimorbid and institutionalised people are under-represented, so transport to them is uncertain by
  the authors' own statement (*By age* above).
- HOPE-3 is one trial: its SBP-third gradient is a nominal test among three hypothesis-based subgroups,
  and its tolerability figures come from people selected by an active run-in (BP drugs plus
  rosuvastatin), so they may understate harm in an unselected population. BPLTTC's per-stratum HRs below 130 mmHg in people without CVD are
  image-only and not held, so the two cannot be compared stratum by stratum.
- Applies to *pharmacological* lowering; do not silently transfer the magnitude to a lifestyle route.
