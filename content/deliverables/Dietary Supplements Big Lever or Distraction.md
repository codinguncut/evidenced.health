For a **generally well-nourished adult**, the honest answer is the boring one: **the isolated vitamins and minerals that have actually been tested for disease prevention are a
distraction and an unnecessary expense — and most of the rest of the shelf is *unproven*, not proven
useless.** The big health levers — not smoking, staying active, sleeping
enough, not carrying excess visceral fat, a decent overall diet — are elsewhere, and no pill substitutes
for them ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]). There is a short list of genuine exceptions,
and they are narrow and specific: **correcting an actual deficiency**, and — for a training goal —
**creatine and protein powder**. Almost everything else is either *no measurable effect*, *not enough
evidence to say*, or, for two named agents, *actively harmful*.

The single most important idea, because it dissolves most of the confusion: **there is a difference
between correcting a deficiency and topping up someone who is already replete.** They are different
questions with different answers, and marketing routinely blurs the two.

## The default for a well-nourished person: no measurable benefit

The best evidence here is unusually good, because — unlike most of nutrition — supplements *can* be put
through large, blinded, randomized trials (you can blind a pill). When they are, the general-population
results are flat.

**VITAL** randomized 25,871 US adults to vitamin D3 (2000 IU/day) and/or fish oil (omega-3, 1 g/day) for a
median 5.3 years. All four of its co-primary endpoints missed:

- Vitamin D on cancer: hazard ratio 0.96; on major cardiovascular events: 0.97 [@manson2019vitd].
- Omega-3 on major cardiovascular events: 0.92; on cancer: 1.03 [@manson2019n3].
- And vitamin D did not reduce **fractures** either — total 0.98, hip 1.01 [@leboff2022].

The **USPSTF**, the US preventive-services body, reviewed the whole randomized literature in 2022 and
landed in the same place for the general adult: **multivitamins and single vitamins/minerals — insufficient
evidence** to say they prevent heart disease or cancer; **vitamin E — no net benefit** [@uspstf2022supp]. Both bodies rest on hard outcomes (deaths, heart
attacks, cancers, fractures), not surrogate markers — so this is not the usual "the number moved but the
patient didn't" problem ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). The full picture is on
[[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

## Why "no benefit" is NOT the same as "useless" — the replete-vs-deficient trap

Here is the caveat that runs through everything. VITAL's participants were **not deficient** — their
average vitamin D level was well inside the adequate range, with only \~2.4% frankly deficient [EXTRACTED
(LeBoff - VITAL Vitamin D Fractures 2022) chunk 01]. So its null result means one specific thing: **adding
vitamin D to someone who already has enough buys nothing.** It does *not* mean vitamin D does nothing for a
genuinely deficient person — that is a different question the trial was not built to answer, and the
authors say so [@manson2019vitd].

This is the general rule for vitamins and minerals: **the effect is repletion, not enhancement.** A
supplement helps if — and largely only if — you are short of the nutrient. In the replete, more does
nothing on the outcomes tested, and for fat-soluble vitamins and a few others, *more can harm* (below). So the useful question is
never "does vitamin X work?" but "**am I actually short of it?**" — which is a question about your diet,
your labs, and your situation, not about the pill ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).

## The tiers — what the evidence actually supports, and in what circumstance

### Tier 1 — genuine evidence, for a specific reason

- **Creatine monohydrate** — **widely regarded as the best-evidenced sports supplement**, cheap, with a
  large randomized literature for strength, power, and lean-mass gains *when paired with resistance
  training* (and emerging signals for muscle in older adults). This is a *performance/function* benefit,
  not disease prevention ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]) — a **provisional KEEP**, pending that source. `[creatine MA — acquisition gap]`
- **Protein powder (whey/casein/soy) — is not a magic supplement; it is food in powder form.** Its evidence
  is simply the evidence that hitting your **total daily protein** target supports muscle and strength
  ([[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]], [[Protein Quality and the DIAAS Score]]).
  So it earns its place only when you struggle to reach that target from food — older adults, athletes,
  people in a calorie deficit. Judge it as protein, not as a supplement.
- **Vitamin D3 — only to correct a deficiency** (low sun, dark skin, older/institutionalized, malabsorption).
  For the replete general population it is null on every hard outcome tested
  ([[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]]).
- **Omega-3 / fish oil — modest and situational.** The general-population capsule is null (VITAL, above).
  A within-trial signal on heart attacks (not the main result, and not a proven benefit) hints it may help
  people with low fish intake — i.e. repletion again. High-dose *prescription* EPA in high-triglyceride
  patients is a separate, drug-level question, not the drugstore capsule.
- **Electrolytes — cheap and situational** (heavy sweating, endurance, heat). For most people, ordinary
  food supplies enough.

### Tier 2 — correcting a deficiency (real benefit IF you are deficient)

The largest legitimate use of supplements. The benefit is real *because* it fixes a shortfall, and it
does nothing (or harms) if you are already replete — the replete-vs-deficient rule again
([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]). The clearest cases: **folic acid before and during
pregnancy** (to prevent neural-tube defects — the single strongest supplement case, and separately
recommended by the USPSTF [@uspstf2022supp]);
**B12** for vegans, older adults, and malabsorption; **iron** for documented iron-deficiency anemia (and
harmful if you are *not* iron-deficient). **Gap:** the wiki does not yet hold the primary trials for these
repletion cases, so no magnitudes are quoted. `[deficiency-repletion sources — acquisition gap]`

### Tier 3 — "not yet": a plausible mechanism, but the human evidence is thin, short, or surrogate-only

This is the large middle band — supplements with a reasonable biological story and *some* human data, but
nothing yet established on an outcome that matters. They belong in the **insufficient-evidence** bucket:
held as candidates, neither asserted as proven nor dismissed. A lab-dish or mouse result is a starting
point, not a finding, and a moved blood marker is not an averted event ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).

Typical members — **magnesium, CoQ10 / ubiquinol, curcumin, berberine, NAC, ashwagandha, inositol,
glycine, zinc**, and many more. Some have a documented role in *deficiency* (magnesium, zinc) or in
specific medical uses (NAC, not as a general supplement); several have short human trials on surrogate
markers. They sit, honestly, in the *insufficient-evidence* bucket — neither proven nor
disproven. `[Tier-3 compounds — acquisition gap]`

### Tier 4 — essentially no credible human-outcome evidence (marketing, or unfalsifiable)

The long tail: **TUDCA, L-carnitine, digestive enzymes** (outside a diagnosed insufficiency), **grapeseed
extract, milk thistle** (outside specific liver toxicity), **"Super K", boron, magnesium L-threonate**
("brain magnesium"), **R-ALA**, and multi-ingredient "stacks" — sold on a mechanism or an anecdote; the wiki holds **no** evidence bearing on a real human outcome for
them, and none surfaced at the appraisal bar — which is not the same as none existing. And a subset crosses the line from *unproven* to *untestable*:
**"liver cleanse" / "detox"** makes no falsifiable, measurable claim on a real outcome — so it is out on
the demarcation test, not on a weighing of evidence. The test is functional — *is there a measurable claim on a real outcome?* — not a
judgment of the belief ([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]).

## The harm side — "more is better" is false

Supplements are not free of downside, and the clearest case is **beta carotene**: in smokers and
asbestos-exposed workers it *raised* lung cancer (risk ratio 1.18), and across trials it raised
cardiovascular-disease mortality (Peto odds ratio 1.10) — which is why the USPSTF **recommends against it**
[@uspstf2022supp]. It is the paradigm case that
pushing a micronutrient *above* repletion can move a hard outcome the wrong way — the "more is better"
fallacy, realized on cancer and death ([[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]). The same review catalogues
dose-dependent harms for vitamin E (bleeding stroke), vitamin A (bone loss, hip fracture), high-dose
vitamin D (kidney stones), and vitamin C (kidney stones) [@uspstf2022supp]. And because the supplement industry is lightly regulated, **contamination and
mislabeled doses** are a real harm channel independent of the compound itself.

## Why the pill so rarely matches the food — the isolate trap

A recurring reason supplements underperform: **a nutrient pulled out of its food is a different thing than
the food.** Whole foods deliver nutrients bound up in a matrix — fibre, fats, phytochemicals, and structure
that change how they act — and isolating one component often loses whatever made the pattern work
([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]). It is also why the evidence base is *skewed toward isolates*:
single compounds are cheap to dose and blind, so they get studied, while whole-diet patterns are hard to
measure — the well-lit search, not necessarily where the keys are. "Meet your needs from food first" is not
a platitude; it is what the evidence keeps saying.

## The bottom line — a low lever, and a real cost

For a well-nourished adult, supplementing to prevent disease sits **at or near the bottom** of the things
worth doing ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]). The proven big rocks are elsewhere; a
supplement stack is money, attention, and adherence spent on a lever that, for most people, isn't there.
The USPSTF explicitly does **not** weigh cost [@uspstf2022supp] — so the expense of a null stack is a real, unpriced downside this evidence leaves to you.
The genuinely useful moves are narrow and identifiable: **fix a deficiency you actually have**, and — if
you train — **creatine and enough protein**. If you are eating adequately and none of those apply, the
honest recommendation is to **stop optimizing supplements for disease prevention** and put the effort
where the levers are.

## What this first cut does NOT yet settle (named gaps)

This is an early cut, built on the vitamin/mineral/omega-3 prevention evidence
([[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]]) plus the wiki's evidence-appraisal machinery.
Several pieces are named gaps, not yet in the fabric, so no magnitudes are invented for them:

- the **creatine** and **protein-timing** ergogenic magnitudes (Tier 1);
- the **deficiency-repletion** trials (folate/neural-tube, B12, iron — Tier 2);
- the **antioxidant-harm primary trials** (beyond the USPSTF pooled figure) and the **high-dose EPA**
  boundary;
- individual grading of the **Tier-3 "not yet"** compounds (magnesium, CoQ10, curcumin, berberine, …).

## Mandatory caveats

- **This appraises evidence; it does not prescribe.** It says what the evidence shows for *groups* of
  people. Whether *you* are deficient, what dose and form, interactions (e.g. vitamin K with blood
  thinners, iron loading, fat-soluble toxicity), and pregnancy are decisions for a clinician who knows you.
  **This is not medical advice.**
- **A stratum-level appraisal.** The deficient, the pregnant, the vegan, the older adult, the athlete, and
  the replete healthy adult get different answers; your situation sets which applies.
- **The loop is open.** This grades whether the reasoning is internally sound and faithful to its sources —
  not whether following it made anyone better off. Nothing here has been tested against a real outcome.
- **Health axis only.** This weighs supplements on physical-health outcomes; it does not price the cost,
  beyond noting it is real and that the guideline bodies leave it out.
