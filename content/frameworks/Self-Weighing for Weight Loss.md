**The decision this page changes.** Whether someone trying to lose weight should step on a scale
regularly, how often, and whether a scale on its own, without a programme, does anything. The lever is
cheap and easy to stop, so the questions are direction, size and which claim the evidence actually
supports. The phrase *self-weighing works* runs together three different comparisons with three
different answers, and the decision depends on which one applies. [inferred from @madigan2015weigh]

## The evidence base

One systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of RCTs (Madigan 2015; searched to August
2014): 24 trials, 21 pooled, adults with overweight or obesity in **weight-loss** trials. Trials had
23 to 415 participants (median 110) and 15 of 24 ran in the USA; four enrolled only women and the
rest were 26 to 91% women (median 75%). Weight was measured objectively in all but one trial. Missing
weights were standardised to baseline weight carried forward (BOCF): «We used BOCF because this
mitigates bias that may arise because participants that do less well may be reluctant to be followed
up.» One trial was an exception: «One trial used a conservative method of imputation that was similar
to this imputation, by adding 0.5 kg to the last weight observed carried forwards. The trial was
included within the analysis as presented [21].» [@madigan2015weigh]

**What it cannot speak to: maintenance after loss.** The review excluded maintenance trials by design:
«Trials were excluded if they enrolled people after weight loss where the prime aim of the
intervention was to prevent weight regain or trials that enrolled people that aimed to help prevent
gradual weight gain.» [@madigan2015weigh]
Eleven full texts were excluded on these grounds. So *weighing helps you keep it off* is not tested
here; see the maintenance section below.

**Appraisal.** Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane tool; most trials gave too little
information to judge sequence generation or allocation concealment (low risk in 11 and 7 trials
respectively; 4 high risk on concealment), 6 were high risk on attrition (of 23 reporting trials, though the counts sum to 24; the Methods and Results also give different attrition thresholds —), and selective
reporting could be checked in only 4. The review gives no GRADE or other certainty rating and does
not assess publication bias. `[searched: GRADE / certainty / publication bias / funnel — zero across the source]`
[@madigan2015weigh]

**A second review with a different scope (Zheng 2014).** A narrative systematic review of 17 studies:
«On the basis of the remaining 13 RCTs, we further identified 4 relevant studies using the Cited Reference
Search strategy, bringing our total to 17 studies.» Its inclusion rule was RCTs or studies ancillary to
RCTs, and its tables state randomisation for only some of them
[@zheng2014weigh]; several are read
below as associations or quasi-randomised comparisons (table below). There is no reported pooled value. Of the 17, 12 are on weight loss or
maintenance and 5 on preventing weight gain. 15 ran in the USA and 2 in the UK; samples ran from 32 to 3768 people,
61-100% women and 62-91% White. It took in maintenance and gain-prevention studies, and psychological outcomes are one of its two
aims. It reports **no risk-of-bias or
certainty appraisal** and no search end date; the only appraisal language is that sample homogeneity
«also influenced the level of evidence». Weighing frequency was self-reported in 14 of the 17.
[@zheng2014weigh]
Madigan excluded maintenance and gain-prevention trials; it also set out to assess psychological
harm, and reports it in an adverse-events section. [@madigan2015weigh]
`[searched: quality / bias / GRADE / PRISMA / Jadad / level of evidence — only quality of life, a reference title and the one limitations sentence; srcgrep across the 1-chunk denominator]`

**The two reviews share five trials** (Gokee-LaRose 2009, Lally 2008, Linde 2011, Steinberg 2013,
VanWormer 2009) of Zheng's 17 and Madigan's 24; the review author lists do not overlap, though
Madigan's group ran one Zheng-included maintenance trial, and Madigan cites Zheng and searched its
reference list. Where the reviews agree on those five, the agreement is the same data read twice, so it
cannot count as type E.

## Three comparisons, three answers

All figures are between-group mean differences in weight at programme end (intervention minus
control). The text and Table 3 give no pooled control-arm weight change, so no absolute baseline sits
beside them.
[@madigan2015weigh]

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scale alone | no intervention | 1 (183) | -0.5 (-1.3 to 0.3) | — | — | end of a 3-month intervention |
| Self-weighing plus other self-regulation parts, added to a programme | the same programme | 4 (274) | -1.7 (-2.6 to -0.8) | 0% | -7.5 to 4.1 | programme end, 10 weeks to 7.2 months |

**Scale alone: insufficient evidence, not no effect.** One trial, run by the review's authors,
tested advice to self-weigh without a programme and found -0.5 kg (-1.3 to 0.3) after three months.
The authors' own reading moves in two steps: «How- ever, unlike Michie and colleagues, we found that
self- monitoring alone was ineffective for weight loss. However, only one study investigated this and
the estimate was imprecise enough to encompass effects that would be worthwhile.»
[@madigan2015weigh]

**Added to a programme: a direction, with the scale's share not isolated.** Four trials gave both
arms the same programme and added self-weighing to one arm. The review's Results text describes an extra
part in all four, though its sentence on three of them cites only two trials: one added training in using the scale like a blood glucose monitor plus weight feedback,
and «The other three trials gave participants the option to record their diet and physical activity
[38, 39].» The Methods had planned a split of this set: «Within this group, there were two subgroups: trials where self-weighing was the only addition to the behavioural programme and trials where several self-regulatory in- terventions including self-weighing were added to the behavioural programme.»
Neither the Results nor Fig. 2 reports that split (Fig. 2 pools the four as one set, and Table 2 codes
all four as the same programme plus «self-monitoring/ self-weighing techniques»), so whether any of the
four added the scale alone is unclear. The authors' summary: «There was evi- dence that adding advice to self-weigh to a
behavioural programme improves its effectiveness, but only four trials have assessed this, and the
estimate of effect was impre- cise and clouded by the use of other self-regulatory ele- ments.»
Heterogeneity was 0% (Fig. 2 prints tau² = 0.00) across the four, but the printed prediction interval
runs from -7.5 to 4.1 kg; an interval that wide is hard to square with a tau² of zero,
so the width may be a reporting error, though the authors' reading is: «in some
interventions participants would lose a considerable amount of weight but in others interventions
participants may gain weight.» Three of the four asked for daily weighing. In the forest plot (Fig. 2)
only one trial's interval excludes zero (Wing 2010, -1.9, -3.0 to -0.8); the other three cross it.
[@madigan2015weigh]
[@madigan2015weigh]

**How much the comparison arm weighed in one of the four is unclear (Zheng 2014).** Gokee-LaRose 2009
is in Madigan's add-on set, coded «Daily», at a 10-week timepoint. Zheng's row for it describes the
arms as: «During the 10 weekly group meetings, BSR were given digital memory scales and were
instructed to SW and use color zones, while SBT were not. After group sessions, SBT were encouraged to
SW weekly at home but were reminded to place primary emphasis on their behaviors, not on their
weight.» The same row then prints «At 10 wk, 95% BSR vs. 94% SBT SW daily.» and «At 20 wk, 70.6% BSR
SW daily vs. 17.6% SBT SW weekly; no SBT Ps SW daily.» Madigan defines the exposure by instruction:
«Self-weighing was defined as participants being asked to weigh themselves rather than being weighed as
part of a programme.» It excluded «n = 9 both groups self-weighing» trials but kept this one.
[@zheng2014weigh] [@madigan2015weigh] The row does not settle what the comparison arm was asked to do during the 10 weeks.
*After group sessions* can mean after each weekly session (the arm was asked to weigh weekly
throughout) or after the 10-week programme (it was not asked during it). Madigan keeping the trial
fits the second reading but does not prove it. The 94% daily figure fits neither instruction
cleanly and sits against the row's 20-week reading; it may be a misprint, or that arm weighed more
often than asked. Zheng's adherence text gives 90% of participants weighing daily at the end of the
10-week programme, which matches neither the 95% nor the 94% figure exactly and so does not decide
between the readings. So this trial's 10-week contrast
was the self-regulation package against either weekly weighing, daily weighing, or no instruction to
weigh. Either way the sign of the -1.7 kg is unchanged; the primary paper would settle it.

**A programme that includes it, against minimal care: the programme's effect.** The -3.4 kg compares a
whole multi-component programme that includes self-weighing with no or minimal intervention. The
authors' reading of it: «There was strong evidence that behavioural weight loss programmes that
incorporate self-weighing are more effective than minimal interventions.» Their scope statement covers
all the pooled numbers, though elsewhere they call the mean differences «the net effect of the
self-weighing inter- vention itself» and conclude «Self-weighing as part of a multicomponent programme
is effective in facilitating weight loss». The scope statement: «Our aim is not to produce a definitive estimate of the effect of self-
weighing on weight loss at a particular point in time; rather it is to find evidence that
self-weighing as a tech- nique is effective.» The next two sentences say how to read the numbers:
«As length of follow-up is the same in both intervention and control groups, any differences are down either to random variation or differences in effectiveness of self-weighing. Thus the estimates we produce should not be read as estimates of the effect of self-weighing, but as valid evidence that self-weighing can be effective in these contexts.»
They add: «It was impossible to separately code those
techniques used to promote self-weighing and those that related to other components of the
intervention.»
[@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] The -3.4 kg and the -1.7 kg come
from different trial sets, so the -3.4 kg cannot be split into a programme share and a scale share.
The closest estimate of adding self-weighing is the add-on comparison, -1.7 kg (-2.6 to -0.8), and it
too carries co-delivered parts, so it is not the scale's effect alone either; the authors' reading
above (evidence that self-weighing can be effective in these contexts, not an estimate of the effect of
self-weighing) applies to it too. Read -1.7 kg as the held magnitude for adding self-weighing, with its
co-delivered parts, to a programme like these, not as the effect of the scale by itself; and in one of
the four trials how often the comparison arm weighed is unclear (above).

## What the second review adds — associations inside trials, not effects

Zheng's inclusion rule asks for RCTs, but most of its weight findings are not randomised contrasts.
The common form compares people who weighed more often with people who weighed less often inside one
trial or cohort, which is observational. The review's design labels have to be read off its tables
[inferred from @zheng2014weigh]:

| Design of the weight result | Studies | What they found |
|---|---|---|
| Within-study frequency association | Sherwood 2006 (1801), Jeffery 1999 (809), VanWormer 2012 (1747), VanWormer 2009 (100; 46% vs 8% reaching 5% loss, «≥ weekly» vs «≤ weekly» as printed), Welsh 2009 (73), Gokee-LaRose 2009 (40), Funk 2010 (348, one arm), Wing 2006 (314) | more weighing went with more loss, less regain or less gain |

## How often to weigh — daily vs weekly is a between-trial contrast

Within the 15 programme-vs-minimal trials, seven asked for at least daily weighing (-3.6 kg, -5.4 to -1.8;
I² 91%) and eight for less than daily, seven of them weekly (-3.3 kg, -4.0 to -2.5; I² 65%); subgroup
difference p = 0.57. These are **different trials** with different programmes, not a randomised
comparison of frequencies: «Our analyses address- ing theoretical constructs were analyses across
trials and therefore observational, as no trial directly addressed these issues.» The authors
conclude: «There was no evidence that daily weighing led to greater weight loss than weekly weighing
and it ap- pears that both may be effective when combined with multi-component interventions.»
[@madigan2015weigh]

No difference shown is not equivalence. The daily subgroup's interval is wide (-5.4 to -1.8) and its
prediction interval (-10.2 to 3.0) crosses zero, while the less-than-daily prediction interval
(-4.6 to -1.0) does not. In the add-on set, weekly weighing rests on one trial (Allen); the other
three asked for daily. Adherence was measured in only three trials, all
daily per the text (44%, 50%, 95%), so whether people keep up daily weighing as well as weekly is
unknown. The frequency coding is not consistent inside Madigan: the text lists VanWormer
[21] among the at-least-daily trials («[18, 21, 35–37, 43, 44]») while Table 2 codes it «Weekly», and
Zheng describes Lally's arms as weekly vs monthly where Table 2 codes «Daily» (below), so the
daily and less-than-daily subgroups may not be cleanly split. A
within-trial randomised daily-vs-weekly trial was in progress when the review was written.
[@madigan2015weigh] — closes when a pooled
or single randomised daily-vs-weekly contrast inside the same programme is held.

**Zheng's frequency evidence does not settle it either.** Inside single studies, Welsh found daily
weighers lost more than those weighing weekly or less, and in VanWormer 2012 daily and weekly weighers
lost about 1.8 and 0.9 kg, «which was more than Ps who SW monthly»; both compare people who chose their
frequency, so they carry the reverse-causation problem above. Where arms were told to weigh at
different frequencies, Lally 2008 (104 people, 8 weeks) found «no difference between weekly and
monthly SW subgroups»; the row does not say whether those arms were randomised [an RCT in Madigan], and Madigan's table
codes the same trial's weighing frequency as «Daily». Zheng's authors: «Although at
this time, the evidence cannot not support endorsement of an ideal self- weighing frequency or
duration for use of this strategy, the recent work provides more evidence that daily weighing is an
acceptable strategy for weight loss intervention.» (*cannot not* as printed.) Gokee-LaRose 2009 also
gave one arm scales, an instruction to weigh and a self-regulation package during the 10 weeks
(Madigan codes it «Daily»), and encouraged the other to weigh weekly at home *after group sessions*
(timing unclear, above); its printed 10-week adherence is unclear (above).
[@zheng2014weigh]
[@madigan2015weigh]

**People weigh less often as time passes, at least early on.** In Gokee-LaRose 2009 about 90%
weighed daily at the end of the 10-week programme, falling significantly by 20 weeks; «This pattern of
initial high level of adherence to self-weighing followed by a gradual decrease was reported by others
(48,52).» A second study by the same group: 91% and 100% of the two arms daily at 8 weeks, 61% and 90%
at 16 weeks. The Discussion: «Although five studies reported short-term (<9 months) patterns of
self-weighing with a gradual decline over time, no studies have reported the pattern of self-weighing
over an extended time period» (the PDF's example is 12 months or more). Single readings at a year or
later do exist in the tables: in Keep It Off, about 60% of the guided arm and 43% of the self-directed
arm reported weighing daily at 24 months, and the text says participants weighed daily on 54% of days (the row: 1029 of 1932 surveys, 53.8%); in
Kong, 88% weighed at least weekly at 12 months.
[@zheng2014weigh]
[inferred from @zheng2014weigh] Under *adherence is part of the effect*: early decline is shown, the longer-term
course is described only by point readings inside supported trials, and the guided-vs-self-directed gap
in Keep It Off suggests support helps keep the habit. That favours picking the frequency you will keep
up, but it is not a shown dose difference.

## Accountability — between-trial, and mostly about weight lost

Trials where participants «knew that they were accountable to a therap- ist/researcher» (14 trials,
-3.6 kg, -4.6 to -2.7; I² 83%, prediction interval -7.5 to 0.3) beat the two without (-2.3 kg; Table 3
prints -3.2 to -1.5, the text -3.1 to -1.5; I² 0%, no prediction interval printed), p = 0.03. Two trials with strong accountability, where the therapist contacted anyone who did
not weigh, found -5.6 and -8.8 kg. The authors add that elsewhere the accountability «was more closely
related to weight lost and not the act of weighing», and the comparison is between trials, with only
two trials on one side. [@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] Read it as a hypothesis that
someone seeing the numbers helps, not as an established modifier: between-trial subgroups are held as
hypotheses (CLAUDE.md, the over-personalization rule).

**One within-trial subgroup, held as a hypothesis.** «Pacanowski reported a subgroup analysis from a
trial of self-weighing that found people with in- ternal weight locus of control and males lost more
weight [44]. However, indicating that on average some groups respond better does not necessarily make
these predictors useful screening tools to exclude people from self-weighing.»
[@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] A single trial's subgroup, reported secondhand, not known to be prespecified: a
candidate modifier, not a reason to advise anyone differently.

## Maintenance and durability — what is and is not held

[@madigan2015weigh] Three trials followed
people beyond the last contact: -8.0 kg (-12.5 to -3.5) about 18 months after, -0.3 kg six months
after a single-session programme, and -7.5 kg (-11.3 to -3.7) nine months after; pooled across 185
people, -5.5 kg (printed interval -11.4 to 4.7), I² 86%. That interval is lopsided
around -5.5, its lower bound repeats the second trial's printed lower bound, and the same Table 3
row prints «0.04» in the column that holds the effect P for the other rows, which fits an interval
excluding zero; so the interval, or the P, is probably misprinted. The three come from
different comparison types (an add-on trial, a single-session programme, a programme vs minimal care).
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] So the pool does not estimate the durability of any one of the three comparisons above. Two of the
three single trials have intervals that exclude zero, but each is one small trial of a different
comparison, the pool mixes comparison types with I² 86%, and its printed interval and P disagree;
durability is thinly evidenced, not established.

The maintenance evidence the review cites is observational, and the authors lean toward it. They
suggest self-weighing may suit the long term because it is easy to keep up, and cite the National
Weight Control Registry: «Additionally because self-weighing is less cumbersome than recording diet and
physical activity it might be a behaviour that can be continued and therefore help weight control in
the longer term. The National Weight Control Registry has found that those who are successful at
preventing weight regain, after weight loss, weigh themselves on a regular basis [46].» On a separate
prospective study (ref 48; the quote's closing marker cites a different paper) they add a caveat: «Higher weighing frequency was associated with greater weight loss
and less weight regain at 24 months follow-up. However, greater motivation to maintain weight or
success in achieving weight mainten- ance may motivate people to weigh themselves frequently, which
makes observational data difficult to interpret [49].»
[@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] The authors' caveat names two
alternatives, motivation (a confounder) and success prompting the weighing (reverse causation); both
would apply to the registry association too, though they do not apply it there. The direction they suggest is plausible; no randomised maintenance estimate is
held. Self-weighing for maintenance is therefore insufficient evidence in the held fabric, not no
effect -> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]. (Superseded in part 2026-10-29: one
quasi-randomised estimate is now held secondhand, below; the state moves from insufficient evidence
to one low-certainty estimate.)

**Updated 2026-10-29 (Zheng 2014): one quasi-randomised estimate, held secondhand.** Zheng includes
the Madigan group's maintenance trial, one of only two studies the review describes as isolating
self-weighing in delivery (the intervention still bundled the offer of free weighing scales, calls and a record card).
People who had recently lost weight were offered free weighing scales, two phone calls and encouragement to
weigh weekly and record it on a card (3290 people), against no maintenance intervention (478). Over
12 months (3 months of intervention, 9 of follow-up) the intervention arm regained 1.23 ± 5.8 kg and
the control arm 1.83 kg (printed «1.83-5.5 kg»); «Adjusting for covariates resulted in a mean
difference of 0.68 kg (95% CI: 0.12-1.24) at 12-mo follow-up.» The arms differed at baseline (86.7%
vs 68.0% women; BMI 35.1 vs 33.7). [@zheng2014weigh]
The other maintenance findings in Zheng are associations inside trials: daily weighing went with a
lower risk of regaining 2.3 kg or more in STOP Regain (314) and weight entries with less regain in
Funk (348, one arm), and less frequent weighing with gain in Sherwood 2006 (1801); Keep It Off (419) reports only that guided participants were more likely to
weigh daily (OR 1.97, 1.31 to 2.99), with no weight result in the row.
[@zheng2014weigh]
[inferred from @zheng2014weigh] So maintenance moves
from no held trial estimate to one: about 0.7 kg less regain over a year, against a control-arm
regain of about 1.8 kg, from a quasi-randomised trial with unequal arms, reported through a review that
did not appraise it. That is a small, imprecise estimate from a quasi-randomised trial; the primary trial and a pooled
estimate are not held. — the same open
token as on [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]].

## Preventing weight gain — two small isolated tests and a null programme trial

Zheng's five gain-prevention studies are a different stratum from the rest of this page: the aim is
to avoid gain rather than to lose, and none is an obesity-treatment sample (mean BMI 26.7 to 29.6 where
given; Levitsky's first-year college women weighed about 62 kg; 31% of VanWormer 2012's worksite staff
had a BMI under 25), though the abstract describes the review's participants as «adults seeking weight loss treatment». Levitsky 2006 ran two one-semester studies in first-year college women
(mean weight about 62 kg; 32 and 41 people; allocation not stated; retention 68.8% vs 93.8% in the
first study and 94.1% vs 66.7% in the second, intervention vs control) and is the other study that
isolated the scale: daily weighing with e-mailed weights gained 0.1 ± 0.99 vs 3.1 ± 0.51 kg
(P=0.001), and in the second study lost 0.82 ± 0.56 vs gained 2.0 ± 0.65 kg (P<0.01). French 2011
randomised households (90 participants) to a programme with daily weighing; at follow-up 71% vs 36% weighed weekly or more, with no significant BMI difference. Jeffery 1999 (809, 3 years) and VanWormer 2012 (1747 at six
worksites, 2 years) are associations: daily weighers in Jeffery lost weight while every other
category gained.
[@zheng2014weigh]
[inferred from @zheng2014weigh] The direction favours
weighing in two very small studies whose allocation is not described and whose arms lost different
shares of participants, and the one household-randomised
trial was null on BMI. That is insufficient evidence, not no effect, and it does not carry to the
weight-loss comparisons above.

## The scale monitors the outcome, not a behaviour

Madigan separates self-weighing from the self-monitoring that behaviour-change reviews found effective
on its own: «Additionally self-weighing is different to the behaviours investigated by Michie and
colleagues, as self- weighing is monitoring the outcome rather than the be- haviour.» Their proposed
mechanism is that people must reflect on the reading and then change eating and activity, which a
scale alone may not prompt. [@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] This is why a scale is not the
same tool as an activity tracker, which monitors the behaviour itself -> [[Wearable Activity Trackers and Physical Activity]].

## Open question — psychological harm

Whether regular weighing causes psychological harm (body dissatisfaction, low mood, disordered eating)
is **not settled by this review**. Most included trials did not report adverse effects; the three
that measured psychological outcomes reported no between-group differences, and the authors' own
caveat is that «few trials assessed whether self-weighing led to psychological problems». Two of

## Labelled default — what to do

[inferred from @madigan2015weigh; @zheng2014weigh] The action is cheap, reversible
and low-cost, so it gets a plain default with its basis stated (CLAUDE.md, firmness scales with
stakes; [[Acting on a Direction Without a Magnitude]]):

- **If you are in a weight-loss programme, weigh yourself regularly** — daily or weekly, whichever you
  will keep doing. Basis: four RCTs adding self-weighing to a programme, each with a co-delivered
  part per the review's Results text (an option to record diet and activity in three, self-regulation
  training with weight feedback in one; the Methods' planned self-weighing-only subgroup is never
  reported): -1.7 kg (-2.6 to -0.8) at programme end within about 7 months, printed prediction
  interval -7.5 to 4.1 (flagged above as possibly misreported); no heterogeneity was detected
  across the four trials (I² 0%). The scale's own share is not isolated.
- **Frequency is your choice.** Basis: a between-trial comparison across programme trials
  (p = 0.57); in the add-on set weekly rests on one trial; no randomised daily-vs-weekly test is held.
  Zheng's within-study associations favour more frequent weighing but compare people who chose their
  frequency; weighing tends to drop off in the first months, and the longer course is known only from
  point readings inside supported trials.
- **Someone seeing the numbers may help** (a coach, a programme, a person). Basis: between-trial,
  two trials on one side; a hypothesis.
- **Without a programme, a scale on its own is untested for weight loss beyond one trial.** Trying it costs little;
  pair it with a plan for eating and activity, since the authors' proposed mechanism is that the
  reading must lead to a change in behaviour. Basis: one 3-month trial, -0.5 kg (-1.3 to 0.3), which
  the authors call imprecise enough to include a worthwhile effect: insufficient evidence, not proof
  of no effect; the pairing is the page's inference from the mechanism, not a tested combination.
- **After a loss, keep weighing, at least weekly.** Basis: one quasi-randomised maintenance trial
  reported through Zheng, whose package (the offer of free weighing scales, two phone calls, a record card) encouraged weekly
  weighing, so the estimate belongs to the package, not the scale alone (more often is supported only by
  associations), about 0.7 kg less regain over 12 months (0.68, 0.12 to 1.24) against a
  control regain of about 1.8 kg (how the outcome weight was measured is not stated), plus associations inside trials; small, and the primary trial is not
  held.
- **Stop if it distresses you.** The harm question is open, and stopping costs little. Basis: about
  six small studies across both reviews, all in adults with overweight or obesity trying to lose or
  keep off weight (three between-group trial results, two within-study associations, and Kong, whose
  row gives no psychological result), detected no excess
  harm; one shared trial is reported differently by the two reviews. Neither review's psychological evidence covers adolescents, people with or at risk of an eating
  disorder, or normal-weight people not trying to lose weight; both cite adolescent or
  normal-weight studies only as background (not held here), and no included study is described as sampling
  people with or at risk of an eating disorder, so for these groups the held evidence does not speak to harm.

- **Trying not to gain: weighing is cheap to try; the effect is not established.** Basis: two very small
  studies in first-year college women favour daily weighing, but their allocation is not described and
  their arms kept different shares of participants; the one household-randomised trial (90 people) was
  null on BMI. Insufficient evidence, not no effect. The only psychological measure in the
  gain-prevention studies is acceptability: in Gokee-LaRose 2010 «Daily SW was perceived positively in
  both groups at 8 wk with no group differences.» Zheng reports no measure of body dissatisfaction,
  mood or disordered eating for them, so the harm evidence above does not reach this group; *stop if it distresses you*
  applies. [inferred from @zheng2014weigh]

**Where it ranks.** A small refinement inside a programme, not a big rock. The closest estimate of
adding it is -1.7 kg (-2.6 to -0.8) on a weight surrogate, at programme end within about 7 months, with
co-delivered parts, a printed prediction interval crossing zero (its width flagged above as possibly
misreported) and thinly evidenced durability
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]. For keeping weight off, the one held trial estimate,
for a weekly-weighing package (the offer of free weighing scales, two calls, a record card), is about 0.7 kg less regain over a
year; it measures a different outcome (regain, not loss), comparator and design than the -1.7 kg, so the
two are not ranked against each other.
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh; @zheng2014weigh]

**Guidance context.** At the time, «Currently, widely used behavioural programmes in the UK advise
their participants against weighing themselves.» That advice ran against the direction of this
review's add-on finding; no current guidance position on self-weighing is held.
[@madigan2015weigh]
Zheng reports, secondhand, that the US position went the other way: «Self-weighing has also been
recommended as part of the AHA/ACC/TOS Guidelines for Managing Overweight and Obesity in Adults from
the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (24).» The guideline itself (Jensen 2014) is not held, so
its wording and grading are unread. [@zheng2014weigh]

## Limits

- **Weight is a surrogate.** Weight is the only efficacy outcome pooled; three trials measured
  psychological outcomes and none pooled a patient-important outcome. [@madigan2015weigh]
- **Short.** Programme-end figures; the add-on trials ran 10 weeks to 7.2 months. [@madigan2015weigh]
- **Mostly US trials, mostly women** (15 of 24 in the USA; four women-only trials, median 75% women
  in the rest; 11 internet-based or mixed). [@madigan2015weigh]
- **No certainty grading** and no publication-bias assessment; trial counts per comparison are small
  (1, 4, 15). [@madigan2015weigh]
- ~~**Single source.** The page rests on one review; a second review of the same lever is the next
  source, not yet held.~~ Superseded 2026-10-29 (Zheng 2014): two reviews now, sharing five trials, and
  the second has no quality appraisal and no reported pooled value, so its weight-loss findings add associations, not
  a second effect estimate. [inferred from @madigan2015weigh; @zheng2014weigh]
- **Exposure self-reported.** In Zheng, 14 of 17 studies measured weighing frequency by self-report.
  [@zheng2014weigh]
- **One stratum.** Mostly women in both reviews (Madigan: above); Zheng's samples were also
  predominantly White and middle-aged, which its authors call the main limitation.
  [@zheng2014weigh]
