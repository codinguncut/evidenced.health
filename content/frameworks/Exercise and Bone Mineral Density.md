**The efficacy question for exercise on the bone surrogate in postmenopausal women — and the
surrogate discipline that gates what the answer is worth.** The safety page
([[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]]) asks whether exercise *breaks* a fragile skeleton; the falls
page ([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]) asks whether it prevents the *fall* that
fractures one. This page asks the third, distinct decision: does loading the skeleton **raise bone
mineral density (BMD)**, and — the load-bearing question — does a BMD gain actually mean **fewer
fractures**, the outcome a person cares about. First source held: Mohebbi 2023, a gold updated
SR-MA (80 studies, 94 training and 80 control groups, 5581 participants pooled) of controlled
exercise trials >= 6 months, applying the inverse-heterogeneity (IVhet) model.
[@mohebbi2023]

## The effect on the surrogate: real, positive, small-to-moderate

Exercise raises BMD at all three sites, but the effect is modest and the magnitude framing is the
source's own:

- **Lumbar spine (LS):** SMD 0.29 (95% CI 0.16-0.42); heterogeneity substantial (I2 = 68%), and
  **publication-bias/small-study evidence HIGH** at this site.
- **Femoral neck (FN):** SMD 0.27 (95% CI 0.16-0.39).
- **Total hip (TH):** SMD 0.41 (95% CI 0.30-0.52); heterogeneity low (I2 = 20%), publication-bias
  evidence negligible.

[@mohebbi2023]

The authors classify the size plainly: «the average SMD for BMD effects on LS, FN, and TH can be
classified as moderate at best (i.e., 0.2 to 0.5)»
[@mohebbi2023].
So the honest headline is a **small positive standardized effect on a surrogate**, best-evidenced and
cleanest at the total hip, and weakest (noisiest, most bias-suspect) at the lumbar spine.

## No significant moderator — no modification shown on the *for whom* question

The update's stated purpose was to find **effect modification** — who benefits more. It found none.
Across three planned group comparisons (named in the review's stated aims) the exercise effect on
BMD did **not** differ significantly (all p > .09):

- **osteopenia/osteoporosis vs normal BMD** (bone status),
- **early vs late postmenopausal** (menopausal status),
- **predominantly supervised vs predominantly non-supervised** programs.

[@mohebbi2023]

This is a **route-(b) absence of shown modification**: the search for a subgroup with a larger *relative* BMD effect came up
empty on the three axes most expected to modify it. It does **not** deny route-(a) stratification —
absolute fracture benefit still scales with baseline fracture risk even when the relative BMD effect
is flat ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]) — but it removes the positive
interaction evidence that would license personalizing the *exercise* prescription by bone or
menopausal status. The high residual heterogeneity (I2 up to 68%) says trials differ a lot in
effect, but the measured moderators do not explain it.

The authors judge their subgroups large enough, with few exceptions. They note some evidence of a
participant-by-exercise interaction, but on review they saw no striking intensity differences
between the bone- or menopause-status cohorts:
«Of importance, with few exceptions (e.g., meno- pausal status: subgroup-analyses for TH-ROI), the number of studies included in the subgroups was high enough to exclude a predominate random effect. Nevertheless, there is some evidence that interaction effects between partici- pant and exercise parameters in particular have affected our findings. As an example, it is plausible that more intense exercise protocols were applied in the younger, i.e., early postmenopausal cohorts or participants with a lower frac- ture risk, i.e., participants without osteopenia/osteoporosis. Reviewing the studies, however, we observed no striking dif- ferences in exercise intensity (or training frequency) between the cohorts with diverging bone or menopausal status. Even so, a simple adjustment of the subgroup analysis for exer- cise intensity is debatable, since the relevance of exercise intensity on a given outcome depends on other variables such as training frequency.»
[@mohebbi2023]

The null is still **absence of shown modification, not evidence of no modification**: these are
between-study subgroup comparisons, not within-trial tests. So the default is *not to personalize*
on these axes, held as a default rather than a settled null.

## The load-bearing limit: BMD is a SURROGATE, and transmission to fracture is not shown here

[inferred from @mohebbi2023] — Mohebbi
measures BMD, **not fracture**. BMD is a surrogate for the patient-important outcome (fracture,
and downstream function/independence), and the whole surrogate discipline applies
([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). Two cautions run in **opposite** directions and both matter:

- **BMD may UNDER-capture exercise's true fracture benefit.** Exercise prevents fractures through at
  least two channels, and BMD sees only one. The **bone-loading channel** (mechanostat: load raises
  BMD) is what this page measures; the **falls channel** (balance, strength, gait -> fewer falls ->
  fewer fractures) is a second, largely **BMD-independent** route to fewer fractures
  ([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]: exercise cuts *falls*, RaR \~0.77 — a falls
  rate ratio, not a fracture effect). The held fracture MA cannot say which channel carries more of
  the fracture reduction: «Considerable differences in addressing the two training aims confounded a joint analysis for meaningful exercise parameters and train- ing principles.»
  [@hoffmann2022].
  A BMD meta-analysis is structurally blind to the falls channel — the streetlight effect on the
  outcome side.
- **A small statistical BMD gain may NOT transmit to meaningful fracture reduction.** The corpus
  already holds a worked **inversion** of exactly the BMD->fracture link: GRADE's handbook names
  «bone density for fractures» as a *proximate* surrogate (rate down only one level)
  [@grade], yet testosterone, which raised BMD in the earlier
  Testosterone Trials, did not lower, and numerically raised, clinical fractures in the TRAVERSE RCT
  (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.04-1.97, unadjusted for multiplicity; BMD was not measured in TRAVERSE itself):
  «testosterone treatment did not result in a lower incidence of clinical fracture than placebo. The fracture incidence was numerically higher among men who received testosterone than among those who received placebo.»
  and «In accordance with the statistical analysis plan, no adjustment was made for multiple com- parisons. All confidence intervals are unadjust- ed and are not a substitute for hypothesis tests.»
  [@snyder2024] ->
  [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. Most fractures in both arms were associated with trauma, more commonly
  with falls; traumatic events and falls were not prespecified end points, though clinically significant trauma was
  captured through serious-adverse-event reporting
  [@snyder2024]. Falls, activity and
  risk-taking were not assessed, so they cannot be excluded as the channel:
  «The increase in serum testosterone con- centrations during treatment was less than in some other studies, but the lesser increase could not explain an increase in fractures. Information about falls was not assessed, except in partici- pants who reported fractures. Physical activity and risk taking were also not assessed.»
  [@snyder2024]. The only mechanism the
  authors suggest is a bone-quality one, offered as speculation and resting on one prior study:
  «Because we did not expect these results, we did not design the trial to assess possible mech- anisms by which testosterone would increase the incidence of fractures, so we can only speculate about possible mechanisms. Although previous studies showed that testosterone treatment in men with hypogonadism improved many mea- sures of bone structure, especially of trabecular bone, one study showed that testosterone treat- ment in men with severe hypogonadism decreased cortical bone volume fraction and cortical bone axial thickness, a measure of bone strength.7»
  [@snyder2024]. The channel is
  unresolved. Even so, a proximate
  surrogate plus mechanism plus a consistently-moving marker did not deliver the expected fracture
  reduction. That case is a different exposure (a drug, not loading) and cannot be transported to
  exercise, but it is the standing proof that a BMD gain is not self-evidently a fracture reduction.
  The corpus also holds a **non-drug inversion** — [[Fluoride and Bone Health]], where fracture risk
  rises above an approximate \~1.5 mg/L water-fluoride threshold
  [@mazzoli2025fluoride]; that page carries the
  density-up / quality-down mechanism.
- **Three exposures, three different fracture stories — no single mechanism shown.** Exercise, fluoride and
  testosterone each raise BMD, with different fracture outcomes. For exercise, the fracture fall
  cannot be attributed to the BMD gain (the fracture trials mix programmes, next sections). Fluoride
  and testosterone both have a bone-quality account on the table: fluoride's is carried on
  [[Fluoride and Bone Health]]; testosterone's is speculative and rests on one prior study, with
  falls and behaviour unassessed (above). That a single mechanism, such as *how the density was
  gained*, explains all three is a hypothesis, not a finding
  -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]] (the three-exposure cluster).

[inferred from @mohebbi2023]
Mohebbi measures BMD, not fracture, and points at the fracture SR from its own group — «the
favorable effect of supervised exercise protocols on fracture»
[@mohebbi2023].
That cited SR (its ref [7]: Hoffmann, Shojaa, Kohl et al., *J Bone Miner Res* 2022, «Does
supervision make a difference?»
[@mohebbi2023])
is **not held**. What is held is a **sibling** fracture SR from the same group (Hoffmann 2022/2023,
*Osteoporos Int*, next section), which tests intensity progression and duration, not supervision.
It measures the patient-important fracture outcome directly, but for **mixed** exercise programmes
(bone-loading, falls-prevention and combined), so it does not isolate the loading channel — and,
being from the **same group**, it refines rather than independently corroborates.
[inferred from @hoffmann2022]

## The fracture endpoint is now held: exercise reduces major osteoporotic fractures (type-F)

Hoffmann 2022 measures the patient-important outcome directly, the one the BMD above is a surrogate
for. It is a gold SR-MA (11 controlled exercise studies; 9715 exercise vs 9592 control
participant-years; mixed-effects conditional Poisson) — the fracture-incidence MA this page had been
holding open. Exercise cuts **major osteoporotic fracture (MOF)**: pooled «RR: 0.75, 95% CI:
0.54–0.94, p = .006» [@hoffmann2022]
(the Results section reports the same 0.75 with a slightly tighter lower bound, 95% CI 0.59-0.94
[@hoffmann2022] — a within-paper
discrepancy; both exclude 1.0; the Conclusion states a different figure again — «Our systematic review and meta-analysis showed a 23% reduc- tion in major osteoporotic fracture incidence»
[@hoffmann2022]). Heterogeneity was negligible (I2 <1%) with no funnel-plot asymmetry.
[@hoffmann2022]
MOF is defined per FRAX (hip, vertebral, forearm/wrist, humerus), a low-trauma / fragility endpoint,
though «in a minor variation from FRAX, all types of humerus and vertebral fractures were included»
[@hoffmann2022]. The authors also
concede «due to unavailable data, we might have not included only “low-trauma fractures.”», and judge
that this limitation might be negligible
[@hoffmann2022].
The programmes pooled are mixed: «The exercise program of three studies focused predomi- nately on
combined fall prevention/bone strengthening [25, 27, 28] or fall prevention protocols [26, 30, 32],
while four studies [18, 24, 29, 31] concentrated on bone strengthening»
[@hoffmann2022] — so the pooled MOF
reduction shows *exercise -> fewer fractures*, not *BMD gain -> fewer fractures*; part of it may run
through the BMD-independent falls channel.
[inferred from @hoffmann2022]

- **Absolute frame** [inferred from @hoffmann2022]: from the
  raw counts (151 MOF in 9715 EG participant-years vs 196 in 9592 CG) the crude rate falls \~2.0 ->
  \~1.6 per 100 participant-years — roughly one MOF averted per \~200 participant-years of exercise.
  Crude (the pooled RR 0.75 is the meta-analytic, study-conditioned estimate, not a
  covariate-adjusted one), shown only to size the rock.
- **Not every MOF component fell.** Forearm/wrist fractures ran the other way — «62 (EG) vs. 52 (CG) forearm and wrist fractures were reported» — and the vertebral counts are incomplete:
  «Unfortunately, some studies did not report vertebral fractures; thus, the number of 25 fractures in the EG vs. 49 in the CG might be considerably underreported.»
  [@hoffmann2022]. This does not
  negate the pooled RR, but the composite hides component heterogeneity.
- **This is a type-F refinement, not independent (type-E) corroboration.** Hoffmann is the SAME
  Erlangen/Kemmler group as Mohebbi (Kemmler, Kohl, von Stengel, Jakob, Kerschan-Schindl, Lange,
  Peters, Thomasius, Uder shared — 9 authors) drawing on an overlapping data pool and shared methods. It supplies the
  outcome leg the BMD MA lacked; it does not independently confirm the BMD effect. The wiki still
  holds no independent group's exercise-fracture MA — the robustness gap persists.
  [inferred from @hoffmann2022]
- **The protocol question stays open — which is where the BMD-moderator nulls and the fracture
  data agree.** Hoffmann's two planned moderators show no significant difference: intensity
  progression (p=.133) and study duration (p=.883; the per-arm estimates, >12 months IR 0.77, 95% CI
  0.61-0.97, vs the wide-CI short arm IR 0.70, 95% CI 0.23-2.15, do not differ — a non-significant
  subgroup contrast, not a duration effect)
  [@hoffmann2022]. These are
  **protocol** features, not strata, and the tests are between-study. The authors rate their
  subgroup power moderate at best, and judge that the analysis does not seem hopelessly underpowered:
  «(7) Finally, the statistical power to address differences between the subgroups can be considered moderate at best. Nev- ertheless, a recent (meta-)analysis on major osteoporotic fracture reduction that focused on supervision of the exercise program revealed significant differences in favor of supervised exercise protocols {Hoffmann, 2022 #16145}. Thus, the present analysis does not seem to be “hopelessly underpowered.”»
  [@hoffmann2022]. The authors decline
  to derive a prescription: «diverging study and exercise characteristics along with the close
  interaction of exercise parameters prevented the derivation of reliable recommendations for
  exercise protocols for fracture reductions»
  [@hoffmann2022]. So on **both** the
  surrogate (Mohebbi's bone-status / menopause / supervision nulls) and the **patient-important
  outcome** (Hoffmann's progression / duration tests), the *effect is real but no modifier is shown
  to separate*. On the fracture leg these are **protocol questions that remain unresolved**
  (between-study; power moderate at best by the authors' account), not a route-(b) stratum null.

## The supervision discordance — null on the surrogate, a second-hand fracture signal

A *possible* instance of why the surrogate can mislead, pending the unheld supervision SR. The
review had **hypothesised** before its analysis that supervision would matter more for bone —
«Considering the higher complexity of exercise protocols for improving BMD compared to decreasing the number of falls [8], the role of supervision might be even more important in the area of bone strengthening. In the present comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis, we thus aimed to»
[@mohebbi2023] —
but its BMD data did not confirm it: «However, our approach does not determine a significant difference between predominantly supervised versus non-super- vised exercise protocols, albeit with slightly higher effects of supervised protocols for BMD at the LS, FN, and TH.»
[@mohebbi2023].
In the next sentence the authors concede that supervision-related exercise parameters diluted the
result, since some exercise types (dynamic resistance) need more supervision than others (jumping)
[@mohebbi2023].
The fracture-side supervision effect is a same-group report from a different population — «a sig- nificant superiority of supervision (versus predominantly non- supervised protocols) on overall and main osteoporotic fracture incidence in middle-aged to older adults [7]»
[@mohebbi2023]
(adults, not only postmenopausal women). That supervision SR (*J Bone Miner Res*
2022) is not held; Hoffmann's only other mention of it cites the same group's paper
[@hoffmann2022], so it is not
corroboration; and the held Hoffmann fracture SR does not test supervision.
So a program feature that is **null on the BMD surrogate** may still bear on the **patient-important
outcome** (adherence, dose fidelity, safety) — a caution against reading *no BMD difference* as *no
decision difference*. [inferred from @mohebbi2023]

## Confidence, and the non-independence caveat

`confidence: medium-high` is the composite of legs that differ: BMD *existence* would be high;
BMD *magnitude* and the decision-relevant **fracture** leg are moderate (below).

- **BMD-surrogate efficacy exists and is positive: well-evidenced** (gold updated SR-MA, positive
  at all three sites, robust IVhet model) — «sensitivity analysis
  on imputation strategy consistently showed signifi- cant effects». The *magnitude* is less certain,
  most at the lumbar spine (I2 68%), and at every site «outcomes for BMD at the LS, FN and TH varied considerably depending on whether
  imputation was conducted with mean, minimum, or maximum correlation»
  [@mohebbi2023].
  The abstract rates publication bias «Evidence for publication bias/small study effects was negligibly low (FN-, TH-BMD) to high (LSBMD).»
  [@mohebbi2023].
  The lumbar-spine signal is real, and the effect remains significant after trim-and-fill adjustment
  (no adjusted effect size reported):
  «The analysis imputes nine missing studies on the left-hand side (i.e., favors control group). However, even the corrected, i.e., imputation-adjusted, intervention effect remains significant (p = 0.008). The significant asym- metry was confirmed by the LFK Index (1.50), the regres- sion (p = 0.011), and the rank correlation test (p = 0.016).»
  [@mohebbi2023].
  The Discussion, from the funnel/Doi plots and tests, judges the chance that small-study effects confound the finding limited:
  «On the other hand, inspecting funnel and Doi plots, LFK indices, rank, and regression tests led to the conclusion that the probability of small study effects (publication bias, outcome reporting bias, dissemination bias, etc.) with confounding effects on our find- ing [128] is limited.»
  [@mohebbi2023].
- **Moderators: no modification shown / low** — no effect modification shown on bone status,
  menopausal status, or supervision (Mohebbi; between-study comparisons, with subgroup sizes the
  authors judge adequate with few exceptions, menopausal status at the total hip among them), so default to not personalizing on them; intensity
  progression and duration (Hoffmann) are non-significant between-study tests, not evidence of no
  effect.
- **Exercise -> fracture: now DIRECTLY measured, graded moderate.** Hoffmann
  2022 (gold SR-MA) shows exercise cuts major osteoporotic fracture (RR 0.75, CI excludes 1.0), so
  the *exercise -> fracture* outcome is no longer a gap. But its trials mix falls-prevention,
  combined and bone-loading programmes, so it does **not** isolate the loading channel:
  **BMD -> fracture transmission for loading is still not shown**, and the surrogate caution above
  stands. What also caps it below high: the fracture MA is from the **same group** (non-independent,
  below), the effect is moderate with a CI near 1.0, and no protocol modifier separates.
  [inferred from @hoffmann2022]

**Non-independence (binding).** Mohebbi is the **Erlangen/Kemmler group** (Kemmler, von Stengel,
Shojaa, Jakob among the authors) and is an explicit **update** of Shojaa et al. — «further evidence»
in the source's own words. **Hoffmann 2022, the fracture endpoint, is the SAME group** (9 shared
authors) — so the surrogate (BMD) and outcome (fracture) legs are not independent of each other:
convergence across these reviews reflects a shared team, data pool, and method, and must not inflate
confidence. The wiki holds **no independent research group's** exercise-BMD *or* exercise-fracture
meta-analysis yet — a genuine robustness gap for both the surrogate effect and its outcome
confirmation.

## The author's own verdict on meta-analytic precision here

Notably, the source itself doubts that pooling delivers a prescription: «their practical application
for deriving dedicated and reliable exercise recommendations is rather limited»
[@mohebbi2023].
The high between-trial heterogeneity means the *mean* SMD hides wide variation in what individual
protocols achieve — the pooled number says exercise-in-general nudges BMD, not which program a
person should do. The load/impact/dose specifics live in the programming frontier
([[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]), kept peripheral by Layer-1.

## Creatine adds no BMD benefit *shown* to loading (bounds the supplement space)

A natural follow-on question — can a supplement *amplify* resistance training's bone effect? — has
no pooled benefit shown for the most-touted candidate. Forbes 2018, a gold MA (5 RCTs, n = 193, adults >50 /
postmenopausal), pooled creatine + resistance training vs resistance training + placebo and found no
added BMD at any site (whole body, hip, femoral neck, lumbar spine — all pooled MD \~0, CIs within
about ±0.01–0.03 g/cm²), despite every trial showing creatine enhanced muscle mass or strength:
«these adaptations did not translate to greater BMD»
[@forbes2018creatine]. The MA is small and
mostly at high risk of bias, two of its five trials were individually positive, and the authors call
the findings equivocal: «Two of the studies reported signiﬁcant beneﬁts of creatine supplementation and resistance training compared to resistance training alone on bone.» and
«Only one study demonstrated a low risk of bias [18]. These biases may have led to the equivocal ﬁndings.»
[@forbes2018creatine]. So creatine's
augmentation of the loading effect on the BMD surrogate is **no-effect-shown over the studied
durations (12 to 52 weeks; [@forbes2018creatine]), insufficient beyond** — the muscle-yes / bone-no dissociation is the authors' own reading, and it
bounds the adjunct space provisionally
[inferred from @forbes2018creatine]. Two escape hatches stay
**insufficient** rather than closed: bone *geometry / strength* (one 12-month trial increased femoral
shaft subperiosteal width, which BMD cannot see) and longer / higher-frequency / relative-dose
protocols showed positive signals
[@forbes2018creatine]; and no creatine
trial measured **fracture**
[inferred from @forbes2018creatine]. Full appraisal:
[[Creatine Supplementation]] (Bone leg).

## Decision relevance

- **The exposure is worth pulling, and the fracture case is now direct.** For a postmenopausal woman,
  exercise produces a small positive BMD effect on a surrogate AND a measured reduction in major
  osteoporotic fractures (Hoffmann RR 0.75, pooled over female and male cohorts aged >50 on average
  — transferred, not stratum-specific) — plus the largely BMD-independent falls channel
  ([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]). Frame the recommendation on the **composite**
  (bone + falls + strength + function), not on the BMD number alone. The fracture endpoint shows that
  exercise cuts fractures, but Hoffmann's trials mix bone-loading and falls-prevention programmes, so
  it does not apportion that cut to the bone leg; the modifiers (which protocol) stay unresolved.
- **Practice default (standard practice, specifics not trial-resolved).** A sustained, progressive
  resistance or impact-loading programme as tolerated, combined with balance / falls-prevention
  work, supervised where feasible. *Progressive* and *supervised* are standard practice here, not
  shown modifiers: Hoffmann's progression test was non-significant (p = .133), and supervision was
  non-significant on BMD (Mohebbi), with the fracture-side supervision SR not held. Expect a modest
  BMD effect and fewer major osteoporotic fractures (RR 0.75, same-group MA, pooled over female and male
  cohorts aged >50 on average — transferred, not stratum-specific). *Sustained* is an adherence / practice
  point — an exercise not kept up has no effect — not a shown duration effect: Hoffmann's duration
  test was non-significant (p = .883), so >12 months is not an evidenced modifier. The mixed
  bone + falls content mirrors what the pooled programmes did, and Hoffmann points prescriptions to
  «more dedicated meta- analyses in the area of osteoporosis [38–40] or fall reduction [2, 41] or even better: from randomized controlled trials with similar or comparable training aims and cohorts»
  [@hoffmann2022]. Low harm in
  Hoffmann's framing — exercise works «without causing relevant adverse effects [5]»
  [@hoffmann2022], a second-hand
  citation; for a fragile skeleton, check movement selection on
  [[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]].
- **Non-substitutable by the obvious drug — for the pleiotropy, not the BMD.** Second-hand via
  Hoffmann's discussion (a self-described «(very) rough overview»), antiresorptive and anabolic drugs
  cut fragility fractures by roughly «20% (risedronate, non-vertebral fractures) to 54% (teriparatide, overall fragility fractures)»,
  in secondary-prevention cohorts with a higher fracture-prevention potential
  [@hoffmann2022]. For primary
  prevention Hoffmann rates the drug evidence far weaker: «Evidence for a fracture preventing effect in the area of primary prevention is much lower to negligible (e.g., [42]).»
  [@hoffmann2022]. So for the
  **bone/fracture outcome** such a drug would shrink the exercise rock in the secondary-prevention
  stratum, much less so in primary prevention. And exercise is **pleiotropic** (falls, muscle, gait, cardiometabolic,
  mortality -> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]) and a single-channel drug does not
  substitute for those other-channel benefits — so the lever's rank falls only for the bone/fracture
  outcome the drug actually covers. The drug's efficacy/limitations are **not held in the fabric** — a genuine G-gap (bisphosphonate/antiresorptive comparator); do not assert its
  effect as fact here, and the choice between lever and drug stays the person's (Layer 3). Hoffmann
  also blocks the opposite substitution — exercise is a complement, not an alternative, for the
  high-risk stratum: «From a socioeconomic point of view, on the other hand, it would be wrong to conclude that exercise might be a true alternative to pharmaceutical therapy. A large proportion of frail elderly persons, the most vulnerable group for fractures, demonstrate low affinity to exercise [46] and will be hard to persuade to start exercising frequently. Nevertheless, the combination of bone strengthening drugs and fall prevention exercise will definitely be the most promising fracture reduction strategy for this cohort.»
  [@hoffmann2022].
- **Do not personalize the exercise prescription on bone or menopausal status** — no modification
  is shown (between-study comparisons, subgroup sizes judged adequate by the authors with few
  exceptions, menopausal status at the total hip among them), so default to not personalizing; the stratifying variable that *does* work is baseline **fracture** risk (route
  (a), absolute benefit), which BMD/FRAX supplies as a prognostic input, not a treatment target.
- **Menopause context:** accelerated post-menopausal bone loss is one of the shifting levers
  ([[Menopause and the Shifting Levers]]); exercise is a modifiable counter-lever on the bone axis,
  weighted alongside the others there.
- **The loading->BMD mechanism may contribute to inverting stress-fracture risk across strata.** The
  mechanostat here (load raises BMD) is a plausible contributor to why a *lean* athlete can be
  bone-fragile despite exercising (the low-energy-availability / RED-S pathway is on that page) — the
  low-energy-availability runner on [[Stress Fracture Risk Factors in Runners]] has low BMD and high
  bone-stress risk — while a *high-mass* obese novice loads the skeleton heavily and plausibly has higher
  BMD, shifting their binding injury constraint to soft tissue/joint rather than bone. Same mechanism,
  opposite stratum conclusions.
