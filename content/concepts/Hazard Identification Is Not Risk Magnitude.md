**A carcinogen classification answers *whether* an exposure can cause cancer at some dose, never *how
much* it raises your risk at the dose you meet.** These are two different objects that one word — "carcinogen"
— hides, and the confusion between them drives most public cancer-scare reactions. `type-B` (one label,
two distinct quantities). IARC does **hazard identification**: is the hazard excludable, given the human,
animal, and mechanistic evidence? It does not do **risk assessment**: what absolute risk this exposure adds
to a given person at a realistic dose. A Group-1, 2A, or 2B label grades the *strength of evidence that a
hazard exists*, not the *size of the risk* — so the label alone licenses no avoidance decision.

## The two objects, kept apart



- **Hazard identification (what IARC does).** Can this exposure cause cancer at *any* dose? The group
  (1 / 2A / 2B) reports how firmly that yes-or-no is established — sufficient, limited, or inadequate
  evidence in humans, combined with animal and mechanistic data. It is a statement about *evidence of
  existence*, close in spirit to a GRADE certainty rating
  -> [[Certainty of Evidence vs Strength of Recommendation]].
- **Risk magnitude (what the label omits).** How much does this exposure raise *your* absolute risk, at
  *your* exposure, over *your* baseline? That is a relative effect applied to a baseline risk — the
  quantity a decision actually needs -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. A large relative
  effect on a tiny baseline is a tiny absolute risk; a firm hazard classification says nothing about where
  on that scale an exposure sits.

Two exposures can share a hazard group and differ in risk magnitude by orders of magnitude. This is why the
public alarm that a food "shares a category with tobacco" is a category error: the shared category is a
statement about *evidence that a hazard exists*, and it is silent on the risk each one actually carries. The
fabric routes the processed-meat decision itself through absolute risk, not through its hazard label
-> [[Red and Processed Meat and Cancer]] (the wiki does not hold IARC's processed-meat monograph, so the
Group-1 label is noted as the public framing, not asserted as a fabric finding).

## Two held instances show the split



- **Aspartame — Group 2B on limited evidence.** IARC classified aspartame "possibly carcinogenic" on
  *limited* human evidence — explicitly a hazard identification, not a dose or risk statement, and JECFA's
  companion intake evaluation left the acceptable daily intake unchanged. The label reads as alarming; the
  risk statement attached to a realistic intake did not move -> [[Non-Sugar Sweeteners]].
- **Night-shift work — Group 2A, later contested on risk.** IARC's probable-carcinogen classification
  rested on limited human plus sufficient animal evidence (a hazard judgement); a large prospective
  analysis then found the exposure has little or no effect on breast-cancer *incidence*. The clearest
  demonstration of the split: an exposure can be firmly classified as a probable hazard and still carry a
  risk magnitude near zero -> [[Night Shift Work and Breast Cancer]].

## Decision relevance



- **A hazard label is not an avoidance decision.** Do not decide to avoid an exposure because it carries a
  carcinogen classification. The classification tells you the hazard is real enough to take seriously as a
  question; it does not tell you the risk is large enough to act on. Ask for the absolute risk — relative
  effect on your baseline — before changing anything.
- **Match the response to the risk magnitude, not the category.** The same Group-1/2A/2B language spans
  exposures whose absolute risks differ enormously; a proportionate response tracks the risk number, which
  the label does not carry.
- **The certainty of the hazard and the size of the risk move independently.** A firmly established hazard
  (high evidential certainty) can add a negligible risk, and a suspected hazard on limited evidence can — if
  real — add a large one. Keep the two questions separate, the way GRADE keeps certainty apart from the
  strength of a recommendation -> [[Certainty of Evidence vs Strength of Recommendation]].
