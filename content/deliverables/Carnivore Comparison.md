**A type of person, not a specific one — and not everyone.** This compares two animal-based diets for one
common situation: the **middle-aged adult who is drifting the wrong way metabolically** — late 40s, BMI
around 30 with fat around the middle, blood pressure and blood sugar creeping up, triglycerides raised,
some low-grade inflammation and early fatty liver. This person is **not low-risk**, and that single fact
drives most of what follows.

**The two diets, both run at the same modest calorie deficit (~10% below maintenance):**

| | Clean Carnivore | Medium-Fat Animal-Based |
|---|---|---|
| Make-up | ~30% protein / ~70% fat / ~0 carbs | ~50% protein / ~35% fat / ~15% carbs; up to 3.5 g/kg protein |
| Foods | fatty ribeye (high in saturated fat) | leaner beef (round roast) + steamed carrots & broccoli; water |

**Why the equal deficit matters.** By holding calories equal in both arms, we take *weight loss off the
table as the variable* — this is not a contest over which diet makes you eat less. It isolates the effect
of the **food make-up** itself.

## Start here: the deficit hands both diets the biggest lever, roughly equally

For this person the single biggest win is **losing the fat around the organs**, and an equal ~10% deficit
delivers a lasting loss on *either* diet. A ~5% loss clears liver fat and 7-10% cools liver inflammation
([[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]); the same loss improves blood sugar, blood pressure, and function.
The honest bound: dieting did **not** cut heart attacks or strokes (the largest trial null; a 54-trial
review agrees), but it **does** lower overall death rates (~6 fewer per 1,000) by a non-heart route
([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]). So the deficit is worth it for the liver, blood sugar,
blood pressure, function, and that survival edge — just not as heart-attack insurance.

**Because the deficit controls weight, the question is no longer *which diet loses more*.** It is which
*make-up* is better for an already-at-risk person, and which they can *stick with* — and both
high-protein diets keep hunger down, which helps. The real dividing lines are the axes below.

## Axis 1 — Saturated fat and cholesterol: the difference that actually matters here

This is where the two diets genuinely part ways, and for *this* person it is no footnote.

- **Clean carnivore maximises saturated fat** (70% fat, fatty ribeye) *and* piles on a second push: eating
  near-zero carbs itself nudges LDL cholesterol up, on top of the saturated-fat effect
  ([[Low-Carbohydrate vs Balanced-Carbohydrate Diets]]). Cutting saturated fat lowers LDL with **high
  certainty**, and the more you cut, the more it falls ([[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]]) — so the
  carnivore arm pushes cholesterol the wrong way on two counts at once.
- **Medium-fat deliberately cuts saturated fat** (35% fat, lean roast), so LDL runs lower.
- **The deficit partly offsets the carnivore's rise** — losing weight lowers LDL — so the net is
  *deficit-down vs saturated-fat-up* in the carnivore arm, and *both-down* in the medium-fat arm
  `[INFERRED — each direction is evidenced separately; the net of a combined diet is our synthesis, not a
  measured value]`.

**How much does the cholesterol difference matter here? Four steps:**

- **On its own, small.** Cutting saturated fat lowers heart attacks/strokes by about **15 fewer per 1,000**
  (RR 0.83, moderate certainty) and shows **no clear effect on death** ([[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]]).
- **But two things scale it up for this person.** Benefit tracks your starting risk
  ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]], [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]), and this person is at **raised** risk — so the same percentage effect is a bigger
  real-world gain than for a lean 30-year-old (for whom it would genuinely be second-order,
  [[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]).
- **And the cause is now settled, not hedged.** LDL and its carrier particle **apoB** genuinely *cause*
  artery disease — shown where genetics, natural experiments, and trials all agree — and the harm is
  **cumulative** (how high × how long) ([[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]). So a sustained diet-driven
  rise is not a cosmetic number; it is added lifetime exposure.
- **For this person it is worse than the LDL number looks.** In this metabolic state, standard LDL
  *under-counts* the harmful particles (they come small and dense, packing more particles per unit of
  cholesterol). So track **apoB — or non-HDL cholesterol, a cheap stand-in** — not LDL alone. What is
  genuinely uncertain is *how much* a given person's apoB rises on a fatty-carnivore diet, not *whether*
  it matters.

**Verdict on this axis:** for an already-at-risk person, the carnivore arm's cholesterol maximisation is a
real cost — *sharpened*, now that the cause is settled — and the medium-fat arm sits closer to the
evidence. But hold the standard both ways: *the deficit's weight loss does not excuse a big cholesterol
rise here, and the cholesterol worry does not erase the shared weight-loss benefit.*

## Axis 2 — Protein: the medium-fat arm overshoots, but the deficit partly defends it

- At a stable weight, the medium-fat arm's **3.5 g/kg is overkill** — muscle gains from protein flatten
  out near **~1.6 g/kg a day** ([[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]), and above
  that you buy no more muscle (the gain is modest to begin with: about +2.5 kg on a one-rep max, +0.3 kg
  of lean mass).
- **Under a deficit the case improves:** ample protein preserves muscle while fat comes off, and the
  useful target rises somewhat — but the defensible figure is *high, roughly 1.6-2+ g/kg*, still short of
  3.5. Both diets are animal-based, so protein *quality* is a non-issue (both score high, [[Protein Quality and the DIAAS Score]]).
- One link worth noting: raising protein is *part of how* the medium-fat arm lowers its fat share, so Axes
  1 and 2 are connected. **The safe upper limit of ~3.5 g/kg is a gap we do not hold** — named, not
  invented.

## Axis 3 — Carbohydrate, fibre, vegetables

- **Cutting carbs helps the sugar-drifting side of this person** — both diets are low-carb, and
  restriction lifts short-term diabetes remission (about a 0.32 higher chance at 6 months, moderate
  certainty). But it **fades by 12 months** (to ~0.10) and **collapses toward zero once no diabetes
  medication is needed** ([[Carbohydrate Restriction and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]]). Low-carb versus
  balanced-carb differs by only ~1 kg — not worth arguing over ([[Low-Carbohydrate vs Balanced-Carbohydrate Diets]]).
- **Fibre and vegetables:** the carnivore arm has essentially none; the medium-fat arm's steamed
  carrots/broccoli add fibre (a modest, population-level benefit, [[Whole Grains Refined Grains and Pulses]]), potassium (Axis 5), and a wider range of micronutrients. Steaming is gentle, forming fewer of
  the browning compounds high heat creates — though that lever is small (low-browning diets move a few lab
  markers but do nothing to weight, blood sugar, or blood pressure, [[Dietary AGEs and Cooking Method]]).
  The worry that broccoli harms the thyroid is defused by cooking and normal intake ([[Antinutrients in Plant Foods]]).
- **Carnivore gives up the fibre and micronutrient channel entirely;** whether a muscle-meat-only diet
  covers micronutrient needs is a gap we do not hold.

## Axis 4 — Red meat: shared, so it decides nothing

Both diets are beef-heavy, so the contested red-meat question applies **equally** and cannot separate
them: one expert body says limit red/processed meat, another says current intake is defensible on the same
evidence — the fight is over the standard of proof and whose values set the line, not whether meat causes
cancer ([[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]]). One small difference: high-heat searing (ribeye)
creates more of the compounds linked to cancer than gentler roasting ([[Red and Processed Meat and Cancer]]) — a minor point for the medium-fat arm's cooking, not a headline.

## Axis 5 — Salt and potassium: live, because this person's blood pressure is up

Cutting salt lowers blood pressure with **high certainty** (~3.4 mmHg at rest, up to ~5.5 mmHg on 24-hour
monitoring, [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]), and the effect is larger in those already hypertensive.
Both diets are meat-heavy, so **salted or processed cuts are the risk to watch**; the medium-fat arm's
vegetables also add **potassium**, which the plant-free carnivore diet forgoes. A small axis, but it
points the same way as Axis 1 — toward the medium-fat diet — for this person.

## Where this lands (net, weighted for this person — not a dogmatic verdict)

- **The shared weight loss is the main event**, and the equal deficit delivers it either way — so
  whichever diet the person will *sustain* pulls the biggest lever available.
- **Among the differences, the saturated-fat/cholesterol axis is the one that genuinely matters here**,
  because this person is already at raised risk; on it the medium-fat diet sits closer to the evidence,
  and the clean carnivore maximises the one exposure with an evidenced (if small) harm.
- **The medium-fat arm's 3.5 g/kg protein is an overshoot** — defensible as *high* for protecting muscle
  on a diet, not at 3.5.
- **Neither diet has hard-outcome evidence**, and no achievable difference in make-up is large next to the
  shared weight-loss benefit. Read the whole thing at the margin.

## Open questions (raised on purpose, deliberately NOT settled)

These are plausible mechanisms stated so their absence does not mislead; we hold no evidence to settle
them and take no position (routed to `ingest-queue.md`):

- **No fibre and cholesterol clearance.** Does a zero-fibre diet hinder the gut's disposal of cholesterol,
  so blood cholesterol runs higher than saturated fat alone would predict — while the medium-fat arm's
  fibre adds a cholesterol-lowering route? We hold only the size of the fibre effect (a daily pulse
  portion lowers LDL ~0.2 mmol/L); the mechanism is unheld.
- **No fibre and clearing toxins during fat loss.** As stored fat is burned, fat-soluble compounds held in
  it are released into the blood — does fibre help carry them out, so a zero-fibre diet lets them be
  reabsorbed? Entirely unheld.
- **High dietary fat during fat loss.** While the body is releasing its own fat, does a high-fat diet add
  to circulating and liver fat compared with a lower-fat diet — even though net weight loss improves the
  liver overall? Unheld.

## What we cannot yet say (routed for later)

- **No hard-outcome trial of *either* diet** — both are assembled from piece-by-piece evidence. The open
  loop, front and centre.
- **The safe upper limit for protein above ~2 g/kg** is unheld — we hold the 1.6 g/kg muscle plateau, not
  a ceiling at 3.5.
- **The minority whose cholesterol shoots up** on very-low-carb, high-saturated-fat eating — unheld.
- **Whether muscle-meat-only meets micronutrient needs** — unheld.
- **Which diet a person can actually sustain** — the real tie-breaker, and one we cannot measure.

## The honest limits

- **This grades the evidence, not the outcome** — whether the reasoning is sound and faithful to its
  sources, never whether either diet actually made someone healthier.
- **It appraises, it does not prescribe:** a big diet-driven cholesterol rise may call for monitoring or a
  doctor's input, which this cannot give.
- **A type, not a person:** how much the cholesterol axis weighs depends on your actual heart risk and
  what you care about, worked out case by case.
- **Same standard, both diets and both directions:** neither the carnivore's "ancestral" framing nor the
  medium-fat diet's cut-the-saturated-fat rationale gets a discount; where the evidence is small or
  missing, this says so.
