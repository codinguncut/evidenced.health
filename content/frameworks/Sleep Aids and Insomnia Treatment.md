Opens the treatment question for the `sleep` cluster: once someone *has* chronic insomnia, what should
they do? Three interventions, three evidence bases, no head-to-head trial. The beyond-summary move is
that the effect numbers cannot order them (different quantities, different controls), and the held
sources support only **part** of an order. **CBT-I first** rests on held warrants: a relayed guideline
recommendation as initial treatment and no held harm signal (its harms are unreported, not shown
absent). Its large benefit on insomnia severity against non-active controls shows that it works; that
number cannot rank it against the drugs. Between **melatonin and a prescription hypnotic** the held
sources state a **trade-off without weighing it**. Hypnotics are likely more effective, an indirect
inference across meta-analyses that the melatonin authors say head-to-head trials would settle. The
authors describe melatonin's side-effect profile as more benign, but that is their statement; the
meta-analysis pools no adverse-event data. Hypnotics also carry a confounded mortality/cancer signal. Which side to favour depends on how a person weights a likely
larger benefit against a harm of uncertain causality, a layer-3 judgement. Durability and CBT-I's own
harms are *not* measured in the held fabric (below), so they carry no part of the order.

## Why the three cannot be ranked by effect size — the commensurability table

Each source reports a **different quantity** on a **different design**, so a numeric league table would
compare a standardized-mean-difference against minutes against a hazard ratio, and the two benefit
estimates use different controls (non-active controls for CBT-I, placebo for melatonin). What the
sources *say* about relative benefit and harm is the usable evidence, not the effect numbers.


| Parameter | CBT-I (van Straten) | Hypnotics (Kripke) | Melatonin (Ferracioli-Oda) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Design | 87 RCTs vs non-treated controls, MA (gold) | 1 matched cohort (moderate) | 19 RCTs vs placebo, MA (gold) | **NO** — RCT-MA vs observational vs RCT-MA |
| Primary effect | ISI g=0.98; SOL g=0.57; TST g=0.16 | mortality HR 3.6-5.3 (dose); cancer HR 1.35 | SOL -7.06 min; TST +8.25 min; quality SMD 0.22 | **NO** — g vs HR(harm) vs minutes |
| What is measured | insomnia severity, sleep continuity (benefit) | death + cancer (harm) | latency/duration/quality (benefit, small) | **NO** — different endpoints entirely |
| Durability / tolerance | no long-term follow-up in the MA; effect similar whether or not trials allowed sleep medication | short-run efficacy, limited long-term evidence (van Straten's introduction, citing earlier MAs — not Kripke) | no evidence of tolerance (larger SOL and TST effects in longer trials; none on quality) | comparable direction, not magnitude |
| Harm signal | not reported (no adverse-event data in the MA) | strong (confounded) | described as benign by the authors; no adverse-event data pooled in the MA | — |



Because every *same quantity?* cell is **NO**, no cross-source magnitude claim is made. The within-source
comparisons stand. van Straten's *smallest* effect is on total sleep time (g=0.16) — CBT-I consolidates
sleep, it does not add hours. [@vanstraten2018]
Ferracioli's own benchmark is an indirect, cross-meta-analysis one: «For instance, the reduction in
sleep latency observed with melatonin in this meta-analysis, slightly less than 7 minutes, was less than
sleep-latency reduction observed in previous meta-analyses of other available sleep medications. A
previous meta-analysis demonstrated a significant benefit of benzodiazepines (10.0 to 19.6 minutes) and
non- benzodiazepine sleep medicines (12.8 to 17 minutes) in reducing sleep-onset latency for primary
sleep disorders [21].» [@ferraciolioda2013]
Their conclusion from that benchmark: «Thus prescription sleep medications are quite likely more
effective than melatonin, although head-to-head trials could definitely alleviate any doubts regarding
the relative efficacy of these agents.» [@ferraciolioda2013]
The *under 7 minutes* is the fixed-effect estimate; the random-effects estimate is 10.18 min (95% CI
6.1 to 14.27). [@ferraciolioda2013] The
source does not set that figure against the earlier range, whose pooling model is not stated, so the
held direction stands: hypnotics are likely more effective on onset latency.

## CBT-I — the guideline first-line `[EXTRACTED]`

van Straten pooled 87 RCTs comparing 118 treatments (3724 treated) with **non-treated controls**,
counting any trial of «at least one component of CBTI», not only the full package.
[@vanstraten2018] The controls were not drugs:
the inclusion criteria required comparison «with a non-active control group (e.g., waitlist control,
care-as-usual, or a minimal intervention such as psycho- education about sleep or sleep hygiene
information)». [@vanstraten2018] The effects were
large on insomnia severity (ISI g=0.98), moderate-to-large on sleep efficiency (g=0.71) and
wake-after-onset (g=0.63), moderate on onset latency (g=0.57), and smallest on total sleep time
(g=0.16). [@vanstraten2018] Decision-relevant
properties:

- **It works whether or not trials allowed sleep medication.** Effects were «quite robust (similar for
  patients with or without comorbid disease, younger or older patients, using or not using sleep
  medication)». [@vanstraten2018] The moderator
  is study-level: «The treatment effects were not signiﬁcantly related to the presence of comorbidity,
  the allowance to use sleep medication, or to age.»
  [@vanstraten2018] It does **not** show CBT-I
  gets people off pills: the MA excluded trials aimed «at tapering of medication».
  [@vanstraten2018] Whether CBT-I is an exit
  route from chronic hypnotic use is a gap in the held fabric.
- **It is guideline first-line.** van Straten's introduction, summarising earlier reviews rather than
  this MA's own result, states «CBTI is at least as effective as pharmacotherapy», and «the American
  College of Physicians recently recommended CBTI as the initial treatment for all adults with
  insomnia». [@vanstraten2018]
- **Delivery and honesty.** «Face- to-face treatments of at least four sessions seem to be more
  effective than self-help interventions or face- to-face interventions with fewer sessions.»
  [@vanstraten2018] Publication bias is present:
  «There were indeed indications of publication bias. This suggests that the actual effects of CBTI on
  insomnia might be smaller although still signiﬁcant.» And: «Finally, many quality criteria were not
  met by the studies which might mean that we overestimate the true effectiveness.»
  [@vanstraten2018]
- **Durability and harms are not measured here.** The MA states it «did not include long-term
  follow-ups» [@vanstraten2018], and it reports
  no adverse-event data (searched: adverse / side effect / safety across chunks 01-03; the only hits
  concern drugs). Adherence and therapist access are likely practical constraints, but no held source
  measures them. -> [[Sleep Duration and Mortality]].

## Hypnotics — short-run sleep benefit, and a confounded harm signal `[EXTRACTED]`

The held fabric's evidence that hypnotics improve sleep is second-hand: van Straten's introduction
reports that «Several meta-analyses have shown that benzodiazepine- receptor agonists are effective in
enhancing sleep in the short run, but with risks of negative side effects and limited evidence for
their long-term efﬁcacy [18e20].» [@vanstraten2018]
Ferracioli's benchmark (above) puts the benzodiazepine onset-latency gain at 10.0 to 19.6 min.
[@ferraciolioda2013]

Kripke's matched cohort found a strong dose-response **mortality** association: «For groups prescribed
0.4e18, 18e132 and >132 doses/ year, HRs (95% CIs) were 3.60 (2.92 to 4.44), 4.43 (3.67 to 5.36) and
5.32 (4.50 to 6.30), respectively, demonstrating a doseeresponse association.»
[@kripke2012] So **HR 3.60 even at <18 doses/year**,
plus a cancer signal (upper-third HR 1.35). [@kripke2012]
In absolute terms, 6.1% of hypnotic users and 1.2% of controls died over a mean of about 2.5 years of
observation, in a population served by Geisinger, a rural Pennsylvania health system, mean age about 54.
[@kripke2012] Exposure is prescription *orders*, not
dispensing. [@kripke2012] The association was «robust
within groups suffering each comorbidity, indicating that the death and cancer hazards ... were not
attributable to pre-existing disease». [@kripke2012]

**The caveat is decisive and must be stated at point of use (symmetric standards).** This is a single
**observational** cohort; the live confounder is **confounding by indication** — the same distress,
psychiatric load and occult illness that lead to a hypnotic prescription independently raise mortality,
and «residual confounding could not be fully excluded».
[@kripke2012] The psychiatric arm of that confounder
was not adjusted for: «We were unable to control for depression, anxiety and other emotional factors
because of Pennsylvania laws protecting the conﬁdentiality of these diagnoses.» Kripke's counter cites
several studies but describes one, with its strongest result in men: «However, several previous
studies reporting hypnotic risks have controlled for these confounders.7 35 Mallon et al found that
when depression, hypnotic use and other risk factors were entered into a multivariate model for
all-cause mortality, hypnotic use was the strongest risk factor among men (stronger than cigarette
smoking).» [@kripke2012] It then concedes the
residual: «In that analysis, depression was not an independent risk factor for death in either men or
women.7 Moreover, one might expect an emotional confounder to cause insomnia, leading, in turn, to use
of hypnotics, but several large studies have reported that insomnia is not a signiﬁcant mortality risk
factor, especially when hypnotic usage is controlled.7 35e37 Nevertheless, to the extent that social
and psychological problems lead patients to receive hypnotics, and to the extent that these problems
cause death through pathways indepen- dent of hypnotics, our ﬁndings might reﬂect some confounding by
those conditions.» [@kripke2012] The models stratified on up to 116
comorbidity combinations. [@kripke2012] Recorded
comorbidity strata cannot reach the unrecorded psychiatric load, so the confounding is reduced at best,
not removed. No chronic-hypnotic mortality **RCT**
exists: «Some American NIH reviewers have opined that a rand- omised trial of hypnotic lethality would
be unethical.» [@kripke2012] The lead author also runs
an anti-hypnotic advocacy site — reason to hold the *magnitude* loosely.
[@kripke2012] So the hazard is a **decision-relevant
harm signal, not a demonstrated causal death toll**: its direction counts against hypnotics as a
first reach, while its size is too confounded to price against their benefit. Kripke's
own conclusion goes further, doubting even the short-term use that guidance gives qualified approval: «A consensus is developing that cognitive-behavioural therapy of
chronic insomnia may be more successful than hypnotics.40 41 Against meagre beneﬁts, it is prudent to
weigh the evidence of mortality risks from the current study and 24 previous reports, in order to
reconsider whether even short-term use of hypnotics, as given qualiﬁed approval in National Insti- tute
for Clinical Excellence guidance,39 is sufﬁciently safe.»
[@kripke2012]

## Melatonin — modest, described as benign, no evidence of tolerance `[EXTRACTED]`

Ferracioli-Oda's 19-RCT MA covers **primary sleep disorders** in adults and children: «Nineteen studies
were included in the analysis, fourteen studies on the efficacy of melatonin for the treatment of
insomnia, four studies on delayed sleep phase syndrome and one study on REM sleep behavior disorder.»
[@ferraciolioda2013] Melatonin cut
sleep-onset latency by **7.06 min** (95% CI 4.37-9.75; fixed effect).
[@ferraciolioda2013] It raised total sleep
time by **8.25 min** (95% CI 1.74-14.75) pooled across measures, fixed effect; the random-effects
estimate is not significant (8.48 min, p = 0.184), and by measure type the gain is 11.93 min on
subjective measures and 0.33 min (NS) on objective ones.
[@ferraciolioda2013] It improved quality
(SMD 0.22, 95% CI 0.12-0.32). [@ferraciolioda2013]

Two features weigh on the melatonin side against its small effect. First, **no evidence of
tolerance**: «Higher
melatonin doses and longer duration trials were related to significant greater effect sizes on sleep
latency and total sleep time. These findings suggest that there is no evidence of the development of
tolerance with melatonin use. This stands in contrast to other commonly used hypnotics such as
benzodiazepines [13].» [@ferraciolioda2013]
The Results section words the dose-latency link more weakly than that Discussion sentence: «Trials using
higher doses of melatonin also reported greater effects of melatonin on sleep latency at trend levels
(PE = 1.95 [95% CI = 20.00 to 3.91], p = 0.05).» (the CI's lower bound prints as *20.00*, a lost
minus sign in the PDF extraction).
The duration effects on latency and total sleep time and the dose effect on total sleep time are
significant in the Results (p = 0.001, 0.004, 0.007); neither dose nor duration changed sleep quality;
and «our meta-regression analysis had limited power».
[@ferraciolioda2013] Second, a
side-effect profile the authors describe as **benign**: «Although the absolute benefit of melatonin compared to placebo is smaller than
other pharmacological treatments for insomnia, melatonin may have a role in the treatment of insomnia
given its relatively benign side-effect profile compared to these agents.»
[@ferraciolioda2013] The introduction
sets this against prescription drugs: «In contrast to most available sleep medications, melatonin has
little dependence potential, is not associated with habituation and typically produces no hangover.»
[@ferraciolioda2013] Both are author
statements, and the introduction's rests on earlier literature: «Given its reported hypnotic effects,
relatively benign side-effect profile and over-the-counter availability, melatonin has been widely
utilized in the United States [16].»
[@ferraciolioda2013] The meta-analysis
pools no adverse-event data (searched: *adverse*, *tolerab* across chunk 01, the only chunk, zero hits).
Melatonin's harm advantage is therefore relayed, at the same evidentiary level as the hypnotic
side-effect mentions below. The held case for melatonin is as a smaller-benefit alternative to a
prescription hypnotic, with a side-effect advantage that is stated, not measured. No held trial combines
it with CBT-I, so its use as an *adjunct* is untested.
-> [[Sleep Regularity and Mortality]]

## Decision relevance — the substitution frame

- **CBT-I first, on held warrants rather than a head-to-head trial.** The order rests on the held
  text's relay of a guideline recommendation of CBT-I as initial treatment and of earlier reviews
  finding it at least as effective as pharmacotherapy (both second-hand, van Straten's introduction),
  plus the absence of a held harm signal. No held source reports a CBT-I harm, but harms were not
  measured, and durability is unmeasured too. Its large benefit on insomnia severity against non-active
  controls shows it works; it does not rank it against the drugs.
- **Melatonin or a prescription hypnotic: a trade-off the held sources state but do not weigh.** On
  benefit, the melatonin MA itself says prescription sleep medications are quite likely more effective
  than melatonin. That is an indirect comparison across meta-analyses, and the authors add «although
  head-to-head trials could definitely alleviate any doubts». On harm, the held side has two legs:
  Kripke's confounded mortality/cancer cohort (quantified), and second-hand, unquantified mentions of
  side effects «from residual daytime sleepiness to dependence». Those mentions cover a broad class
  (benzodiazepine receptor agonists, benzodiazepines, sedating antidepressants, anxiolytics,
  antipsychotics, antihistamines), not prescription hypnotics alone. They are set against melatonin's
  «little dependence potential», which is likewise an author statement, not a pooled result.
  [@ferraciolioda2013] The harm signal
  points away from hypnotics; its size is not known well enough to settle the trade. Whether a likely
  larger benefit is worth a harm of uncertain causality is the person's weighting (layer 3), not a
  finding of this page.
- ***Do sleep aids give real sleep?*** Not answerable from held fabric. No held source measures sleep
  architecture or separates sedation from sleep for hypnotics, CBT-I or melatonin (searched: *sleep
  architecture* across van Straten chunks 01-03, zero hits; Kripke measures mortality and cancer only).
  What is held: CBT-I improves self-rated severity and diary continuity, mostly not total sleep time.
  [@vanstraten2018]
  [@vanstraten2018] Melatonin nudges latency;
  benzodiazepines shorten onset latency by 10.0-19.6 min in an earlier MA.
  [@ferraciolioda2013] The three are not
  interchangeable routes to one outcome.
- **Stratum.** Chronic insomnia is the target. For chronic hypnotic users, CBT-I's effect did not differ
  in trials that allowed sleep medication, and the mortality signal points away from continued use, so
  CBT-I is a reasonable next step; whether it helps them stop hypnotics is not tested in held fabric.

## Limits

- **No head-to-head trials among the three.** CBT-I first rests on a relayed guideline recommendation
  and the absence of a held harm signal; the melatonin-vs-hypnotic choice is a benefit-vs-harm weighing
  the sources leave open. Durability is unmeasured for CBT-I and supports no comparison.
- **The harm anchor is one confounded cohort.** The hypnotic-mortality magnitude is the weakest-design
  input; it is used as a labelled counterweight (moderate tier, gold-gate override), not a precision
  figure -> [[Sleep Duration and Mortality]].
- Benefit endpoints are mostly **self-report** (ISI, diaries, subjective quality), with some objective
  PSG/actigraphy subgroups in the melatonin MA. Insomnia severity is itself a patient-important
  outcome for this decision; what is missing is any hard-outcome trial showing that treating insomnia
  lowers mortality. Coherence, not validity (R1).
- **AWAITS** the ACP/AASM insomnia guideline as a second guidance family, and any hypnotic-vs-placebo
  long-term safety RCT (none exists).
