**The verdict up front: "too fast is bad" is mostly the wrong frame, because the folk question conflates
three different things - the RATE of loss (kg/week), the METHOD (moderate deficit vs a very-low-calorie
liquid diet vs a drug vs surgery), and the SOURCE of the energy deficit (eating below your needs vs adding
large exercise volume).** Once you hold total weight lost and method fixed, the rate itself is a weak
independent predictor of the outcomes people worry about. The real costs that *do* track going fast are
**method-mediated and stratum-dependent**: a larger energy gap costs more muscle unless protein and
resistance training defend it, and certain organ risks (gallstones, a transient worsening of a fatty
liver, the historical cardiac deaths of unsupervised crash diets) attach to *very-low-calorie or extreme*
methods, not to speed as such. The honest headline is therefore **"pick the fastest rate you can sustain
with adequate protein and resistance training, and above a threshold under supervision - the ceiling is
set by muscle preservation, organ safety and adherence, not by a magic slow rate."** Confidence is **low**
throughout: several load-bearing magnitudes - the gradual-vs-rapid regain trial, the lean-mass-loss-by-rate
number, the very-low-calorie-diet safety evidence, and the exercise-vs-diet body-composition review - are
**not held here and are named as gaps, not guessed at.**

This is a stratum-level appraisal applied per person at layer 3
([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]] for where rate ranks - it is a second-order knob).

## Separate the three things "rapid" conflates - RATE, METHOD, DEFICIT-SOURCE

The single most important move is to stop treating "rapid" as one exposure. A 1 kg/week loss from a
moderate, protein-adequate deficit plus resistance training is a *different exposure* than the same
1 kg/week from a very-low-calorie liquid formula, from a GLP-1 drug, or from surgery - even though the
scale moves at the same speed. The label "rapid" is not the exposure; judge each claimed harm against
*which* of the three it actually tracks ([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]] - a broad label whose
within-category variance swamps the boundary describes no actual intervention).

- **RATE** = kg/week or % body weight/week. This is what the folk question fixates on, and it turns out
  to carry the least independent signal.
- **METHOD** = moderate deficit vs very-low-calorie diet (VLCD - a nutritionally-complete formula, roughly
  <=800 kcal/day) vs drug vs surgery. Most claimed "harms of speed" are really harms of a method that
  *happens* to produce speed.
- **DEFICIT-SOURCE** = eating below your energy needs vs adding large exercise volume to widen the gap.
  This changes body *composition* even when total weight lost is identical (see the diet-vs-exercise
  section).

Keeping these three apart is what the rest of this deliverable does; conflating them is the error it
exists to prevent.

## Is there a U/J-curve on the SPEED of loss? Test it, do not assume it

The popular picture is a U/J-curve on speed: too slow is ineffective and demoralizing, too fast rebounds
and harms. Run it through [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]], whose rule is that the fragile arm of any
U/J-shape must survive a confounder-immune check before it is believed.

- **The lower arm (too slow) is real but mundane.** A deficit too small to matter, or so slow that
  motivation fails, is an *effectiveness and adherence* problem, not a *safety* one. Nothing exotic here.
- **The upper arm (too fast rebounds) is the contested one, and it is heavily confounded.** Much of "rapid
  losers regain" is confounded **by method** (rapid loss is often achieved by crash diets and
  unsustainable behaviours) and **by who does it** (confounding-by-indication - people who choose or
  achieve very fast loss differ systematically), plus simple regression to the mean. That is exactly the
  profile [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]] flags as an artifact-suspect arm: a shape equally
  consistent with the causal and the confounded explanation has no diagnostic value on its own.

**What would adjudicate it is a randomized rate-of-loss trial** - assign people to gradual vs rapid loss to
the *same* target and compare regain - and that evidence is **not held here** (see gaps; the TEMPO /
Purcell-2014-type trial). Where such trials exist they tend to run *against* the folk wisdom (rapid initial
losers reach target more often, with no worse regain), but the wiki does not hold the magnitude, so this is
flagged as the likely-but-unconfirmed direction, not asserted. **Evidence state: the speed upper-arm is
contested/confounded, not established.**

## Does regain track speed? Weak at best - and everyone regains

Once method and total loss are fixed, speed is a weak predictor of how much comes back; the dominant
determinant of maintenance is the *maintenance behaviours and environment*, not how fast the loss
happened. Two held facts anchor the "regain is near-universal" half of this:

- **Even a supervised, well-designed programme regains.** In DiRECT, engagers lost sharply during the
  formula phase and then partly regained through food reintroduction and maintenance - a net trajectory of
  roughly -11.6 kg for completers (derived from the completer trajectory) against a -10.0 kg intention-to-treat mean, with regain beginning
  immediately after the trough [@lean2018].
- **The body defends the deficit regardless of how it was opened** - the mechanism behind regain and behind
  exercise "not counting" ([[Exercise Energy Compensation]], quantified below).

NICE makes weight regain itself the reason it will only *weakly* recommend the rapid formula route: it
lowered the recommendation's strength because rapid regain and weight cycling (repeated loss-and-regain)
could be harmful [@nice]. Note this is strength lowered by a harm the
evidence base *did not measure* - a caution, not a demonstrated rate effect.

**The magnitude behind this section - a gradual-vs-rapid regain trial or long-term (>=1 yr) maintenance
review - is a named gap** (Purcell/TEMPO; Franz-2007-type). Direction inferred; size not held.

## Muscle / lean-mass loss - this is the real cost of going fast, and it scales with the DEFICIT

**Evidence state: harm - real, and the load-bearing cost.** Lean (fat-free) mass loss scales with the
*size of the energy gap*, not with the calendar speed per se: a bigger deficit loses more muscle per kg
lost unless protein and resistance training defend it. The wiki holds the *defence* magnitude with
RCT-grade evidence but **not** a clean lean-mass-loss-*by-rate* number (named gap).

- **The defence: adequate protein plus resistance training.** From 49 RCTs, adding protein to resistance
  training lifted 1RM strength by «2.49 kg (0.64, 4.33)» and fat-free mass by «0.30 kg (0.09, 0.52)», with
  the benefit plateauing near 1.6 g/kg/day: «Protein supplementation beyond total protein intakes of 1.62
  g/kg/day resulted in no further RET-induced gains in FFM», break point «1.62 (1.03, 2.20)»
  [@morton2018]. Read the magnitudes before
  the significance: the training is the driver, protein a modest adjunct, and the useful move is *reaching*
  \~1.6 g/kg, not exceeding it -> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]].
- **A worked failure when the deficit is careless.** In the TREAT trial, a self-selected 16:8 eating window
  with no protein guidance produced no weight advantage over normal eating («-0.26 kg», «P = .63») - but
  the modest weight it did shed was «approximately 65% ... lean mass» versus the normal 20-30%, which the
  authors flagged as a caution for populations at risk of sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss)
  [@lowe2020]. This is the harm concretely: an uncontrolled
  deficit that under-feeds protein burns muscle.

**The sign of this cost flips by stratum.** For a young overweight adult, some lean-mass loss inside a
large fat loss is minor. For an older, sarcopenia-risk adult, **muscle and function are front-line
outcomes in their own right, and aggressive/rapid loss flips from lever to hazard** - there the top lever
becomes resistance training plus protein, and any weight loss must be muscle-protective and gradual, never
rapid -> [[Big Rocks (Elderly)]]. This is route (c) (a harm firing in a stratum) and route (d) (the outcome
priorities themselves change) acting together.

## Liver, gallbladder, kidney - the organ risks are real but specific, and mostly method/severity-tied

**Evidence state: mixed - one held dose-response, the rest mechanism or named gaps.** These risks track
the *severity/method* of the loss far more than a modest sustained rate.

- **Liver (held).** For fatty liver (MASLD), the lever is framed as **sustained, gradual** weight loss with
  a dose-graded histological payoff: «>=5%» reduces liver fat, «7-10%» improves inflammation, «>=10%»
  improves fibrosis [@easl2024]. The guideline documents
  that weight is typically regained toward «a net weight loss of about 5% at 12-24 months and partial
  regain of liver lipid content and stiffness» [@easl2024]. The specific rapid-phase worry - that very fast lipolysis floods the liver with free fatty acids
  and can *transiently* worsen steatosis before sustained loss improves it - is **mechanism, not a held
  finding here** ``. Net direction is benefit; the rapid-phase worsening is the caveat.
  Same guideline: «very little evidence for a beneficial effect of time-restricted eating over regular
  caloric restriction» on liver fat [@easl2024] - i.e. the
  schedule does not beat the isocaloric deficit.
- **Gallstones (gap).** Rapid weight loss, especially via a very-low-calorie diet, raises gallstone risk
  via bile stasis (bile sitting still in a gallbladder that is emptying less) - a genuinely *rate/method*-
  linked harm, but the sizing source is **not held** ``. A DiRECT adverse-event note is consistent (a serious
  event of biliary colic plus abdominal pain in one intervention participant) but is a single case, not an
  incidence [@lean2018].
- **Kidney / metabolic (mechanism).** Very restrictive loss can raise uric acid (gout flares), and severe
  or prolonged restriction carries refeeding-electrolyte risk on reintroduction - both flagged on
  [[Fasting Benefits and Risks]] as mechanism/clinical, not held magnitudes.
- **The historical hard stop - the true "too extreme is lethal" evidence (gap).** The early-1980s
  very-low-calorie *liquid-protein* diets caused cardiac (arrhythmic) deaths - the reason modern VLCDs are
  protein-adequate and medically supervised. This is the genuine upper bound on "too fast/too extreme," and
  a dedicated **VLCD-safety source is not held** (named gap); it is stated as the historical reason for
  supervision, not quantified here.

## Supervised rapid protocols show fast loss is not intrinsically unsafe - when done right

The strongest held counter to "fast is bad" is that structured, protein-adequate, supervised rapid loss
works and is recommended.

- **DiRECT** put people on an **825-853 kcal/day** formula diet (59% carbohydrate - a *high-carb,
  low-energy* diet), a genuinely rapid loss, with «All oral antidiabetic and antihypertensive drugs were
  discontinued on day 1». It achieved 46% type-2-diabetes remission with a mean -10.0 kg loss, and
  remission rose **monotonically with kilograms lost** - from 0% in those who gained weight to 7% (0-5 kg),
  34% (5-10 kg), 57% (10-15 kg), and 86% (>=15 kg) [@lean2018]. Note
  what the *method* required: an aggressive deficit and the medication list had to be managed *together*.
  -> [[Total Diet Replacement and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]].
- **NICE** positively (if weakly) recommends low-energy total diet replacement at 800-1200 kcal/day, but
  caps it: «maximum of 12 weeks», and it «should not be used as a long-term strategy to manage obesity»,
  offered only inside a specialist multicomponent service [@nice].
  -> [[Diets for Weight Loss - What NICE Recommends]].

**Read the NICE cap correctly: it is a *duration* bound on the rapid phase, not a prohibition of speed.**
The evidence-based position is "fast is acceptable within a bounded, protein-adequate, supervised
protocol," not "fast is bad." The same evidence that licenses the rapid formula phase also insists the
value is realized only if the maintenance scaffold holds afterwards - which returns to the regain point
above.

## Deficit source - eating below your needs vs adding large exercise

For a *given* total deficit, the SOURCE of the deficit shifts **body composition, not just the scale**. The
practical held finding is that **exercise cannot realistically create or defend the deficit a diet can**,
because the body compensates.

- **Exercise energy is substantially offset, and the offset grows over time.** Across 61 intervention
  studies, «The overall mean energy compensation was 18% ± 93%» - and it worsens with duration: «For
  studies of longer duration (about 80 weeks), the energy compensation approached 84%»
  [@riou2015]. A cross-sectional landmark using
  doubly-labelled-water finds «energy compensation by a typical human averages 28% due to reduced BEE
  [basal energy expenditure]; this suggests that only 72% of the extra calories we spend on additional
  activity translates into extra calories burned that day» [@careau2021]. So a large exercise-driven deficit partly refills itself.
- **Compensation is largest in exactly the person who most wants to burn fat off.** «People that are at the
  10th percentile of the BMI distribution compensate 27.7% of activity calories, whereas people at the
  90th percentile compensate 49.2% of activity calories» [@careau2021]. The higher-adiposity person should anchor the deficit on intake and use exercise for its other
  benefits.
- **But intensity is not the problem.** «Sex, frequency, intensity and dose of exercise energy expenditure
  were not significant predictors of energy compensation» [@riou2015] - so HIIT does not compensate *more* than moderate work; do not down-rank vigorous exercise for a
  compensation reason -> [[Exercise Energy Compensation]].

**The practical answer: diet creates the deficit; resistance training defends the muscle - not "out-
exercise a bad diet."** Adding exercise (especially resistance) to a diet-created deficit is muscle-sparing
and preferentially trims visceral fat, at the cost of energy compensation, injury risk and adherence; but a
purely exercise-driven deficit hits the compensation ceiling and cannot match what diet can create.
``

**The magnitude that would settle the composition question - an exercise-vs-hypocaloric-diet (and combined)
body-composition / visceral-fat systematic review (Verheggen-2016 / Khalafi-2023-type) - is not held**
(named gap). What is held is the compensation ceiling that makes "out-exercise it" implausible, not the
composition split itself.

## The ranking verdict - rate is a second-order knob, and weight loss itself is bounded on hard outcomes

Rank the whole question by decision-value. The **first-order** levers are *that* a sustained deficit exists
and *that* muscle is protected; the exact **rate** is a refinement
([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]). And keep expectations calibrated to what weight loss
actually moves:

- **On hard cardiovascular events, lifestyle weight loss did not deliver.** In the large Look AHEAD trial,
  intensive lifestyle weight loss did not cut cardiovascular events («hazard ratio 0.95; 95% CI 0.83 to
  1.09») [@look2013]. A 54-trial meta-analysis
  confirms the CV-event null («risk ratio 0.93, 95% confidence interval 0.83 to 1.04») while finding a
  modest all-cause mortality benefit not routed through the heart («risk ratio 0.82, 95% confidence
  interval 0.71 to 0.95», about six fewer deaths per 1000) [@ma2017] -> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].
- **So weight loss is a real-but-bounded lever**, largest for the outcomes it demonstrably moves (diabetes
  remission, liver histology, glycaemia, function) and unproven for others (heart attacks via the lifestyle
  route). The *rate* at which you get there does not change this bounded ceiling.

**Bottom line.** Choose the fastest rate you can *sustain* with adequate protein (\~1.6 g/kg/day) and
resistance training, and - above a threshold of restriction, or with the risk factors below - under
supervision. The ceiling is set by muscle preservation, gallstone/organ safety, and adherence, not by a
slow rate being intrinsically safer. Rapid is a property of a *method* to appraise, not a danger in itself;
the danger lives in an unsupervised, protein-poor, very-low-calorie method, and in the wrong stratum.

## The gaps - this deliverable's main product

The thin parts here are load-bearing, so they are named for acquisition (gold-tier first) rather than
papered over with invented numbers:

- **A gradual-vs-rapid weight-loss RCT/SR on regain and target achievement** (the TEMPO / Purcell-2014
  Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology type) - the magnitude behind the speed-U-curve, the regain question, and
  the "supervised rapid is fine" case. **Not held.**
- **A lean-mass-loss-by-rate-of-loss source** - how much fat-free mass is lost per kg at different rates,
  with and without protein plus resistance training. **Not held.**
- **A VLCD / rapid-loss safety source** - gallstones, the historical liquid-protein cardiac deaths, uric
  acid, and refeeding electrolytes, sized. **Not held** (partial cover via EASL and
  [[Fasting Benefits and Risks]]).
- **An exercise-vs-hypocaloric-diet (and combined) body-composition / visceral-fat SR/MA** (Verheggen 2016;
  Khalafi 2023 dose-response) - the magnitude behind the deficit-source composition split. **Not held.**
- **A long-term (>=1 yr) weight-loss-maintenance SR** (Franz-2007-type) - the regain trajectory that
  reframes the sustainability sub-question. **Not held.**

## Caveats

- **Open loop (R1).** This grades coherence and fidelity to the sources it cites, never real-world
  validity. Nothing here was tested against a realized health outcome; a clean appraisal is not a validated
  recommendation.
- **Appraise, do not prescribe.** This appraises *what the rate of loss does to outcomes and where it
  harms* - it is **not** a weight-loss protocol. A very-low-calorie or rapid regimen with fatty liver
  (NAFLD), on glucose-lowering or antihypertensive medication, with a gallstone history, in the frail or
  older adult, or with an eating-disorder history is a **prescriber-supervised** matter: dosing,
  monitoring, refeeding management and drug de-prescribing are out of scope.
- **A stratum-level appraisal, applied per person.** Starting weight and adiposity, age, muscle mass,
  metabolic and gallbladder/liver status, medications, and what a person will actually sustain set the
  individual rate (layer 3). It is **not** a re-appraisal of whether to lose weight at all - that is
  [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].
- **Health axis only.** This weighs health outcomes; it prices no other objective.
