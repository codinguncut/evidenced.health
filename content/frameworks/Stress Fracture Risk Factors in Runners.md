**Peripheral scope** (exercise-programming), but the outcome is **patient-important**: a lower-extremity
stress fracture is a bone overuse injury that ends the training attempt for weeks to months (route-(c)
harm). Stress fractures are «common overuse injuries with up to 95% occurring in the lower extremities»
and account for «15-20% of all musculoskeletal injuries» among runners.
[@wright2015] The decision this page answers: *for a runner,
what raises stress-fracture risk, by how much — and does that risk-factor set transport to someone who is
not a runner (the obese novice; the military recruit)?* The headline is a strong negative with two
exceptions.

Headline conclusion: «there was a striking lack of conclusive evidence for any risk factor, but with two
exceptions: previous history of stress fracture and female sex».
[@wright2015] Only these two are «strongly supported by the
data». [@wright2015]

**Evidence-tier note (`confidence: low`).** Single gold SR-MA (Wright 2016; tier `gold`,
`meta-analysis`), but the base is thin: «8 articles met the inclusion criteria; 7 were considered low
risk. 4 articles qualiﬁed for meta-analysis.» [@wright2015]
and only **four independent cohorts** underlie the eight studies. [@wright2015] Prospective cohorts only; runners defined as cross-country, track-and-field,
triathlon, duathlon, and recreational. The two pooled estimates below are the firm core (both I2=0%); the
rest is conflicting single-study evidence, which is why the page is held `low` overall.

## The two supported risk factors — pooled estimates

Both are **intrinsic / non-modifiable** (the decision they drive is screening and vigilance, not
behaviour change), and both were pooled by random-effects inverse-variance meta-analysis with zero
measured heterogeneity:

| Risk factor | Pooled OR (95% CI) | Studies / N | I2 | Read |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Previous stress fracture** | **4.99 (2.91 to 8.56)**, p<0.001 | 3 / N=939 | 0% | \~5x risk; the strongest factor |
| **Female sex** | **2.31 (1.24 to 4.29)**, p=0.008 | 3 / N=480 | 0% | \~2.3x risk (distance runners) |

[@wright2015]

- **Previous stress fracture (\~5x).** The individual studies ranged OR 2.90 to 6.36; the pool confirmed
  them. [@wright2015] A caveat the authors raise and cannot
  resolve: «it is unknown whether this represents new injuries or a previous stress fracture which was not
  fully healed» [@wright2015] — i.e. the predictor may be
  partly incomplete healing rather than a stable trait.
- **Female sex (\~2.3x).** Significant on pooling, but the underlying studies *conflicted* (Yagi OR 3.10
  significant; Bennell OR 1.08 and Tenforde OR 1.35 both null), and the mechanism is contested — sex *per
  se* is likely a **proxy** for the energy-availability / menstrual / bone-density cluster below rather
  than an independent cause [inferred from @wright2015]. The pooled OR is reported
  bare of a located mechanism; treat it as a prognostic marker, not a causal lever.

## The rest — conflicting or single-study, none pooled

Every other candidate was either conflicting across studies or rested on a single cohort. The effect
sizes below are **individual-study** estimates, not pooled, and most carry wide CIs:

| Candidate | Direction / estimate (single studies) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Training volume** | >32 km/wk OR 2.40 (1.20-4.82); females >32 km/wk OR 3.08 (1.31-7.20); other study null | one study, threshold suspect (below) |
| **Low bone density** | spine BMD RR 1.89 (1.04-3.45); whole-body BMC RR 1.79 (1.02-3.13) | 1-2 studies, no OR poolable |
| **Low BMI / lean mass** | BMI<19 OR 2.76 (1.18-6.46); lean mass per-kg-decrease OR 1.14 (1.01-1.28) | single study each |
| **Menstrual dysfunction** | late menarche OR 5.30 (2.11-13.34), amenorrhoea OR 2.73 (1.16-6.4) in one study; null in two others | conflicting |
| **Nutrition / calcium** | protective (dairy, milk, calcium HRs \~0.3-0.6) in one cohort; harmful in another | conflicting |
| **Oral contraceptives** | ORs 0.55-2.22 across 4 studies | null — «no evidence» |
| **Biomechanics** | — | **gap: no included study examined it** |

[@wright2015]

- **Training volume — the threshold is suspect.** Only one study found a volume association, and the
  threshold is low: «the reported threshold for increased risk was a relatively low volume (32 km/week) in
  relation to the training regimens many runners undertake.» [@wright2015] The authors go further — the received wisdom is unsupported: «clinicians have
  little evidence to support the widespread anecdote that higher mileages are associated with increased
  risk of stress fracture, or the corresponding recommendation that reducing running volume can prevent
  stress fractures.» [@wright2015]
- **Calcium supplement reverse-causation.** Tenforde found calcium-*supplement* users at OR 3.36
  (1.4-7.9) — but flagged that «supplementation was also strongly correlated to previous history of
  stress fracture», so injured athletes may have been prescribed calcium as treatment, not primary
  prevention. [@wright2015] A protective exposure read
  backwards.
- **RED-S reframes the menstrual / nutrition cluster.** The conflicting menstrual findings may reflect a
  stale paradigm: «Menstrual dysfunction may be considered simply as one group of signs which occurs
  secondary to the issue of 'relative energy deficit in sports (RED-S)'» — with subclinical energy
  deficiency unmeasured in the risk-factor studies. [@wright2015] So low BMD, low BMI, late menarche, amenorrhoea and a protective-nutrition signal are
  plausibly **one underlying construct** (low energy availability) seen through different instruments,
  not independent levers [inferred from @wright2015].

## The interdependence caveat — why single-variable ORs mislead here

The risk factors are confounded with one another, and adjustment collapses them. In the best-measured
cohort, «some apparently significant risk factors (eg, training volume, body mass, racing performance)
became non-significant when multivariate analysis was conducted».
[@wright2015] This is the standard warning that a univariate
OR (training volume, BMI) is likely capturing a shared upstream cause (energy availability, body
composition), so the single-factor estimates in the table above should be read as associations entangled
with each other, not as separable causal levers [inferred from @wright2015].

## Transportability I — military data do not transfer to runners (within-paper)

Wright's framing motive is a transportability failure the field commits routinely: most stress-fracture
*knowledge* comes from military cohorts, and does not carry to runners. «Although military data are
commonly extrapolated to the general running population, this is likely inappropriate due to major
differences between these populations.» [@wright2015] The
support-factor mismatches are concrete: «Studies involving military personnel include smokers and
individuals with poor overall physical fitness, both of whom would not be expected in a population of
serious runners», recruits run far less (<10 miles/wk vs runners' 40-100+), carry heavy loads, wear
different footwear, and fracture during a 16-week acclimation window unlike a veteran runner's first
fracture after years. [@wright2015] This is a worked instance
of the fabric's transportability rule — *similar enough means similar in the support factors the
mechanism needs*, not in the label *does running*.

## Transportability II — the obese / sedentary novice: the host-factor set INVERTS

[inferred from @wright2015] — the fabric's own exercise-programming taper places the
**obese / deconditioned novice** in the fabric zone for injury (where a wrong prescription causes the
injury that ends the attempt). Wright's risk-factor set must **not** be imported wholesale into that
stratum, because its host factors run the opposite direction:

- Wright's bone-stress host profile is **low BMI (<19), low lean mass, low BMD, low energy availability,
  female-athlete-triad / RED-S** — markers of a *lean, often female, energy-deficient* athlete.
- The obese novice is the mirror: **high BMI, high mechanical loading**. Wright states the relevant
  mechanism — «Mechanical loading is associated with increased bone mineral density ... healthy bone
  mineral density requirements of active running individuals may be greater than that required by the
  general public». [@wright2015] Higher habitual body mass
  loads the skeleton, which raises BMD, so the obese novice is plausibly at **lower** bone-stress-fracture
  risk than the lean runner, not higher [inferred from @wright2015].
- **Mechanism, not outcome — and net effect, not the intended one.** This is a directional inference from
  Wright's stated loading->BMD mechanism, not a measured outcome in the obese stratum (Wright studied
  runners; no obese-novice stress-fracture cohort is held). What would confirm or refute it: a prospective
  cohort of obese novices tracking bone stress vs soft-tissue injury by baseline BMI/BMD. And the net
  injury picture does **not** favour the obese novice: higher mass that protects bone simultaneously
  **overloads tendon, plantar fascia and joints**, so the binding lower-extremity injury constraint for
  this stratum is likely **soft-tissue / joint**, not bone stress -> [[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]], [[Tendon Adaptation to Mechanical Loading]], [[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]]. So the decision-change is *which injury to screen and program around*: bone-stress screening
  (prior fracture, low BMI/BMD, menstrual/energy status) is for the lean runner; the obese novice's
  prescription guards the soft tissue and the joint instead.

## Parameter table — a DISTINCTION from Damsted, not a tension

Wright's *training volume -> stress fracture* and Damsted's *load-change -> running-related injury* look
like the same question and are not. The matched parameters fail the *same-quantity* test on every row, so
this is a distinction to record, never a tension to file (op-weave: no cell, no claim):

| Parameter | Wright (this page) | Damsted (Training Load Progression) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure | **absolute** weekly running volume (km/wk) + host factors | **rate of change** of weekly load (% ramp) | **NO** — level vs rate-of-change |
| Outcome | **bone** stress fracture (site-specific overuse) | **any** running-related injury (whole-body RRI) | **NO** — bone-specific vs general |
| Population | runners (cross-country/T&F/tri/duathlon/rec; often lean/female) | novice & amateur runners | partial overlap, not matched |
| Design | SR-MA, prospective cohorts, 2 factors pooled | SR, 4 heterogeneous studies, no pool | different synthesis depth |

The two are **complementary, not opposed**, and even agree where they touch: Wright explicitly leaves
room for load *change* — «Rapid changes in training volume and intensity are often considered in the
development of injuries, though this is difficult to quantify in epidemiological studies»
[@wright2015] — which is exactly the exposure Damsted
isolates. No clash to reconcile; they answer adjacent questions.

## Synthesis — the training-load injury heuristics are evidentially unsupported (type-A)

[inferred from @wright2015] — read together, Wright and Damsted (two independent
groups, different exposures, different outcomes) converge on a non-obvious meta-conclusion that neither
states alone: **the popular training-load rules for preventing running injury lack evidential support.**

- Wright: the «widespread anecdote that higher mileages are associated with increased risk of stress
  fracture» and the «corresponding recommendation that reducing running volume can prevent stress
  fractures» have «little evidence». [@wright2015]
- Damsted: no evidence exists for the *10% rule* (the ramp-rate heuristic), and the one direct test found
  10% and 24% weekly increases equivalent (the verbatim finding and quote live on its page) ->
  [[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]].

Two different load heuristics — one on absolute *volume*, one on rate of *change* — both turn out to be
received wisdom without an evidence base. This is independent backing for a *structural* claim about the
field (the heuristics are oversold), not for a single number. It does **not** license the opposite (*train
however you like*): both SRs are *insufficient-evidence* verdicts (measurement error and small
heterogeneous bases, not demonstrated safety), and the conservative gradual-progression default survives
on mechanism and low-regret grounds, as argued on the Damsted page. The convergence sharpens *what is
unknown*, not a permission.

## Decision relevance

- **Screen the lean runner on two things only: prior stress fracture (\~5x) and the energy-availability
  cluster.** A female runner with a prior stress fracture and clinical signs warrants early imaging; the
  low-BMI / low-BMD / menstrual-dysfunction markers are best read as one RED-S construct, not separate
  checkboxes.
- **Do not transport this risk-factor set to the obese / sedentary novice.** Their bone-stress risk is
  plausibly *lower* (higher mass -> higher BMD), and their binding injury constraint is soft-tissue /
  joint, not bone — so the prescription guards tendons and joints (gradual load, strength tolerance),
  not bone-stress screening.
- **Do not treat *cut your mileage* as evidence-based stress-fracture prevention.** The volume-threshold
  evidence is one study at a suspiciously low cutpoint, and the authors say the reduce-volume
  recommendation lacks support. (Volume is also a poor proxy for skeletal load — intensity and surface
  vary within a fixed mileage.)
- **Do not transport military stress-fracture findings to runners** (or vice versa) — different smoking,
  fitness, load-carriage, footwear and acclimation-window support factors.
- **This is insufficient evidence on most factors, not reassurance.** Biomechanics was unstudied; most
  factors conflict; only four cohorts underlie the field. The firm core is the two pooled ORs; everything
  else is a named gap.
