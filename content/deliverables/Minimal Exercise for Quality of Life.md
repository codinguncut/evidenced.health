## Walk most days and lift twice a week, and build both slowly over the first three months

**An obese, inactive adult who starts to move pulls one of the large levers, alongside weight itself.**
 -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]], [[Big Rocks (Median)]], [[Losing Fat and Keeping It Off]]

- **Walk briskly most days, building from 5-10 minutes toward about 20-40 minutes a day.** The first few minutes go with the largest observational drop in mortality. -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]
- **Lift twice a week:** two or more sets per exercise for every major muscle group, stopping 2-3 reps
  short of failure and adding load as sets get easier.
  -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]
- **Ramp both over two to three months.** ACSM's screening panel sets a 2-3-month transitional phase
  for aerobic exercise, to limit cardiac risk (consensus, not a trial)
  [@riebe2015screen]; this card extends it to
  lifting.
- **No trial has tested any such package for quality of life, function or the shape of later-life
  decline in obese, sedentary middle-aged adults.** The plan rests on strength and fitness trials,
  observational mortality data and consensus safety rules.
- **Once both habits hold, stop chasing extra minutes for mortality.** The observational mortality
  curves flatten at \~20-40 min/day of device-measured MVPA (roughly brisk walking). Risk sits lowest with \~30-120 min/week of strength work (observational); beyond that the added benefit is unclear and higher volume may warrant caution. Strength
  itself still rose most with heavier multi-set training, so more is optional, not useless.
  -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]], [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]

| Session | What | How long | How hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walk, 5-7 days | brisk walk; cycle or water exercise if a knee hurts | \~10 min, adding a few minutes a week toward \~20-40 | breathing noticeably up, RPE 12-13, 40-<60% of heart-rate reserve |
| Strength, 2 days | 4 movements: upper and lower body, push and pull | \~20-30 min | >=2 sets each, 2-3 reps in reserve |
| Optional, from \~week 8-12 | one walk or ride becomes intervals | same | hard bouts, easy recovery; saves time, not required |

ACSM sets the effort band; the durations, step-ups and rest days between strength sessions are untested
practice defaults.

| Period | What happens | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1-4 | easy walks, light loads; expect minor aches and adjust rather than quit | consensus; trial-pooled minor harms |
| Weeks 5-12 | minutes and loads rise; tendons lag muscle | consensus; tendon data in young men -> [[Tendon Adaptation to Mechanical Loading]] |
| Month 3 on | the maintained dose; the minimum that keeps gains is unknown | adults >=60 only -> [[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]] |

Start with the aerobic half. Walking is the safe way to start and, in the observational data, its first minutes go with the largest drop in mortality per minute.

## Start with 5-10 minutes of brisk walking a day and build the minutes; the first few count most

**Add about 5-10 minutes a day of walking at about 4-5 km/h.** In seven device-measured cohorts (adults
40 and over, n=40,327), an extra 5 minutes a day of MVPA, from about 1 to 6, went with roughly **30%
lower all-cause mortality**; from 9 to 14 minutes, roughly 18% lower. The paper prints no confidence
interval for either. Under 60, the age of this card's stratum, the data were too thin to show it (five
studies, 257 deaths): insufficient evidence, not a null.
[@ekelund2026small]

**The observational cohorts counted deaths, not function**, and frail people who move less can inflate this low end of the curve. The model credited an extra hour a day of light activity with roughly the same share of deaths averted as five extra minutes a day of MVPA, an option for someone who cannot walk briskly.
[@ekelund2026small]
The 5-10 minutes is a practice default. -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

**Build toward about 20-40 minutes a day; past that the gains are small.** The linked page reads about
20-40 min/day of device-measured (hip-sensor) MVPA, roughly brisk walking, as a broad flattening
zone, not a precise dose. The data run to about 50 min/day.
 -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]
The lowest point, against the least active, was HR 0.39 (0.26-0.59)
[@ekelundacc2019], with no clear further
reduction beyond the flattening [@ekelund2026small].
**The data do not show more to be worthless; they show its gain as small and uncertain.**

Step counts show the same shape, flattening at
about 8,000-10,000 a day under 60 [@paluch2022].
These step and minute analyses share authors and cohorts, so they are one line of evidence.
 -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

**Moderate effort already raises fitness.** An AHA scientific statement puts the fitness (CRF) gain
from meeting activity guidance at about 10% in previously sedentary adults. It draws on trials in
sedentary adults, several of them overweight or obese (STRRIDE; a 24-week trial in 300 obese adults).
CRF appears to respond more to intensity than to longer or more frequent sessions, and fitter people
need harder work. Below 10 METs, about 50% of heart-rate reserve was enough
[@ross2016].

Most people in this group probably sit below 10 METs, though no held source measures it, so harder
work is optional for a beginner. In trials in adults with a BMI of 30 or more, the point gains in fitness did not reach
significance [@odonoghue2020]. CRF is a
surrogate. -> [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]], [[Exercise Modality for Body Composition in Obesity]]

**Bunch or spread the minutes as suits you.** People who packed their activity into one or two
sessions a week showed no detectable mortality difference from those who spread it (observational). As a hedge, favour sessions of about 30 minutes or more:
in one self-reported cohort, sessions under 30 minutes showed no mortality association (HR 1.03,
0.94-1.14 bunched; 0.95, 0.89-1.01 spread)
[@odonovan2024]. Count short walks
toward the daily total; that is a practice default. Brief *exercise snacks* may raise
fitness, on very uncertain evidence: count them as extra, not a replacement.
-> [[Weekend Warrior Activity Pattern and Mortality]], [[Exercise Snacks and Cardiometabolic Health]]

## Lift twice a week: two hard sets per exercise, every major muscle group, and keep adding load

Walking trains the heart and lungs, not strength or muscle. Resistance training (RT) is the other half.

**Work every major muscle group at least twice a week, two or more sets per exercise, stopping about 2-3
reps short of failure.** Bands, machines and free weights all count. The ACSM stand proposes 2-3 reps
in reserve as a gauge, a default rather than a tested target
[@acsm2026rt]. In a network meta-analysis of RCTs, **all 12 prescriptions had point estimates favouring more strength over no training (SMD 0.75-1.60)**; the two sparse once-weekly single-set arms had intervals crossing zero. Across strength and muscle size, 91% of
head-to-head comparisons between prescriptions were inconclusive
[@currier2023].

Two sets twice a week is a dose shown to work, and single sets twice weekly also beat no training on
strength [@currier2023]. ACSM reports
strength gains flattening beyond about 2-3 sets per exercise
[@acsm2026rt].
-> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]

**Add load or reps as the sets get easier.** Load drives strength; sets drive muscle size. The ACSM 2026
stand words progression two ways, but both give a beginner this advice.

**This card carries these defaults over to people with obesity; the evidence behind them excluded that group.** The stand's definition of *healthy adults* excluded obesity, sarcopenia and frailty [@acsm2026rt]. The trials measured strength and
size only: «We do not know how these RTx affect relevant health outcomes.»
[@currier2023]

**A modest weekly volume goes with lower mortality.** In self-reported cohorts (very low GRADE), any
muscle-strengthening activity versus none went with **15% lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.85,
0.79-0.93)** [@momma2022].
All-cause risk was lowest at 40 min/week (RR 0.83, 0.79-0.86) and stayed below no activity to about 130-140; above that the effect is unclear, and higher volume may warrant caution [@momma2022].
A pooled US analysis saw no added benefit in its top category, above 120 min/week
[@zhang2026rtmort]. Two 20-30-minute sessions
make 40-60 minutes a week.
-> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]

**Do both, and keep lifting even with plenty of aerobic exercise.** People who did both had **40% lower mortality than those who did neither (RR 0.60, 0.54-0.67; observational)** [@momma2022]. If only one, aerobic
activity has the larger observational estimate (not matched for time)
[@zhang2026rtmort], but only RT builds strength.
Above 45 MET-h a week of aerobic activity, one analysis pooling three US cohorts (its men also sit in
Momma's pool) detected no further mortality trend from RT
[@zhang2026rtmort]. It measured no strength,
function, falls or quality of life, which are the reasons to lift.

Grip strength is a number to measure, not to train toward: it partly reflects inheritance and ill-health.
-> [[Grip Strength and Mortality]]

## Ramp slowly for two to three months: heart, tendons and joints adapt more slowly than you will want to progress

Walking and lifting both load tissue that adapts slowly, so a programme lasts or fails in its first months. The harms below change how you start, not whether you start. The ACSM
panel set its 2-3-month progressive phase within an aerobic-exercise screening model, to limit cardiac
risk; it is consensus, not a tested ramp
[@riebe2015screen]. This card extends it to
strength training.

**Start light-to-moderate exercise without a doctor's clearance if you are inactive, have no symptoms,
and have no known heart, kidney or metabolic disease (including diabetes).** With known disease or any symptom, get clearance first. The rule is consensus: the ACSM panel found no evidence that clearance or
exercise testing lowers risk.
[@riebe2015screen]
-> [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]]

**Build effort up, and skip unaccustomed hard one-offs.** Exercise-related sudden cardiac death is
rare: **0.31 to 2.1 per 100,000 person-years**.
[@franklin2020acute] The relative
risk during a bout is highest in sedentary people with known or hidden heart disease doing strenuous
work they are not used to, and it shrinks as regular sessions accumulate. The bout figures come from
case-crossover and case-control studies.
[@franklin2020acute]
The advice comes from one ACSM/AHA consensus lineage. -> [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]]

**Expect more minor aches; change the load or the exercise rather than quit.** In exercise-therapy
trials, exercise raised non-serious events (mostly pain, fatigue, bursitis, low back pain) by about a
fifth, **RR 1.19 (1.09-1.30)**: one extra event per six people exercising. Serious events: no excess
detected (RR 0.96, 0.90-1.02), which is not proof of safety.
[@niemeijer2020] Participants were mostly patients and older adults, so this card assumes the result transfers to an obese novice, and it labels adjust-rather-than-stop as practice. -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]

**Raise lifting loads over weeks, because tendons adapt more slowly than muscle.** Heavy loading
(above 70% of maximal contraction or repetition maximum) stiffened tendons, SMD **0.90 (0.71-1.08)**;
light loading showed no detectable change (SMD 0.04, -0.46 to 0.53).
[@bohm2015] Participants were healthy adults aged
18-50 [@bohm2015], mostly men (237 of 264)
[@bohm2015]; obese or deconditioned tendons are unstudied.
Heavy loads are optional for this goal: stiffness is a tissue marker, not an injury outcome, and you
need not test a one-rep max. -> [[Tendon Adaptation to Mechanical Loading]]

**If a knee hurts or is arthritic, start low-impact: walking, cycling or water.** In knee and hip
osteoarthritis trials (mostly knee), exercise cut pain at about 8 weeks by SMD **0.56 (0.44-0.68)**
against usual care. The effect did not differ detectably between trials with a mean BMI of 30 or more and those below it (study-level, limited power).
[@goh2019]
-> [[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]]

> **When to stop and get checked.** Pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw or arms; unusual
> breathlessness; lightheadedness; a rapid or irregular heartbeat; ankle swelling; burning or cramping in
> the legs on short walks.
> [@franklin2020acute]
> ACSM: anyone exercising who develops such signs should stop and get medical clearance before
> resuming [@riebe2015screen].
> No held source gives a rule for a novice's sharp or joint pain. The labelled practice default: stop
> that movement and reduce the load. The pain allowance used in chronic-pain rehab trials does not
> transfer. -> [[Exercising Into Pain for Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain]]

**Things to watch: injury and safety**

- Stretching is not shown to prevent injury. Three trials pooled to RR 0.963 (0.846-1.095)
  [@lauersen2013injury],
  an interval running from about a 15% cut to a 10% rise. Most of those injuries come from
  one trial in general adults, and Lauersen's counts for it give a crude ratio of about 0.94.
  Liddle gives the same trial HR 0.75 (0.59-0.96), and its table lists the trial's outcome as overall
  injuries and soreness
  [@liddle2023effects].
  Which outcome that HR covers is not stated, so the two readings stay unresolved: insufficient evidence,
  not a tight null. Stretching does not meaningfully relieve next-day soreness
  [@herbert2011stretch].
  No held source sets a warm-up dose. -> [[Flexibility Training and Stretching]]
- Avoid eccentric-only sessions at the start: they can cause severe muscle damage and soreness
  [@garber2011acsm].
  -> [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]]
- With osteoporosis, swap repeated forward-bending exercises of the spine for extension work, on what the
  authors call limited evidence of fracture harm
  [@kunutsor2018adverse];
  if frail, start lower, ideally with guidance. -> [[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]], [[Frailty]]

**Running, if you take it up**

- Leave running out of the first months: a practice default for unconditioned tissue.
  Higher BMI (aOR 1.17 per unit, 1.10-1.24) and a past knee injury (aOR 2.67, 1.41-5.05) raise the odds of radiographic knee OA
  [@duong2025kneeoa]. Hip or knee OA in
  recreational runners vs sedentary controls: OR 0.86 (0.69-1.07); competitive: 1.34 (0.97-1.86)
  [@alentorngeli2017].
  -> [[Knee Osteoarthritis Incidence and Risk Factors]]
- The 10% weekly-mileage rule is unsupported: 10% and 24% increases did not differ, HR 0.8 (0.6-1.3),
  insufficient evidence rather than a null.
  [@damsted2018]
  -> [[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]]
- A runner may try an injury-prevention programme as this card's optional default, not a tested one
. Wu finds such programmes do not appear to reduce injury, and calls supervised sessions
  two to four times a week most likely to help. Only four of nine trials used strength exercises, at
  relatively low volume; Baltich, the one low-risk-of-bias trial, had more injuries in the programme
  arm, its interval crossing no effect; no harms outcome is reported
  [@wu2024exercise]. Exponentiated, risk is RR 0.81
  (0.63-1.05; 7 trials), rate IRR 0.86 (0.64-1.16; 6 trials): insufficient evidence, not a null. The
  supervised split is post hoc and tied to compliance, so a hypothesis; silence on harms is not proof
  of safety.
  -> [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]]

## Pick the mode you will keep doing; add intervals later, for time, not because you must

Once you can handle the ramp, **decide the rest by what you will keep doing.**

**Choose walking, cycling, water or a mix by preference and joint tolerance.** A network
meta-analysis compared aerobic, resistance and combined programmes in trials of adults with a BMI of
30 or more. None raised fitness significantly, even against no exercise
[@odonoghue2020]. The trials separate the
programmes too little to rank them; that is not proof that they are equal. Combined aerobic and strength work ranked
at or near the top on body composition
[@odonoghue2020], and this plan already
combines them. For cycling with knee osteoarthritis, see
[[Stationary Cycling for Knee Osteoarthritis]].
-> [[Exercise Modality for Body Composition in Obesity]], [[Is the Exercise Modality Doing Any Work]]

**Add one interval session once walking is a habit, and add it to save time.** In overweight or obesity,
three reviews put the fitness edge of intervals over moderate continuous training at SMD 0.26 to 0.99, a
range across reviews, not a confidence interval
[@poon2024]. Most reviews in that umbrella
scored moderate to critically low on quality
[@poon2024]. The two-to-three-month wait
is practice, not a tested rule.

**Intervals feel worse while you do them** because of the intensity
-> [[Affective Response to High-Intensity Interval Exercise]]; no held study shows that this makes people
quit. With known or suspected coronary disease, an AHA statement says more studies are needed
before interval training is widely adopted, especially unsupervised
[@franklin2020acute].
-> [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]], [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]]

## Use fitness numbers as gauges, not goals

**Track fitness as a readout; you control the activity directly.** In cohort studies, each 1 MET of
higher cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF; 1 MET = 3.5 ml/kg/min) goes with lower all-cause mortality, RR
0.87 (0.84-0.90) [@kodama2009].
An umbrella review grades the CRF mortality evidence very low to moderate, mainly because most studies
included only men; two further, overlapping meta-analyses give 0.88-0.89 per MET (one HR 0.89,
0.86-0.92) [@lang2024crf].
The linked page reads the per-MET all-cause grade as low. -> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]
Fitness and activity each track lower mortality; cohort studies show both as associations.
**Treat a 1-MET gain as a milestone**, and do not multiply the per-MET figure.

**Set effort by feel, not by the activity's label.** Moderate means 40 to <60% of heart-rate reserve,
RPE 12-13, breathing noticeably up
[@riebe2015screen].
For an unfit starter a brisk walk can already be vigorous, so **judge by heart rate and breathing**; on a
beta-blocker, by perceived effort alone.
-> [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]], [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]

**Measure with a repeated walk test and read the trend.** The UKK 2-km walk test estimates fitness from
walk time and finishing heart rate. Two estimates a week apart differed by up to about 8 ml/kg/min (95%
limits −8.13 to 7.54)
[@marinjimenez2023walk].
Oja's group advises a first walk for familiarization, because the second test ran faster and gave a slightly higher estimate [@oja1991walk].

**The \~10% gain a sedentary adult can expect** (Ross's estimate,
[@ross2016]) **is about 3
ml/kg/min, inside that noise**, so one before-and-after pair cannot confirm it. If you are
losing weight, read fitness in litres per minute: the per-kilogram figure rises with weight loss alone
[@odonoghue2020].
-> [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]]

## Keep going: treat it as a dose to keep, not a course to finish

In knee- and hip-osteoarthritis patients, a different group from this card's, exercise gave the most relief at about 2 months and did no better than usual care by 9-18 months. The trials show the fade,
not its cause; the authors suggest poor adherence may be one reason
[@goh2019].
A planned break does not erase the gains: trained adults aged 60 and over kept a function advantage
after 4-36 weeks off (standardized difference 0.88, 95% CI 0.47-1.29)
[@buendiaromero2025]; this is untested in middle age
. **Those trials tested training cessation**
[@buendiaromero2025], **so the smallest dose that keeps
the gains is unmeasured**.
-> [[Detraining and Residual Effects in Older Adults]], [[Exercise Therapy for Osteoarthritis Pain and Function]]

### Things to watch: adherence and compensation

- **Write an if-then plan and book a check-in.** Pooled across all trials, plans did not significantly
  raise self-reported activity (SMD 0.15, -0.01 to 0.31; trials of 2-32 weeks)
  [@silva2018implint].
  The check-in is labelled practice. -> [[Implementation Intentions and Action Planning for Physical Activity]]
- **A tracker adds steps, for a while.** Across trials, trackers added about 627 steps a day (417-862,
  low-quality evidence); the two trials of a year or more reported lower tracker adherence
  [@brickwood2019wearable].
  Pick one that sends prompts; that is practice. -> [[Wearable Activity Trackers and Physical Activity]]
- **Exercise at home by default.** In people aged 60 and over, supervised exercise beat unsupervised exercise on one measure that held up, knee-extension strength (SMD 0.18, 0.07-0.30) [@gomezredondo2024].
  A coach for technique is optional. -> [[Supervised vs Unsupervised Exercise]]
- **Expect little weight loss from exercise alone.** In trials in adults with a BMI of 30 or more, mean
  losses ranged from 0.05 to 1.01 kg across programmes
  [@odonoghue2020]. On average, 18% of the
  energy deficit exercise should create failed to show up in the body (SD 93%)
  [@riou2015]. Fitness and function do not depend
  on those calories. -> [[Exercise Modality for Body Composition in Obesity]],
  [[Exercise Energy Compensation]], [[Losing Fat and Keeping It Off]]

Several of these tools share repeated contact (a call, a text, a coach), which may be what works, but that rests only on between-trial comparisons.

## Where it stops paying, what the evidence cannot see, and how this compares with guidance

Once the habit holds, ask two things: where the gains stop, and what the evidence cannot show.
-> [[Physical Activity]]

### Where the gains flatten

**Once both habits hold, stop chasing extra minutes for mortality.** The observational mortality curves
flatten at about 20-40 min/day of device-measured MVPA (roughly brisk walking).
 -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

For strength work, the lowest risk sits across about 30-120 min/week: the all-cause low point was 40 min/week (RR 0.83, 0.79-0.86), and higher volume may warrant caution [@momma2022]. A pooled analysis
saw no added benefit above 120 [@zhang2026rtmort].
All of it is observational.
-> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]

For strength itself, gains plateau beyond about 2-3 sets per exercise
[@acsm2026rt]. But the largest point
estimate came from heavy multi-set training three or more times a week (SMD 1.60, 1.38-1.82)
[@currier2023], so more is optional, not
useless.

Separately, Lang, citing a family study, writes that genes account for nearly half the variance in
fitness and that habitual activity mainly shapes the rest
[@lang2024crf].

### How this compares with guidance

**This plan agrees with WHO's 150-300 min/week of moderate activity plus strength work on two or more
days, and the curve shows why the low end is enough.** -> [[Physical Activity]]
The 150-min floor is about 22 min/day on one hip-sensor definition of MVPA, near where the curve
flattens [@ekelund2026small].
In one pooled cohort analysis, people who met both guidelines had HR 0.71 (0.57-0.87) against those who met neither (observational, moderate certainty) [@who2020]. That analysis overlaps
the cohorts behind Momma's RR 0.60, and it uses guideline thresholds rather than any activity. A guideline
sets one clear floor for everyone; it does not claim a measured best dose.

**ACSM's *exercise participation* is a screening label, not a target**: planned, structured activity of
at least 30 min at moderate intensity on at least 3 days a week for at least the last 3 months
[@riebe2015screen]. Earlier guidance set 2-3
minutes of rest between sets; ACSM's 2026 strength stand found strength unaffected by short versus long
rest [@acsm2026rt]. Guideline bodies split on
testing before starting [@riebe2015screen], and
the evidence they cite does not settle it.
-> [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]], [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]]

**Balance training: the falls data come from older adults.** In community-dwelling older adults,
exercise of all types cut the rate of falls by 23% (rate ratio 0.77, 0.71-0.83, high certainty)
[@sherrington2019]. For middle age, ACSM 2011 graded
any benefit of neuromotor (balance) training D, panel consensus for lack of data
[@garber2011acsm]. That is
insufficient evidence, not no effect. -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]

### What the evidence cannot see

- **The shape of decline.** Trials count deaths, not years of function held. Exercise, alone or within a
  multidomain programme, was linked to better intrinsic capacity and quality of life in 11 of 13
  studies, mostly in older adults, but the effects were generally small and not pooled
  [@zheng2026healthspan].
  -> [[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]
- **Safety and choices.** No held source gives a strength-training injury rate, a stop rule for sharp
  joint pain, tendon data for heavy beginners, mode-vs-mode function trials in obesity, or supervised
  vs home data below 60. -> [[Supervised vs Unsupervised Exercise]]
- **Fitness as a lever.** No trial shows that raising one's own fitness changes survival. In change
  cohorts, people who went from unfit to fit had lower mortality (observational).
  -> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]

### The plan in seven lines

1. **Walk at moderate effort**, building from 5-10 extra minutes a day toward 20-40 min/day.
2. **Lift twice a week**: two or more sets per exercise for the major muscle groups, 2-3 reps short of
   failure, adding load. Every load, set and frequency combination had a point estimate favouring more strength over no training, though once-weekly single sets had intervals crossing zero. With at least two sets or two
   sessions a week, there was a 95% probability that the training increased strength over no training
   [@currier2023].
3. **Ramp over two to three months.** Expect one extra minor adverse event per six people exercising
   (NNTH 6, 4-11, mostly patients) [@niemeijer2020].
4. **Pick the mode you will keep.** Intervals are an optional later add-on, to save time (fitness SMD
   0.26-0.99 over moderate training, overweight or obese adults)
   [@poon2024].
5. **Track fitness; do not target it.** Each 1 MET of fitness goes with RR 0.87 (0.84-0.90) for death,
   between people [@kodama2009]; cohort
   data, low certainty. -> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]
6. **Keep going.** In knee and hip osteoarthritis patients (mostly knee), a different group, the benefit
   was no better than usual care by 9-18 months; the trials show the fade, not its cause
   [@goh2019].
7. **Then stop chasing extra minutes for mortality.** The observational curves flatten at \~20-40 min/day of device-measured MVPA, and risk sits lowest with \~30-120 min/week of strength work (observational). More strength work is optional, not useless. -> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]
