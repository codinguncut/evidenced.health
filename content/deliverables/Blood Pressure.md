## Lowering blood pressure prevents heart attacks and strokes, but how much it buys depends on your risk, not your reading

**Blood pressure drugs cut major cardiovascular events by about a tenth for each 5 mmHg they take off
systolic pressure**, counting heart attacks, strokes, heart failure and cardiovascular deaths together.
The trials found that slope in people with and without prior heart disease, and at every starting
pressure they studied, from normal to high
[@bplttc2021]. What that
tenth is worth depends on how likely a heart attack or stroke was in the first place. **The same fall buys a lot for someone at high overall risk and little for someone young and otherwise
healthy.** So decide on absolute cardiovascular risk, not on the reading alone
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).

Exercise, weight loss, cutting heavy drinking, a DASH-style diet and less salt each lower blood pressure
by a few points, exercise by more in people whose pressure is already high. **Their published effects overlap and cannot simply be added.** The one trial that tested a combination,
DASH plus a sodium cut, found that the pair lowered pressure more than either change alone, but by less
than the two effects added together.

For most of these changes, no trial has counted heart attacks and strokes. **Any benefit on heart attacks
and strokes is assumed.** This guide applies the drug-trial slope to their fall in pressure. That step rests on the
untested premise that a millimetre lowered by diet or exercise is worth as much as a millimetre lowered by a pill. For alcohol, a population risk model projected the
benefit from the BP fall. A potassium-containing salt substitute is the exception: one large Chinese
trial, mostly in people who had already had a stroke, measured fewer strokes and deaths.

The one large lifestyle weight-loss trial that counted events found no clear reduction.
A pool of weight-loss trials, dominated by that trial, was also null on CV events but found fewer deaths
from any cause; the pool does not show whether that gain came through fewer CV events. One weight-loss drug,
semaglutide, cut CV events in a trial that enrolled only people with established cardiovascular disease
(a past heart attack or stroke, or symptomatic peripheral arterial disease); it is untested for primary prevention. Its authors call the mechanism speculative. At the drug-trial
slope its 3.3 mmHg fall would explain only about a 7% cut against the 20% seen. So the BP fall cannot
account for most of the benefit
 ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]],
[[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]], [[Surrogate Outcomes]]).

Trials of garlic, yoga, mindfulness and slow-breathing programmes report some of the largest drops, but
no trial has tested any of them on heart attacks or strokes. **For each mind-body method, the large fall
shrinks under a stricter test**: an active control, 24-hour monitoring, unmedicated patients or the
low-bias trials.
Fibre, sweeteners and treating sleep apnoea barely move blood pressure
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

For a person at high risk or with established heart disease, a drug is the main option, and
lifestyle adds to it. **For someone with untreated grade 1 hypertension (140-159 / 90-99 mmHg) and low overall risk,
neither a drug nor lifestyle buys much protection against heart attacks and strokes over the five or so
years the trials ran.** The direct drug trials in that group are too small to confirm or rule out the benefit. Starting a drug
also has a cost, measured at low certainty in one old trial: more people stopped treatment because of
side effects.

At intermediate risk the one modern drug trial found no clear benefit
overall, and the benefit expected from the slope is small. A small remaining gain over those years is a result, not a gap to close with more effort: a person in that
group can stop chasing a large near-term cut in heart attacks. For a younger adult, the answer can point the
other way once risk is counted over a lifetime, as the risk section does ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]],
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]).

**Exercise and weight loss do things a blood pressure pill cannot**: they also work through pathways other
than pressure, so a pill does not replace them
([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]).

In high-risk people, a target below 120 mmHg
(achieved about 121) prevented more events than one below 140, at a real cost in side effects that each
person has to weigh.

The worry that pressure can go too low comes mainly from observational curves.
The main pooled analysis of drug trials (BPLTTC) does cite one randomised trial and one tabular
meta-analysis that found no benefit below 140 in people without heart disease. But in BPLTTC's own
patient-level data the relative benefit held down to baseline pressures below 120, so within those trials
the extra risk that observational curves show at low pressure is not an effect of treatment.
One group stays contested: people without heart disease whose pressure is
normal or only mildly raised at the start. At 85 and over, and in frail people, the trials cannot yet
say whether the benefit holds ([[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]],
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

This guide is for an adult whose blood pressure is raised or drifting upward. It does not cover
diagnosis, choosing a drug or setting a dose. The person, not this guide, decides how to weigh a longer life, a lower dementia risk and a drug's side
effects against each other. Everything that follows rests
on one measured slope from the drug trials, so start there.

## A drug-induced fall of 5 mmHg cuts major CV events by about a tenth, at every starting pressure studied (below 120 to 170+ mmHg)

This guide judges every option first by what it does to blood pressure. Only the drug trials counted
heart attacks, strokes and deaths at scale, so where no trial counted events, this guide borrows their
slope to estimate them. The Blood Pressure Lowering
Treatment Trialists' Collaboration (BPLTTC) pooled individual participant data, each person's own record rather than each
trial's summary, from 344,716 people in 48 randomised trials. **Each 5 mmHg fall in systolic pressure cut
major cardiovascular events by about a tenth:** hazard ratio 0.91 (0.89-0.94) in people without prior
CVD and 0.89 (0.86-0.92) in people with it
[@bplttc2021].

The authors' summary: «In this large-scale analysis of randomised trials, a 5 mm Hg reduction of systolic blood pressure reduced the risk of major cardiovascular events by about 10%, irrespective of previous diagnoses of cardiovascular disease, and even at normal or high–normal blood pressure values.»
[@bplttc2021]

Stroke and heart failure fell most: per 5 mmHg, stroke 13%, heart failure 13%, ischaemic heart disease
8% and CV death 5%. Across seven baseline-SBP bands, from below 120 to 170 mmHg and above, the trials
found no reliable heterogeneity, and no subgroup had a hazard ratio above 1
[@bplttc2021].
The evidence is less firm for one group: people without CVD in the lower two thirds of one modern
trial's baseline SBP, where a prespecified test pointed the other way. The section on how low to go
takes it up [inferred from @lonn2016hope3].

The trials enrolled people who were older and at higher risk than a typical adult with a mild reading.
Mean age was 65. Without prior CVD, the comparator arms had 31.9 major CV events per 1000 person-years
and the treated arms 25.9, so the untreated rate ran about 3% a year
[@bplttc2021]. **Those
crude rates are not a per-5-mmHg absolute effect:** they pool trials with different achieved falls and
follow-up. The trials ran from 1972 to 2013 on older regimens, and the slope describes drug-induced lowering; it does not by itself carry over to pressure lowered by
lifestyle change
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], *Limits*).

Across whole populations, high systolic pressure also comes first. In the Global Burden of Disease (GBD)
2019 ranking, at GBD's mid-level grouping of risk factors, it was the leading risk factor for deaths
worldwide:
10.8 million deaths (9.51-12.1 million), 19.2% (16.9-21.3%) of all deaths, ahead of tobacco at 8.71 million
[@gbd2020rf]. It was also the leading risk factor for
attributable DALYs at ages 50-74 and 75 and over
[@gbd2020rf]. **That ranking counts deaths attributed to
high BP across a population; it does not say what one person gains by lowering theirs.** It also rests on
modelled relative risks, not on primary effect sizes ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]],
*cross-domain superset*).

### Treat on absolute risk, not on the reading

Because the relative effect held regardless of current BP or prior heart disease, the BPLTTC authors call
for considering treatment in anyone at a sufficiently high absolute CV risk. They also call for judging
the drug by the risk it removes rather than by the BP fall itself. They add a caution:
«However, the fact that stratified effects were similar among the phenotypes investigated does not necessarily mean that it is worthwhile treating every patient, or that there is no particular subgroup for whom proportional risk reductions will be greater or smaller.»
[@bplttc2021]
They write that the decision also has to take in the person's overall risk, the chance of adverse
effects, the cost and the person's preferences [@bplttc2021].

The authors warn against reading the trials' absolute differences as policy numbers: randomised trials are strong because they give unbiased relative effects, and absolute risk is better
taken from representative populations
[@bplttc2021].
Inside BPLTTC, prior CVD and baseline SBP did not sort people into larger and smaller absolute gains.
**Scaling the benefit by baseline risk covers the much wider gap between a trial population and a
low-risk adult, a gap the trials' own subgroups do not span** ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). The
risk section gives the inputs; the drug comparison gives the per-1000 figures at low and intermediate
risk.

BPLTTC leaves two questions open. It fixed a 5 mmHg unit, so it does not set a target; SPRINT, the intensive-target trial in the section on how low to go, fills in the target and its harms.
Nor does BPLTTC quantify adverse effects for an individual; the authors name them as an input but do not
measure them
[@bplttc2021].

### Drug lowering passes the surrogate test; most lifestyle changes borrow its slope

A BP fall is a surrogate. It counts toward events only as far as someone has shown that lowering pressure that way cuts events.
GRADE's test is whether the outcome can be predicted from the marker, not merely correlated with it
([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). **Drug lowering passes that test; for most lifestyle changes the benefit on
events is borrowed from the drug slope.** The table gives each option's typical SBP fall, how certain that
fall is, and the section the figures come from. It then sorts the options by what backs their effect on
events. *Measured* means a trial counted events. *Modelled* means a risk model projected them. *Assumed*
means the drug slope is applied without an event trial of that option. *Untested* means there is no event
trial, and the BP effect itself rests on weak, short or observational data, so even the borrowed slope is
not credited.

## Exercise, weight loss and cutting heavy drinking each lower SBP a few mmHg, but no trial shows that fall cutting CV events

Three behaviour changes each lower systolic pressure by a few mmHg: structured exercise, losing weight,
and cutting down from heavy drinking. Exercise lowers it most in people who start at 140 mmHg or above.
**None of the three has a trial showing that its BP fall prevents heart attacks or strokes.** One weight-loss drug did cut CV events in people with established cardiovascular disease, but the trial
did not show that the cut came through lower BP.
 -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]

### Structured exercise lowers SBP by about 5 mmHg, and by about 9 mmHg in people with hypertension

Naci's network meta-analysis of 391 RCTs set structured exercise against no-exercise control on SBP,
with readings that ranged from office to ambulatory. Across all exercise trials, whose participants
averaged 132 mmHg at baseline, exercise changed SBP by -4.84 mmHg (95% CrI -5.55 to -4.13). **In the 56
trials (3508 people) that started at 140 mmHg or above, the change was -8.96 mmHg (-10.27 to -7.64).** A
higher starting pressure leaves more to lower. [@naci2019exercise]

These trials are weaker than the drug trials they are usually set beside. Of 20 sampled exercise RCTs,
17 were at high risk of performance and detection bias because nobody was blinded; 1 of 20 drug RCTs
was [@naci2019exercise]. **Participants cannot be blinded to exercise, so performance bias is built into this evidence**; blinded
outcome assessment could reduce detection bias.

**The type of exercise matters little, except that combining endurance and resistance training does
best.** Endurance, dynamic resistance and isometric training lowered SBP by similar amounts; the
combination beat dynamic resistance alone by -2.98 mmHg (-5.04 to -0.93)
[@naci2019exercise]. In the trials starting at
140 or above it beat every single type and reached -13.51 mmHg (-16.55 to -10.45) against control
[@naci2019exercise].

Few trials tested the combination, and they did not consistently report how often or how long people
trained [@naci2019exercise], so the edge may reflect
more total training rather than the mix itself. For a person with hypertension who can do both, the trials favour combined training. Among single types, pick the one the
person will keep doing. -> [[Is the Exercise Modality Doing Any Work]]

No trial has tested exercise head to head against a BP drug; the drug comparison below takes up how the
two compare. [inferred from @naci2019exercise] ->
[[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

### Diet programmes lower SBP by about 5 mmHg at six months, and most of it fades by a year as the weight returns

Ge's network meta-analysis of 121 RCTs (21,942 overweight or obese adults) compared weight-loss diet
programmes with usual diet. At six months, low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets took off 4.63 and 4.37 kg
and lowered SBP by 5.14 mmHg (moderate certainty) and 5.05 mmHg (low certainty). Both clear the 3 mmHg
SBP difference Ge set in advance as the smallest worth having. **By 12 months the BP improvements had
almost completely disappeared**, as the weight loss against usual diet shrank to about 3 kg.
[@ge2020] Which programme a person picks barely matters for BP: no two diets differed by as much as that 3 mmHg
bar, so choose by preference and by what the person will stick to. -> [[Named Diet Programs Compared]]

Interventions that take off more weight also lowered BP. Tirzepatide, in two 52-week manufacturer-funded
trials of 469 people with obstructive sleep apnoea and obesity, cut body weight by 16-17% more than
placebo, and changed SBP by -7.6 mmHg (-10.5 to -4.8) and -3.7 mmHg (-6.8 to -0.7)
[@malhotra2024surmount]; neither trial had a
cardiovascular endpoint [@malhotra2024surmount].

**One weight-loss drug has counted fewer events, in a trial that enrolled only people with established
cardiovascular disease; primary prevention was not studied.** In SELECT, 17,604 adults aged 45 or over
with a BMI of 27 or more, established CVD (a previous heart attack or stroke, or symptomatic peripheral
arterial disease) and no history of diabetes took semaglutide or placebo. Semaglutide lowered SBP by 3.31
mmHg (-3.75 to -2.88) at two years and cut major CV events by
20% (HR 0.80, 0.72 to 0.90) over a mean 39.8 months of follow-up, and the effect appeared early after
treatment began [@lincoff2023].

The authors write: «Although our understanding of the mechanisms of cardiovascular protection with semaglutide remains speculative, the consistent effects on cardiometabolic risk factors support the hypothesis that clinical benefit is achieved through multiple interrelated pathways.»
[@lincoff2023] At the drug-trial
slope for people with previous CVD (0.89 per 5 mmHg), a 3.31 mmHg fall predicts about a 7% cut in events,
against the 20% observed, so the BP fall does not explain most of the benefit
[inferred from @bplttc2021].

In STEP-1, which enrolled adults with a BMI of 30 or more (27 or more
with a weight-related condition) and without diabetes, the same drug lowered SBP by 5.10 mmHg (-6.34 to -3.87) against placebo at 68 weeks, with no event endpoint
[@wilding2021].

**The drug's gains last only while it is taken.** After the drug and the lifestyle programme stopped at
week 68, «participants regained two-thirds of their prior weight loss, with similar changes in cardiometabolic variables»,
and «Cardiometabolic improvements seen from week 0 to week 68 with semaglutide reverted towards baseline at week 120 for most variables.»
In the semaglutide arm, observed mean SBP went from 129 mmHg at baseline to 121 at week 68 and 131 at week
120, against 130, 128 and 132 on placebo
[@wilding2022]. In SELECT, adverse events led 16.6% on
semaglutide to stop it permanently, against 8.2% on placebo
[@lincoff2023]
-> [[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]].

In DiRECT, a total-diet-replacement programme for recent type 2 diabetes stopped every antihypertensive
drug on day one. At 12 months the weight difference against controls was -8.8 kg (-10.3 to -7.3),
and 68% were still off antihypertensives with no rise in mean BP
[@lean2018]. At 24 months
their SBP was 3.43 mmHg lower than controls', with fewer on antihypertensives (47.3% vs 60.1%)
[@lean2019]. **None of the weight-loss trials here reports
how much BP falls per kilogram lost**, so there is no dose figure to plan with; the gaps section returns to this.
 -> [[Total Diet Replacement and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]]

**The largest lifestyle weight-loss trial found no fall in cardiovascular events.** Look AHEAD
randomised 5,145 overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes to an intensive lifestyle programme or
diabetes education. The programme improved fitness and every cardiovascular risk factor except LDL, at least initially.
Yet over a median 9.6 years the primary event rate was 18.3 vs 19.2 per 1000 person-years (HR 0.95, 0.83 to 1.09),
and the trial stopped early for futility.
[@look2013]

That interval still allows a reduction of up to about a sixth. The trial also tested a small dose: the
weight gap between arms shrank from 7.9 percentage points at one year to 2.5 at the end (6.0% vs 3.5% of
body weight) [@look2013], against a
diabetes support-and-education control. Ma's review of 54 RCTs in adults with obesity pooled CV events
from 24 of them (15,176 people): RR 0.93 (0.83 to 1.04). But Look AHEAD carries about 82% of that pool's weight, so the pool largely restates the same trial rather than confirming it
[@ma2017]. So the BP fall from weight loss is real, but no trial has shown that lifestyle weight loss cuts CV
events.

**Ma did find fewer deaths from any cause**: RR 0.82 (0.71 to 0.95) across 34 trials, rated high quality,
about six fewer deaths per 1000 (2 to 10) over a median trial length of two years, and 0.78 (0.63 to 0.96)
with Look AHEAD left out. CV deaths were not significantly reduced (RR 0.93, 0.67 to 1.31), and the
authors note that few trials reported these events
[@ma2017].

With CV events and CV deaths both non-significant, the trials cannot show whether this gain comes through fewer heart attacks and strokes. So it does not
show that the BP fall from weight loss prevents events. The trials, mostly of low-fat diets, also cannot
separate weight loss from what the diet contained.
Weight loss remains a priority for the outcomes it does move, such as deaths from any
cause, type 2 diabetes remission and fatty liver; the drug comparison below explains why a BP pill does
not substitute for it. -> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]

### Cutting heavy drinking lowers SBP in step with how much was drunk; light drinkers gain little

Roerecke's meta-analysis of 36 alcohol-reduction RCTs (2865 people) rates the dose-response as high
certainty, while noting that only three trials reported results for women
[@roerecke2017alcohol]. Overall, cutting alcohol
changed SBP by -3.13 mmHg (-3.93 to -2.32). **The fall grew with baseline intake**: -1.18 mmHg
(-2.32 to -0.04) at 3 drinks a day (a drink here is 12 g of alcohol), -3.00 (-3.98 to -2.03) at 4-5, and -5.50 (-6.70 to -4.30) at 6 or
more. Baseline intakes averaged 15, 30, 49 and 76 g/day across those bands, with single trials up to
about 380 g/day. [@roerecke2017alcohol]

**At 2 drinks a day or fewer, the trials showed no significant fall in SBP** (-0.18, -1.02 to 0.66). That band
rests on five trials whose alcohol arm averaged 15 g/day, and most of its weight comes from one 4-week
crossover in 24 women. Its interval still admits a fall of about 1 mmHg, so it shows no significant
fall, not a proven zero. [@roerecke2017alcohol]

Cohorts followed for longer suggest a small effect at light intakes. Di Federico pooled seven cohorts (19,548
adults, 4 to 12 years) and found SBP rose 1.25 mmHg (0.49 to 2.01) more at 12 g/day than with no
alcohol, with no threshold over 0 to about 55 g/day
[@difederico2023alcbp]. The trials
measured BP weeks to months after a change in intake; the cohorts measured how BP drifted over years by
starting intake, so the two do not clash. **For a light drinker, stopping is expected to lower SBP by
between zero and about 1.25 mmHg**, the upper figure holding only if the cohort gradient is causal.
[inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol; @difederico2023alcbp]
-> [[Does Light Drinking Raise Blood Pressure]]

Roerecke counted no cardiovascular events. Its projection of 678 UK cardiovascular deaths prevented a
year comes from a comparative risk-assessment model that assumes the lower drinking persists and acts
without lag [@roerecke2017alcohol].

At the drug-trial rate, a 5.50 mmHg fall in a heavy drinker would predict about 10% fewer major CV
events. For the light-drinking range the figure is between 0 and about 2% (up to about 4% at the cohort
interval's edge), and the non-zero figures hold only if the cohort gradient is causal. **Both figures are arithmetic
on the drug slope, not measurements**, and how many events they mean per 1000 people depends on the
drinker's baseline risk, which the risk section below works through.
[inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol]
For a light drinker, the stronger reasons to cut lie outside BP, in alcohol's cancer and vascular risk.
-> [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]

What people eat moves BP too, by similar amounts.

## Diet pattern and salt each lower BP a few mmHg; together they lower it by less than the sum

**A DASH-style diet and a sodium cut each lower SBP by roughly 3 to 5
mmHg; done together they lower it by less than the two numbers added.** Most figures in this section are
BP changes measured over weeks. Only the salt substitute has trials that counted strokes and deaths.


### DASH lowers SBP by about 3 to 5 mmHg against a typical diet

DASH replaces a typical Western diet with one higher in fruit, vegetables, low-fat dairy and whole grains,
and lower in red meat, sweets and saturated fat. Two pooled analyses give two different numbers, and this guide reports both.
Siervo pooled 20 RCTs (1,917 people, 2-24 weeks): SBP -5.2 mmHg (-7.0 to -3.4), DBP -2.6, with high
heterogeneity between trials (I2 76%) [@siervo2015].

Filippou pooled 30 RCTs (5,545 people, mean follow-up about 15 weeks, mean baseline 134/85 mmHg) on attained
BP: SBP -3.2 (-4.2 to -2.3), DBP -2.5, at moderate certainty, marked down for problems with blinding and
incomplete outcome data [@filippou2020dash].
**The two estimates are not two confirmations**: 15 trials are shared, the metrics differ, and none of the analyses this guide draws on separates the two causes of the gap. DASH probably lowers SBP somewhere in the 3 to 5 mmHg range.
[inferred from @filippou2020dash; @siervo2015]

The trials enrolled adults with above-optimal BP or grade-1 hypertension and a BMI of about 23 to 37, almost
all in the US or outside Europe. **The effect is not shown for a lean person with optimal BP**, and the
trials cannot say whether it lasts past about 15 weeks
[inferred from @siervo2015]
[@filippou2020dash].

DASH has no trial
that counted events. In cohorts, people with higher DASH-adherence scores had about a fifth lower incident
CVD (RR 0.80, 0.76 to 0.85), graded low certainty, the GRADE starting level for cohort evidence
[@chiavaroli2019]; a diet score
also tracks other healthy habits. Any benefit on events is therefore assumed from the drug-trial slope, with weak support from cohorts.

### Who gains more from DASH is unsettled

Siervo found larger falls in people with higher baseline BP or BMI, about 0.1 mmHg more per mmHg of baseline
[@siervo2015].
Filippou did not reproduce it: hypertensive and normotensive trials both came out at -3.9 mmHg (P 0.96)
[@filippou2020dash].

One signal leans toward Siervo. In untreated hypertensives SBP changed by -5.9 mmHg (-9.9 to -1.8), against -2.1 (-2.5 to -1.8) in
people already on BP drugs; the gap missed significance (P 0.07)
[@filippou2020dash].
**Neither between-trial analysis establishes that a higher baseline BP changes DASH's effect** (Filippou's larger falls with
higher sodium intake and younger age are also between-trial subgroups). So scale DASH's value by a person's overall CV
risk, and do not promise a bigger mmHg fall to someone because their reading is higher. DASH still lowers BP on top of drug treatment; the point estimates suggest, but do not show, that the
add-on is smaller.
[inferred from @filippou2020dash; @siervo2015]

### A Mediterranean diet lowers BP by about 3 mmHg against no change

Rees's Cochrane review found SBP -2.99 / DBP -2.0 mmHg against no or minimal intervention, at moderate
certainty but from only 2 RCTs and 269 people. Against another diet, the SBP difference shrank to an
imprecise -1.5 mmHg at low certainty [@rees2019medcochrane].
**Most of the BP gain comes from leaving a typical diet, not from choosing between healthy patterns.**


### Cutting sodium or adding potassium each lowers SBP by about 3.5 mmHg, more in hypertension

Cutting sodium lowers SBP by 3.39 mmHg (2.46 to 4.31) across 36 RCTs, at high certainty
[@who2012]. In He's 34 trials, which cut salt by about 75 mmol/day on average (about 4.4 g of salt), SBP fell by 5.39 mmHg (4.15 to 6.62) in people with hypertension [@he2013].

Raising potassium lowers SBP by 3.49 mmHg (1.82 to 5.15) at high certainty, but **the potassium effect shows up in people with hypertension** (5.32, 3.43 to 7.20) and is essentially
zero (0.09) in people without
[@aburto2013]
[@aburto2013]. Dose, salt sensitivity and the
population-versus-targeted question are worked through in [[Sodium]] and
[[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]].

### DASH plus a sodium cut beats either alone, but falls short of the sum

The DASH-Sodium trial (Sacks) fed 412 US adults with SBP 120-159 and no BP drugs all their food, holding
weight constant. It randomised them to DASH or a typical US control diet, then gave each person high,
intermediate and low sodium for 30 days each (urinary sodium about 142, 107 and 65 mmol/day)
[@sacks2001dashsodium]. DASH's
effect shrank as sodium fell: -5.9 mmHg (-8.0 to -3.7) at high sodium, -2.2 (-4.4 to -0.1) at low
(interaction P<0.001) [@sacks2001dashsodium].

**DASH plus low sodium lowered SBP by 8.9 mmHg (6.7 to 11.1) against the control diet at high sodium** —
the largest effect in the trial [@sacks2001dashsodium].
The two single effects, measured from the same start (DASH -5.9, sodium cut -6.7), would predict about -12.6
mmHg if they simply added; the trial observed -8.9. So the two effects are less than additive. Someone who has
already cut sodium to about 65 mmol/day should expect a smaller extra fall from DASH. Take the combined effect from the trial's -8.9, never from a
sum. [inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]

The overlap reaches beyond this one trial. DASH itself raises potassium intake
[@siervo2015], and some pooled
DASH trials added sodium restriction, weight loss or exercise
[@filippou2020dash], so **the DASH,
sodium, potassium and weight-loss numbers in this guide overlap and cannot be added up**. The trial's -11.5
(hypertensive) versus -7.1 (non-hypertensive) split is one unadjusted subgroup comparison, not an estimate to apply to either group. And 30-day
controlled feeding shows what the diet can do, not what people achieve on their own.
[inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]

### A salt substitute is the one diet change with event trials, and they come from China

The Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS, China) switched 20,995 high-risk adults, 72.6% with a prior
stroke, from regular salt to a 75% sodium chloride / 25% potassium chloride substitute. SBP fell 3.34 mmHg;
stroke fell (rate ratio 0.86), as did major CV events (0.87) and death (0.88), against a control death rate
of about 45 per 1,000 person-years [@neal2021].

The drug-trial slope predicts a stroke ratio of about 0.91 from that BP fall. The observed 0.86 (95% CI
0.77 to 0.96) [@neal2021] is larger, but
**the prediction sits inside the interval, so the excess may be chance**. If it is real, it would fit an
electrolyte effect beyond BP, since the substitute moves sodium and potassium together, or a sustained BP
fall larger than the one measured. [inferred from @neal2021]

Lai's network meta-analysis puts 25-40% potassium chloride substitutes at SBP -4.64 mmHg (-6.05 to -3.24;
18 trials, moderate certainty) [@lai2026salt],
but about 86% of the participants in that SBP estimate come from SSaSS, so it is not independent of that trial
.

Applying the effect to baseline risk, Lai puts the moderate-potassium (25-40% KCl) and low-sodium
substitutes at seven fewer deaths from any cause per 1,000 over 5 years at intermediate CV risk (a 5-10%
5-year CV event risk; moderate certainty). At high risk (20-30%; high certainty) the figure is 16 to 17
fewer. Lai says these benefits were «primarily supported» by
SSaSS [@lai2026salt].

Without SSaSS, the effect on deaths
from any cause lost significance, and no trial outside China reported mortality
[@lai2026salt].
**A salt substitute probably lowers BP and deaths in the Chinese adults studied**, mainly SSaSS's high-risk
rural adults, who add most of their salt at home. No trial has tested whether it does the same where most sodium arrives in processed food.
[inferred from @lai2026salt]

Brand's Cochrane review (26 RCTs) found hyperkalaemia RR 1.04 (0.46 to 2.38) and blood potassium +0.12
mmol/L (0.07 to 0.18), both at moderate certainty
[@brand2022lsss].

Brand notes that all the hyperkalaemia information in that meta-analysis came from two trials in people
at possible or unclear risk of hyperkalaemia, and that no study reported hyponatraemia, «a gap in the
evidence related to the safety of LSSS» [@brand2022lsss].
**Brand's hyperkalaemia estimate is closer to insufficient evidence than to a shown null.** About 92% of
its participants come from SSaSS, which did not measure serum potassium routinely, and its roughly 20 events come
mostly from one nursing-home trial at high risk of bias
[inferred from @brand2022lsss].

It is also not the same quantity as SSaSS's own hyperkalaemia rate ratio of 1.04 (0.80 to 1.37), which used
a different event definition: about 315 events, only 2 of them definite or probable and 302 of the possible
cases deaths [@neal2021].
Detail in [[Sodium]].

### The largest non-drug BP falls have never been tested on events

Among the non-drug options, **the bigger the BP fall, the weaker the evidence on events**. Garlic,
mindfulness programmes and yoga post the largest office SBP falls in the table, and none has been tested
on events. The one non-drug option with measured events, the salt substitute above, moves SBP only modestly. Drugs sit outside the pattern: they lower SBP a lot and are
validated on events, as the drug comparison below sets out. Read the pattern only on numbers where the people studied and the comparison groups match, because an
unblindable exercise or yoga trial and a placebo-controlled
garlic trial do not measure the same quantity. [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]


Three mind-body methods, each reviewed by a separate research group, follow the same pattern. **Each large
headline shrinks under a stricter test, and the test differs each time.** Yoga loses significance against
an active control and its SBP effect disappears on ABPM [@geiger2025yoga]. Mindfulness programmes stay significant against active controls (-8.78) but show no fall
in unmedicated patients, which Chen attributes to better pill-taking while noting only two trials enrolled
unmedicated patients [@chen2024mbi];
participants' expectations in unblinded trials could also explain it. Slow breathing's effect
falls to a non-significant -2.14 in the low-bias trials [@chaddha2019]. Expect a small, fragile effect from any of them.

### Garlic lowers BP in people with hypertension, but no trial has tested it on heart attacks or strokes

Two meta-analyses agree on about 8 mmHg in hypertensive adults, but **the agreement is not independent**:
the later pool re-uses the older trials, and four of its 12 are the earlier author's own. The normotensive
arms are near null, so the effect is concentrated in people who are already hypertensive. The trials tested
dosed supplements, dried powder or aged garlic extract, not garlic eaten as food. The newer review concludes:
«Given the abundant research that has investigated the efficacy of garlic in managing hypertension, further
studies are unnecessary.» [@ma2025garlic] That claim
cannot hold for cardiovascular outcomes no trial has measured. [[Garlic and Blood Pressure]]

### Nitrate, magnesium and urate lowering move BP only a little, or only in one group

Nitrate needs about 8 mmol a day to reach a 2 mmHg SBP fall (about 500 mg, roughly one concentrated
beetroot-juice shot). The slope is steeper in hypertensives (0.38 vs 0.28 mmHg per mmol in the
whole pool), a subgroup finding that hints hypertension changes the effect, not an established one
[@norouzzadeh2025nitrate]. **Ambulatory trials, including one in older, overweight
and obese adults with type 2 diabetes, did not confirm the fall** [@siervo2013nitrate]. For magnesium, a larger effect in magnesium-deficient people is plausible, but no significant interaction
has shown it. [[Dietary Nitrate and Blood Pressure]],
[[Dietary Magnesium and Cardiometabolic Outcomes]]

Allopurinol is a drug, not a lifestyle change, and its trials cannot tell whether the BP fall comes from
lower urate or from the enzyme the drug blocks [@qu2017urate]. The effect is shown only in hyperuricaemic patients. [[Uric Acid Lowering and Blood Pressure]]

### Fibre, grains, sweeteners, coffee and CPAP barely move BP

Fibre lowers SBP by a real but small amount; whole grains, sweeteners, coffee and CPAP show no meaningful
effect on it. **None moves BP enough to rank as a way of lowering it**; their value, where they have any, lies in other
outcomes. [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]

Most sweetener trials WHO reviewed lasted 3 months or less, while its cohorts link sweetener use to
incident hypertension (HR 1.13, 1.09-1.17, low certainty). WHO weighs reverse causation but does not think
it explains the whole link. So the evidence on long-term use is insufficient, not settled [@who2023nonsugar].
The fibre estimate in people with diabetes, -1.86 (-4.85 to 1.12), is too imprecise to call either way
[@reynolds2020].
[[Dietary Fibre and Health]], [[Whole Grains Refined Grains and Pulses]], [[Non-Sugar Sweeteners]],
[[Coffee Consumption and Health]], [[Sleep Apnea Treatment and Cardiovascular Risk]]

Time-restricted eating has been tested in two different forms. Eating early at a stable weight cut SBP by 11 mmHg in one
5-week trial of eight prediabetic men. **A 12-month trial of an early 8-hour window (8 am to 4 pm) found no BP difference**,
-0.3 (-3.7 to 3.1), when both arms ate the same reduced calories [@sutton2018] [@liu2022].
The designs differ, so the two results conflict without settling whether eating early matters.
[[Time-Restricted Eating]]

Next to a BP drug, which trials have validated on events, **these options add little through blood
pressure**. [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]


### People with normal BP drifting upward: cohorts link a few foods to hypertension, at low certainty

**For someone not yet hypertensive, the evidence this guide draws on for preventing hypertension is
observational and low certainty.** One research group's dose-response meta-analysis of cohorts found these associations with incident hypertension, per daily increment [@schwingshackl2017htn]:

| Food (increment per day) | RR for incident hypertension (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Whole grains (30 g) | 0.92 (0.87-0.98) |
| Fruit (100 g) | 0.97 (0.96-0.99) |
| Dairy (200 g) | 0.95 (0.94-0.97) |
| Red meat (100 g) | 1.14 (1.02-1.28) |
| Processed meat (50 g) | 1.12 (1.00-1.26) |
| Sugar-sweetened drinks (250 mL) | 1.07 (1.04-1.10) |

All six estimates are rated low certainty, and the curves for whole grains, fruit and processed meat are

of a food against eating less of it, not one food replacing another, and self-reported intake blurs every gradient toward no effect. The same group's analyses of other outcomes draw on these same cohorts, so agreement across outcomes is not independent
confirmation [[Food Groups and Health Outcomes - A Dose-Response Matrix]].

risk.

## Against a BP drug, lifestyle changes rank by a person's risk, and at low-to-intermediate risk neither prevents many events through BP

For most people the realistic alternative to a lifestyle change is a BP drug. This section compares the
two on the same terms: how far each lowers BP, how sure the trials are, what each costs, and how many
events each prevents in a given risk group -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

### Exercise against a drug: the gap closes when starting pressures are closer, but no trial tested them head to head

Naci's network meta-analysis put structured exercise and BP drugs in one network: 391 RCTs, 197 of
exercise (10,461 people) and 194 of drugs (29,281). «No RCTs compared directly exercise against
medications.» [@naci2019exercise] **Every
exercise-versus-drug number is therefore indirect**, inferred across separate trials. Across all trials,
drugs lowered SBP by 8.80 mmHg against control (CrI -9.58 to -8.02) and beat exercise by 3.96 mmHg (CrI
-5.02 to -2.91). But the exercise trials started at a mean SBP of 132 mmHg and the drug trials above 150,
and SBP may fall further from a higher start [@naci2019exercise].

When Naci restricted the exercise trials to people at >=140 mmHg, the gap
closed: 0.18 mmHg (CrI -1.35 to 1.68) [@naci2019exercise].
**With the exercise trials restricted to baselines of 140 or above, exercise and drugs lowered SBP by comparable amounts.** But the
restricted exercise evidence is thin, and it comes
from a literature whose sampled trials were mostly unblinded. And Naci counted no heart attacks or strokes, so it is an assumption that a BP fall from exercise prevents
events at the drug rate. One bias runs the
other way: Naci screened the drug trials for effective doses and did not screen the exercise trials
[@naci2019exercise]
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### A drug replaces only the part of a lifestyle change's benefit that runs through BP

A lifestyle change is worth what it adds over the best alternative a person could realistically reach,
and an effective BP drug is one such alternative. Three limits stop that rule from overstating what a drug
replaces
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]:

- **A drug replaces only the part of a benefit that runs through BP.** It shrinks the value of exercise, weight loss, salt, alcohol and
  diet pattern for BP-mediated outcomes only. Exercise keeps its effects on fitness, glycaemia and weight,
  and weight loss keeps its non-BP value, so taking a drug leaves those benefits intact
  ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]], [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]).
- **The drug's own costs count:** side effects, dependence on a daily pill, and leaving untouched whatever raised the BP in the first
  place.
- **Check, rather than assume, that the drug is mature and low-harm.** BP drugs pass that check on the
  relative effect on events. In low-risk grade 1 they are unproven on direct hard outcomes, and the tolerability cost
  was measured on old drugs (below).

In a high-risk group the drug captures the BP benefit, so it shrinks the BP value of lifestyle changes
most. At low risk it shrinks them less, **but that does not make the lifestyle changes large.** Their
absolute benefit is small for the same reason, low baseline risk. And a BP fall from diet or exercise
needs a second carry-over (a *transport*), from drug trials to diet or exercise, that the drug itself does
not need
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### Starting a drug in low-risk grade 1: Wang's trials are too small to settle it

Grade 1 here means untreated BP of 140-159 / 90-99 mmHg (ESC's grade 1, which the reviews call *mild
hypertension*), with no CVD, diabetes or CKD. Wang's Cochrane
review found five trials of starting a drug against placebo or no treatment in that group: 9124 people,
weighted mean age 50, mean follow-up 4.4 years, most excluding diabetes. Their control arms had 25 CV
events per 1000 over 2-5 years, roughly a fifth to a sixth of the rate in BPLTTC's no-CVD participants (part of the gap is
BPLTTC's broader composite).
Every outcome is low certainty
[@wang2025mildhtn]
.

| Outcome (2-5 y) | Control per 1000 | Drug per 1000 | RR (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-cause mortality | 21 | 18 | 0.85 (0.64-1.14) |
| Total CV events | 25 | 23 | 0.93 (0.69-1.24) |
| Stroke | 7 | 3 | 0.41 (0.20-0.84) |

**On deaths and CV events, Wang's trials give insufficient evidence, not evidence of no effect.** The CV-event
estimate rests on roughly 180 events, and its interval contains the effect BPLTTC's per-5-mmHg slope would
predict for any plausible BP difference. Trials this size could not have detected that effect. Wang does
not report how far BP fell in the drug arms compared with control, so its RRs cannot be put on BPLTTC's
per-mmHg scale -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

The one significant result, stroke, rests on 35 events, which Wang places mostly in one trial of people
with microalbuminuria on an ACE inhibitor
[@wang2025mildhtn].
Sundström's per-trial counts would instead put 30 of the 35 in the MRC trial. This guide does not have
Wang's own per-trial counts, so the mismatch is unresolved. **The stroke signal does not
drive any recommendation here.**

The harm is measured but low certainty: about 87 more withdrawals per 1000 over 5 years. It comes from one single-blind 1980s trial, MRC, whose whole population entered the estimate,
including people with prior CVD; many withdrawals were for headache, on drugs no longer first-line
[@wang2025mildhtn].
The figure plausibly overstates the withdrawals on current low-dose drugs, but that is this guide's
inference, not a measurement, and Wang treats the tolerability concern as still standing. **Symptom-driven withdrawals in a
single-blind trial mix drug effect with expectation**; only a comparator the participant cannot tell from
the drug separates the two -> [[The Nocebo Component of Drug Side-Effects]].

Carried over from BPLTTC, a 5 mmHg drop buys about 2 fewer CV events per 1000 over \~4.4 years (1.5 to
2.8 from BPLTTC's interval, baseline uncertainty not included). Neither Wang nor Sundström (for the
non-diabetic trials) reports how far BP fell in this group's trials. The one achieved figure in the grade-1
pool, about 3.6/2.4 mmHg, comes from Sundström's mostly diabetic half
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn].

At that fall the same carried-over slope gives about 1.6 fewer per 1000 (1.1 to 2.0). The \~9 mmHg that drugs
achieved in Naci's trials, which started above this BP band, is not this group's fall; it would give about 4. If the larger relative effect that Rahimi's BPLTTC
analysis by age found under 55 applies, a 5 mmHg drop gives about 4.5 (3 to 6) (the risk section below).
**This guide does not net benefit against harm:** a withdrawal is a reversible tolerability outcome, not
commensurate with a CV event
[inferred from @wang2025mildhtn; @bplttc2021]
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### The same trials inside a larger pool: Sundström's significant results come mostly from a half that was almost all diabetic

Sundström's BPLTTC analysis pooled 15,266 people without CVD enrolled at 140-159 / 90-99 mmHg, in 13
comparisons, reporting odds ratios over about 5 years. It contains Wang's review: 9117 of Wang's 9124
participants are in it. **The two reviews are one data set, never two confirmations.** On the matched
trials they agree closely: deaths 0.85 vs 0.85, coronary events 1.12 vs 1.12, CV events 0.93 vs 0.97
(Wang's RR against Sundström's OR for the three trials outside the BPLTTC collaboration)
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn]
[@wang2025mildhtn].

The whole pool is significant on deaths, OR 0.78 (0.67-0.92), and stroke, 0.72 (0.55-0.94), and
borderline on CV events, 0.86 (0.74-1.01)
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn].
**Those results come mostly from one half of the pool, which was 96% diabetic and more than half
already treated**: that half supplies 498 of the 665 deaths and 196 of the 226 strokes. The
other half, three trials that excluded diabetes and prior treatment, returned 0.97 (0.71-1.33) on CV
events (reported as coronary events and stroke; heart failure and CV death only in the BPLTTC trials)
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn].

The subgroup on no BP drug at baseline gave 0.84 (0.68-1.05), but 201 of its 366 CV events came
from untreated, almost all diabetic participants
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn]
.

Applied to the non-diabetic trials' own baseline (5-year risk 2.4% for CV events, 2.0% for deaths), the
pooled OR gives about 3 fewer CV events (0 to 6) and 4 fewer deaths (2 to 7) per 1000 over 5 years
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn]. **Those figures carry over a relative effect measured mostly in people with diabetes**, the same order as Wang's \~2 per 1000 above and not independent of it. On
those trials' own OR, the same baseline gives about 1 fewer CV event, anywhere from about 7 fewer to 8 more
 -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**For a person with grade-1 BP and diabetes, the diabetic half of the pool is the closer match, and there
the trials measured the benefit.** At its 7.4% 5-year CV risk, the authors' table gives about 10 fewer CV events (-1 to
19) and 14 fewer deaths (5 to 22) per 1000 over 5 years; the authors call such absolute point estimates
probably unreliable, with wide CIs
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn]. ADVANCE, a
modern-drug trial in that diabetic group, adds about 28 more withdrawals per 1000 over \~4.4 years, which
this section does not net against the benefit. For a non-diabetic person already on treatment, the pool offers no close match: at most about 25 of the 3505 already-treated
participants were non-diabetic.

### Wang wants direct trials; Sundström carries the pooled effect over

Wang and Sundström disagree on method, not on any estimate. Wang wants direct trials: «It is critical that we use data from high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to make
decisions around initiation of pharmacotherapy in people with mild hypertension without pre-existing
cardiovascular disease, rather than relying on extrapolations from indirect data.»
[@wang2025mildhtn] Sundström, like BPLTTC's 2021 pool, treats it as a reasonable assumption that the relative effect carries
over to grade-1 hypertension generally, and treats the absolute effect as uncertain
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn]. Neither reports a
different relative effect in the untreated, non-diabetic group, whose own intervals contain the pooled
estimates (0.86 inside 0.71-1.33 for CV events; 0.78 inside 0.62-1.15 for deaths). **So the two reviews differ on method; their estimates do not conflict**
-> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

Neither review takes an absolute line. Wang still suggests considering a drug case by case, and Sundström calls an
adequately powered trial in uncomplicated grade 1 an important addition
[@wang2025mildhtn]
[@sundstrom2015mildhtn]. By default this guide carries the relative effect over, labels that step a transport, and states this
group's own inconclusive trials beside it. For a costly or harm-bearing action, the low certainty of that
step should count in the decision. **That default is this guide's own method choice, open to revision, not
a verdict the sources reach.** Any of these would move it: an interval in this group that excludes the
pooled effect, a within-trial test showing the effect differs in this group, or an adequately powered
direct trial -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

### Intermediate risk, no CVD: HOPE-3's overall result is too imprecise to show or rule out a benefit

HOPE-3 is the only modern-drug trial at intermediate risk among this guide's sources. It randomised 12,705 people
without CVD (men >=55 or women >=65 with at least one risk factor, or women >=60 with two), not selected
for hypertension, to candesartan 16 mg plus hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg or placebo, double-blind. Mean age
was 65.7 and baseline BP 138.1/81.9 mmHg. The drug lowered BP by 6.0/3.0 mmHg over a median 5.6 years, and
the placebo arm had CV death, MI or stroke at 0.8% a year
[@lonn2016hope3].

The drug did not significantly cut that composite: HR 0.93 (0.79-1.10). Stroke was 0.80 (0.59-1.08),
death from any cause 0.98 (0.84-1.14) and new diabetes 1.07 (0.89-1.29)
[@lonn2016hope3]. **The trial's result is insufficient evidence, not proof of no effect.** BPLTTC's 0.91 per 5 mmHg, scaled to 6.0 mmHg, predicts about 0.89, inside the interval; at the
4.4% placebo risk that is about 5 fewer events per 1000 over 5.6 years. Wang's review describes HOPE-3 as
having demonstrated no benefit, which states it more firmly than the trial report does
[@wang2025mildhtn]
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3].

Total permanent discontinuation did not differ between arms (24.4% vs 25.2%). More people stopped the drug
for symptomatic hypotension, dizziness or light-headedness, 3.4% vs 2.0%; for renal or potassium problems the trial detected no excess (0.5% vs 0.3%)
[@lonn2016hope3]. That is about 14 more per 1000 over 5.6
years. **Everyone randomised had passed a 4-week active run-in**, which
also gave rosuvastatin, and 12,705 of 14,682 got through it, so in an unselected population the hypotension
excess may be larger
[@lonn2016hope3]. This figure and Wang's
+87 differ in measure, drugs, population and blinding, so this guide does not compare them.

HOPE-3 prespecified a test across thirds of baseline SBP, and it found a gradient. The thirds were cut at
131.5 and 143.5 mmHg (means 122, 137.6 and 154), with nearly the same BP drop in each; HRs were 1.16
(0.82-1.63), 1.08 (0.80-1.46) and 0.73 (0.56-0.94), P for trend 0.02, with no trend for stroke (P 0.22)
[@lonn2016hope3]. BPLTTC's 2021 pool found no
modification by baseline SBP (all adjusted interaction P >0.28, no subgroup HR above 1.0)
[@bplttc2021]. **The gradient is contested: a hypothesis, not an established difference in effect**
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

In the upper third, the two ways of reading the trial give different numbers. As a subgroup it had 6.5% events on placebo against
4.8% on the drug: about 17 fewer per 1000 (4 to 29) over 5.6 years. Applying the same relative effect to that 6.5% baseline risk, with no subgroup claim (baseline-risk
scaling), the \~0.89 gives about 7 fewer (5 to 8). In the lower two thirds the same scaling gives about 3-4
fewer per 1000 (2 to 5), while their own
subgroup intervals run from about 5-8 fewer to 17-18 more [inferred from @lonn2016hope3].

**Neither 131.5 nor 143.5 is a threshold the trial found**: they are the cut-points that split baseline
readings into thirds. Corrected for regression dilution, the thirds' usual SBP averaged 127.1 (lower) and 140.9 mmHg (upper), so a person whose multi-visit clinic
average is 130-140 cannot be placed in a third
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021].

So decide on absolute risk. At about 0.5-0.7% a year (crude CV death, MI or stroke), the drug buys little,
and trying non-drug changes first, or taking no BP drug, is a reasonable labelled default. Toward about 1.2% a year,
starting a drug is reasonable to consider. Those rates are very roughly 5-7% and 12% over 10 years if the
rate held constant, which understates how risk rises with age; a 10-year calculator's composite may
differ. At ages 50-69 a 5-7% ten-year risk falls in ESC's *treatment should be considered* band (the
risk section), so this default leans further toward waiting than ESC does. ESC's category, though, covers risk-factor
treatment in general, not BP drugs alone
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021].

**This guide prefers non-drug changes first because of harm, cost and non-BP value, not because a trial
measured an advantage.** For a person at the low end of risk whose baseline SBP is above the trial's upper
cut, the two readings of the trial disagree. The choice then turns on how much weight that person gives the
unconfirmed gradient
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021]
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### At low and intermediate risk, neither a drug nor a lifestyle change prevents many events through BP

**For people without CVD at low or intermediate risk, the benefit that runs through BP is small for every
option, the drug included.** Even at the favourable end of their intervals, the direct trials stay under
about 10 fewer events per 1000 over 5-6 years: HOPE-3's 0.79 on a 4.4% placebo risk (CV death, MI or stroke), and Sundström's
non-diabetic 0.71 at a 2.4% baseline (stroke and coronary events only).
Two results point higher and stay in view: Wang's low-certainty stroke result, and HOPE-3's upper-third subgroup. At its own 6.5% placebo
risk that subgroup estimate is about 17 fewer per 1000 (4 to 29), above that mark, but it is a nominal subgroup; baseline-risk scaling on the same risk gives about
7 (5 to 8). Carried to the 2.4% non-diabetic
baseline, the subgroup's 0.73 buys about 6-7 per 1000
-> [[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]].

These small numbers are a result, not a gap to fill with more effort. **A person in these groups can stop
chasing a heart-attack reduction the trials do not promise over their 5-6 years**, and for the benefit
that runs through BP, the choice between a drug and lifestyle is theirs. For adults under 50, the
lifetime-benefit view in the risk section is a separate question and can point the other way -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]],
[[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]].

A person's absolute risk sets this ranking; the next section covers what raises or lowers that risk.

## A millimetre of mercury is worth more at higher baseline risk, and age drives most of that risk

The drug comparison above turned on one input: a person's absolute cardiovascular risk. **That risk, more than
the BP reading itself, sets how many events a given fall in pressure prevents.** Age moves it most; smoking,
cholesterol, diabetes, kidney function and region move it too
[[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]. At the two ends of the age range, and in frail
people, the trials also leave the relative effect itself uncertain.

### The same relative cut buys more where events are commoner

**Absolute benefit is the relative reduction times the person's baseline risk**, so it varies between people
even when the relative effect does not. GRADE spells out what follows: a single relative effect can apply to
everyone while the recommendation still differs by baseline risk [@grade]. This guide calls that baseline-risk scaling. It needs only prognostic information. A claim that a drug
works *differently* in a subgroup needs positive interaction evidence instead, and such claims produce
most false positives [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

**Systolic pressure enters this product as part of the baseline risk, not as a modifier of the drug's
effect.** A higher
SBP raises the baseline, as the risk chart below shows. Within the pooled trials it did not change the
relative effect: inside every age band, the BPLTTC collaboration found no evidence that the benefit per 5 mmHg
varied with baseline SBP from below 120 to 170 mmHg or more (all adjusted p-interaction above 0.070)
[@rahimi2021bpage]. One trial found a signal the other way within its own data, in HOPE-3's no-CVD participants; the drug
comparison above and the section on how low to go below cover it.

### SCORE2 supplies the baseline, and its thresholds move with age

The ESC uses SCORE2 to estimate 10-year risk of fatal and non-fatal CVD, then sorts people into treatment categories whose cut-points rise with age [@esc2021]:

| ESC category | Under 50 | 50-69 | 70 and over |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-to-moderate: risk-factor treatment generally not recommended | <2.5% | <5% | <7.5% |
| High: treatment should be considered | 2.5 to <7.5% | 5 to <10% | 7.5 to <15% |
| Very high: treatment generally recommended | >=7.5% | >=10% | >=15% |

**The same 10-year risk can mean different things at different ages**: 8% is very high under 50 and high at 72 [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]. These are guidance cut-points for risk-factor treatment in general, not thresholds that BP trials
found.

**Two people of the same age and condition can land in different categories.** For a non-smoking man aged 60-64 in
the ESC's moderate-risk region, with non-HDL cholesterol of 4.0-5.9 mmol/L, the chart reads 10-11% at SBP
140-159 (very high) and 8-9% at SBP 120-139 (high); the same man as a smoker reads 15-17%
[@esc2021]. **For a smoker, smoking moves this baseline more than the BP band does.** Stopping smoking is outside
this guide, but on this chart smoking is the larger input to the same risk; that is a prognostic
difference, not a measured treatment effect. Region matters too:
the four regional charts differ substantially for identical inputs
[[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].

**The ESC charts apply only to people whose risk factors are untreated or have been stable for several
years**, and the ESC says to adjust the estimate upwards as a person approaches the next age band [@esc2021]. The base model also excludes
diabetes. SCORE2 is a prognostic tool: it says what a person's risk is, never what a treatment does to it
[[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].

### Under 50, the 10-year number understates the case

**A young person with high risk factors usually scores low over ten years but carries a high lifetime risk**,
and the ESC says so [@esc2021]. Its own rule allows treatment even from the lowest band, at every age, when the estimated lifetime
benefit is substantial;
the same logic raises the thresholds at 70 and over, where lifetime benefit is smaller
[@esc2021]. That provision is expert opinion
(class IIa, level C), not trial evidence [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

The ESC expresses lifetime benefit as years free of heart attack or stroke, a composite that also counts
death. It comes from a model (LIFE-CVD) multiplied by a trial relative effect, so no one has measured it; and
a longer horizon also means a longer treatment commitment
[@esc2021]. **Below 40 the ESC calls the predictions imprecise,
does not usually consider BP drugs except for specific BP disorders, and puts a lifelong healthy lifestyle
first** [@esc2021]
([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

### Age works through baseline risk from 55 to 84; at the ends the relative effect is uncertain

Rahimi and colleagues, for the BPLTTC collaboration, re-cut its trial data by age at entry: 51 trials, 358,707
people aged 21 to 105, with previous heart failure excluded
[@rahimi2021bpage]. Rahimi's
analysis comes from the same collaboration and largely the same trials as the main BPLTTC estimate, so it
refines that estimate by age; it is not an independent check. The bands mix people with and without prior CVD,
so none is a pure primary-prevention estimate [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]
.

| Age at entry | People | Major CV events, HR per 5 mmHg SBP (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| Under 55 | 42,960 | 0.82 (0.76-0.88) |
| 55-64 | 128,437 | 0.91 (0.88-0.95) |
| 65-74 | 128,506 | 0.91 (0.88-0.95) |
| 75-84 | 54,016 | 0.91 (0.87-0.96) |
| 85 and over | 4,788 | 0.99 (0.87-1.12) |

[@rahimi2021bpage]

**From 55 to 84 the relative effect is the same in every band, so higher risk at older ages means more events
prevented.** The authors found absolute reductions larger in older groups (adjusted p-interaction 0.024) but
limit those trial-derived figures to internal comparison; for a decision they say to combine the relative
effect with absolute risk from a current registry
[@rahimi2021bpage]. That is the
SCORE2 method above [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

**At both ends the estimate departs from 0.91, but one borderline test covers both** (adjusted p-interaction
0.050). The authors say a chance finding cannot be ruled out, and they also keep open a real attenuation with
age; among their candidate reasons, treatment in the oldest group lasted about half as long as in the youngest
[@rahimi2021bpage]. Under 55, the 0.82 suggests the
default may understate the benefit, which is why the drug comparison above gives a range when it carries the effect over to low-risk
people. That band is not primary prevention either: 30.8% had ischaemic heart disease at entry
[@rahimi2021bpage].

**At 85 and over, the trials cannot settle whether the benefit holds.** The band accrued about 1000 major CV events over a
median 2.8 years [@rahimi2021bpage]. The interval around its 0.99 runs from 0.87, a larger benefit than in the middle bands, to 1.12, a possible harm: compatible with the same
benefit, a smaller one or none. That is insufficient evidence, not evidence of no benefit. This guide keeps about 0.91 as its working default, with wider uncertainty, so starting a drug is
reasonable to consider, though less firmly than at 55-84 [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**In the oldest band, deaths from any cause and harms also stay unresolved.** For all-cause death the
authors found no obvious effect above 75, and they note that more competing non-cardiovascular deaths
dilute a proportional effect. They read the lack of excess deaths as suggesting that fatal harm from treatment is unlikely at any age. Falls, syncope
and tolerability are not pooled by age
[@rahimi2021bpage]. The authors add that, to their
knowledge, no randomised comparison suggests a fixed BP reduction does more harm than good in older people
[@rahimi2021bpage].

### Frail people were largely left out of the trials

**Frailty raises baseline risk, but for BP drugs the relative effect has to be carried over to frail people
rather than measured in them.** Frailty predicts worse outcomes; (pre)frail older adults have about 1.7 times
the odds of falls of robust ones (OR 1.70, 1.18-2.44)
[@vermeiren2016frailty]. The BPLTTC
authors state that people with heavy multimorbidity and polypharmacy, frail people and care-home residents
were under-represented, and that generalising to them remains uncertain
[@rahimi2021bpage]. The pool reports no adverse-event
outcome, so whether drug harms are larger in frail people is as untested as whether the benefit carries over
[[Frailty]].

**For a frail person, starting a drug is therefore an individual trade-off.** For someone already on one, the
authors give a default: «We acknowledge that clinical decisions cannot be deferred until such evidence
emerges. However, in the absence of any strong evidence for excess harms from randomised studies, we believe
that it is appropriate for patients who are on blood-pressure-lowering pharmacotherapy to continue receiving
such treatment if well-tolerated and when prevention of fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular events remains of
importance.» [@rahimi2021bpage] That default is the
authors' own, extrapolated to a group the trials under-represented, and it does not cover a drug that
is not well tolerated. On the efficacy trials, continuing has no better support than starting: no trial in this guide's sources
tests stopping at 85 or over
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### Diabetes and kidney disease raise baseline risk; this guide has no direct measure of salt sensitivity

**Diabetes needs its own risk calculator.** SCORE2-Diabetes adds HbA1c, age at diagnosis and eGFR to the base
model. For a 60-year-old non-smoking man in the moderate-risk region with SBP 140, it gives 11.0% with a
favourable diabetes profile (diagnosed at 60, HbA1c 50, eGFR 90) and 17.2% with an unfavourable one (diagnosed
at 50, HbA1c 70, eGFR 60) [@score2diabetes2023]. The mild-hypertension drug trials also accrued most of their events in people with diabetes; the drug
comparison above gives those figures and treats the diabetic half as the closer match for a person with
diabetes.

**Kidney function enters mainly as a risk input and as a contraindication.** Moderate or severe chronic
kidney disease puts a person straight into a risk category. ESC classes CKD without diabetes or ASCVD as
high risk when moderate and very high risk when severe (both defined by eGFR and albuminuria bands),
without a SCORE2 estimate [@esc2021]. With baseline-risk scaling, the same mmHg therefore buys more; that does not mean the drug works
differently in CKD ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]).

Lower eGFR raises estimated risk in SCORE2-Diabetes (SHR 0.94 per SD of ln eGFR, so risk rises as eGFR falls)
[@score2diabetes2023], and chronic kidney disease ran at 16-19% in every BPLTTC
age band [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. Kidney disease is one of the conditions in which a
potassium salt substitute is contraindicated, as the callout in the diet and salt section warns. Managing BP
inside diagnosed kidney disease is outside this guide. This guide has no direct measure of salt sensitivity; the split between people with and without
hypertension is the closest proxy, covered in [[Sodium]] and
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

Risk sets how much a fall in BP buys; the last question is how low BP can go and still pay off.

## In high-risk trials a lower target prevented more events, and the pooled randomised data do not reproduce the observational J-curve

**In
high-risk people, a systolic target below 120 mmHg prevented more events and deaths than a target below
140, and caused more serious side effects.** The worry that pushing BP too low raises risk comes mainly from observational curves, and **the pooled
randomised data do not reproduce it**. One group stays open: people without CVD in the lower two thirds of one trial's baseline systolic pressure (143.5 mmHg or less), where that
trial points the other way [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

### SPRINT: a lower target bought fewer events in high-risk people, at a cost

SPRINT randomised 9,361 high-risk hypertensive adults without diabetes to a systolic target below 120 mmHg
(intensive) or below 140 mmHg (standard). Achieved pressures were about 121 and 136, and the trial stopped
early at a median 3.26 years. **Intensive treatment cut the primary composite (heart attack, acute coronary
syndrome, stroke, heart failure, CV death) by about a quarter**, HR 0.75 (0.64-0.89), and all-cause death
by a similar share, HR 0.73 (0.60-0.90). The trial reports 61 people treated to prevent one primary event
over 3.26 years [@sprint2015]. That is about
16 fewer primary events per 1,000 people over 3.26 years, in
a population whose standard arm had about 2.2% primary events a year, as HOPE-3's authors give it
[@lonn2016hope3].

**SPRINT behaves as the drug slope predicts.** Its achieved gap of about 15 mmHg, at about 0.91 per 5
mmHg, predicts a composite HR near 0.75, which is what SPRINT observed
. This guide's sources do not settle whether SPRINT is an independent check: BPLTTC pooled studies
published between 1972 and 2013; SPRINT was published in 2015 [@sprint2015]; and SPRINT's investigators sit among BPLTTC's collaborators
[@bplttc2021].

The trial compared two targets and identified no optimum between or below them
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Its authors name the groups it does not cover:
«The lack of generalizability to populations not included in the study — such as persons with diabetes, those with prior stroke, and those younger than 50 years of age — is a limitation.»
[@sprint2015] It also did not enrol older
adults living in nursing homes or assisted-living facilities
[@sprint2015].

**The lower target also raised serious harms.** Intensive treatment increased serious hypotension,
fainting (syncope), electrolyte abnormalities, and acute kidney injury or failure, though not injurious
falls [@sprint2015]. This guide lists those harms by type, not as rates per 1,000, so it does not net them against the benefit. **Whether about 16
fewer events per 1,000 is worth those risks is the person's call**, and the trade applies to people at
SPRINT's level of risk, not to everyone with a raised reading
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

### The pooled randomised data do not reproduce the registry J-curve

A registry of people with ischaemic heart disease showed a J-shaped association between BP and events,
with risk lowest around 130/75 mmHg, which suggested that lowering BP below that level could raise risk
[@bplttc2021]. BPLTTC
notes that the concern was not confined to observational data: a randomised trial and a tabular
meta-analysis it cites found no benefit below 140 mmHg in people without CVD. BPLTTC attributes the conflicting reports partly
to analyses built on aggregate trial data
[@bplttc2021].
This guide does not have that meta-analysis.
**Randomised treatment shows no such rise.** The BPLTTC pooled analysis found the proportional benefit of
lowering similar across categories of baseline systolic pressure «down to less than 120 mm Hg»
[@bplttc2021].

**So the J-curve's low arm is not a treatment effect.**
But the per-stratum HRs below 130 appear only in a figure image this guide has not transcribed, so the
guide cannot give the lowest stratum's confidence interval
 ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

The general rule comes from [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]: a U- or J-shaped observational curve is
not evidence for its low arm until that arm survives a stronger check, and a randomised trial is the
strongest one. **Illness can lower blood pressure before it shows up as an event or a diagnosis**, as in
the years before dementia, so low readings can look dangerous without causing harm. Rahimi, re-analysing the BPLTTC trials by age, reads the observational worry about low BP
and cognitive decline in older people as likely reverse causation, not substantiated in any randomised
trial [@rahimi2021bpage].

Below age 85, Rahimi's check within each age band (the risk section above) found no randomised support
for harm at low BP. At 85 and over only 121 people started below 120
mmHg [@rahimi2021bpage], so harm at low BP is
untested there for want of power, not refuted (the risk section above).

### Still contested: people without CVD and a lower baseline pressure

HOPE-3, the intermediate-risk trial in the drug comparison above, is not in the BPLTTC pool. It found a
nominally significant trend across thirds of baseline systolic pressure, and its authors read this as support for a J-curve for major CV events
other than stroke [@lonn2016hope3]. **That gradient measures something different from the registry J-curve**: how the treatment effect
varies within one randomised trial, not how risk varies with untreated pressure
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

It does not overturn the pooled finding either. The lowest-third interval (drug comparison above) spans
benefit and harm, stroke
showed no trend, and the BPLTTC test found no modification in far more people (14,928 without CVD below
130 mmHg, against 4,202 in HOPE-3's lowest third). But BPLTTC's no-CVD population ran about four times
HOPE-3's risk (on a broader composite that includes heart failure). Its matched per-stratum estimates also sit in a
figure image this guide has not transcribed, so the two cannot be set side by side
estimate for estimate
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021].

**This guide keeps its default, a constant relative effect with absolute benefit scaled by baseline risk.
For people without CVD in HOPE-3's lower two thirds the question is contested, not settled: the trial's signal
there is not refuted**.

Two things would decide the question. One is BPLTTC's own HRs for people without CVD below 130 mmHg, with
that group's make-up. The other is a low-comorbidity no-CVD group from pooled patient-level data, with its
own estimate ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

### Lowering BP also cuts dementia; within the trials, risk tracked achieved BP down to 100/70

Peters pooled patient-level data from five double-blind placebo-controlled BP-drug trials (28,008 people,
861 dementia cases). **Treatment lowered the odds of dementia**, OR 0.87 (0.75-0.99), with an average fall
of 10/4 mmHg; dementia occurred in 2.9% on active treatment and 3.3% on placebo (median follow-up 4.3
years) [@peters2022bp].
That is about 4 fewer cases per 1,000 people over that median, and it likely understates the effect because the trials stopped early on their CV endpoint
while dementia accrues more slowly [inferred from @peters2022bp].

**Dementia is a second outcome from the same evidence, not independent confirmation**: the five trials
sit inside the BPLTTC base, so Peters extends the same evidence to a new endpoint
[inferred from @peters2022bp; @bplttc2021].

Observational studies show a U-shaped link between BP and dementia in older age; the trials show none at
any age. Plotting achieved BP at one year (an achieved-BP analysis, not a randomised comparison), Peters
found «a linear relationship between lower risk of
dementia and lower BP, down to at least 100 mmHg systolic and 70 mmHg diastolic»
[@peters2022bp]. **100/70 is the floor of what was
studied, not evidence about pressures below it** ([[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]).

The preDIVA trial of nurse-led vascular care in 3,526 unselected people aged 70-78 lowered systolic pressure by 2.06 mmHg over 6.7 years. Its dementia HR of 0.92 (0.71-1.19) leaves the
evidence insufficient either way. It detected no cognitive harm, but that is a single-trial null, not
shown safety
[@mollvancharante2016].

The 2024 Lancet Commission turns BP lowering into a target: «Aim to prevent hypertension and maintain systolic BP of 130 mm
Hg or less from age 40 years.» [@livingston2024] That is a
guidance number for dementia prevention, not a cut-point the trials found
([[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]]).

### A clinic reading and a trial reading are different numbers

**White-coat hypertension is common**: ESC puts it at up to 30-40% of patients, with a risk lower than
sustained hypertension but possibly higher than normal pressure
[@esc2021]. ESC also gives SPRINT's intensive-arm mean as
124 mmHg and says it probably reflects a conventional office systolic range of 130-139 mmHg
[@esc2021]; the SPRINT report itself gives 121.4 mmHg at
one year [@sprint2015].

**SPRINT's below-120 target therefore does not carry over to a routine clinic cuff reading**; the shift is
roughly 6-15 mmHg, as large as or larger than the whole effect of cutting salt
. The trial is not wrong; its number simply belongs to the way it measured BP. Before comparing a personal reading with any target in this section, check that both come from the same method
[[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]. The supplements section above flags which supplement and mind-body effects shrink on ambulatory
monitoring (ABPM).

Together, these estimates give a short action list for each group.

## What to do: make the large non-drug changes first, then decide on a drug by absolute risk

**Almost everyone with raised or rising blood pressure should make the same non-drug changes. Whether
to add a drug differs from person to person, and absolute cardiovascular risk should decide it, not the
reading.** Each action below states what it rests on. A
*labelled default* rests on practice and guidance, not on a trial. Cheap, reversible, low-harm actions get
a plain do-this even where their benefit on events is borrowed from the drug trials, because getting them
wrong costs little. This guide states drug decisions only as firmly as the absolute benefit and the harm
data allow [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

| Who | Non-drug changes | BP drug | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| High absolute risk or established CVD | yes, on top of the drug | yes: the main option | drug: measured on events |
| Untreated grade 1, low risk | 3-6 month trial first | a reasonable choice either way | labelled default, not trial-tested |
| Intermediate risk, no CVD, SBP \~130-145 | yes | decide on absolute risk | baseline-risk scaling; SBP gradient unconfirmed |
| Grade 1 with diabetes | yes | closer to measured benefit | measured in a mostly diabetic pool |
| 85 and over, trial-eligible | yes | reasonable to consider, less firmly | band's own estimate insufficient |
| Frail, multimorbid, in care | as tolerated | starting: individual; continuing: if well tolerated | authors' default, extrapolated |
| Normal BP, drifting up | yes | no | small BP gains; non-BP value |

### Cheap, low-harm swaps come first

**Start with exercise, drinking less if you have more than two drinks a day, a DASH-style diet and less salt where intake is high; all four are cheap and low-harm.** Turn some inactive time into movement, and some of it
into structured exercise: endurance, resistance or both, with the combination ahead in the BP trials (the exercise, weight and
alcohol section has the sizes). Pick the type you will keep doing and are least likely to get hurt
doing, because the BP differences between types are small and an abandoned plan buys nothing
[[Is the Exercise Modality Doing Any Work]]. The BP trials in this guide give no dose to plan around.

For a starting dose, cohorts of adults aged 40 and over with activity measured by accelerometer found the
largest mortality gain in the first step up from the least active, at every intensity. In separate analyses by
intensity, about 60 more minutes a day of light activity separated the least active quarter from the next
(hazard ratio 0.60, 0.54 to 0.68), and about 5 more minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity did the same
(0.64, 0.55 to 0.74). So movement at any intensity counts, not only exercise. Further gains flattened, and wide intervals at the top of the
curve leave the flattening point imprecise
[@ekelundacc2019]. This guide reads the flat
region as roughly 20 to 40 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity, a labelled default from observational mortality data, not a BP-trial dose
([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]).

If you drink more than two drinks a day (a drink here is 12 g of alcohol), cut down. Randomised trials show that BP falls, and falls more the more you drank (high certainty); the
largest fall came in heavy drinkers who roughly halved their intake
[@roerecke2017alcohol]
[@roerecke2017alcohol].

Eat a DASH-style diet in place of a typical Western one; it lowers BP with or without a drug
[@chiavaroli2019]. Where salt intake is high, cut
it, toward WHO's target of under 5 g of salt (2 g of sodium) a day
[@who2012]; the average cut in He's trials was about
4.4 g of salt a day [@he2013]. The target, and who gains most, are worked through in [[Sodium]].

**Two drinks a day is not a safe ceiling.** Below it, the case for drinking less comes from other outcomes.
The cancer risk curve rises from zero intake, and the protective arm of the mortality J-curve is largely an artifact
 -> [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]], [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

**Switch home-added salt to a potassium-enriched substitute (25-40% potassium chloride) only after
checking everyone in the household.** Do not use it if anyone who eats the food has kidney disease or takes a
potassium-sparing diuretic or a potassium supplement. If anyone in the household has diabetes or takes an
ACE inhibitor or ARB, ask a clinician before switching.

**Lose weight if you are overweight, for more than your BP.** Weight loss lowers BP, but the largest
lifestyle weight-loss trial did not cut cardiovascular events. Pursue it for the outcomes it does move:
fewer deaths from any cause in the pooled trials, glucose control, diabetes prevention and remission,
fatty liver, function
[[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]. Named diet programmes barely differ on
weight or BP, and their gains shrink by 12 months, so choose the one you can sustain
[inferred from @ge2020].

**Expect the combination to deliver less than the sum.** Plan on the measured combined figure in the
diet and salt section, never a total added up change by change
[inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium].

### The drug decision differs by risk group

**High absolute risk or established CVD: take the drug, and do the list as well.** Here drug-induced lowering is the main option, and trials measured it on events at every starting
pressure studied. Lifestyle changes add to the drug, though the diet trials hint that the extra fall is smaller in people
already treated. A lower target, as in SPRINT, prevents more events in high-risk people but raises serious
harms [@sprint2015]. The section on how low to
go gives both sides.

**Untreated grade 1 (140-159 / 90-99), no CVD, diabetes or kidney disease: a three-to-six-month non-drug
trial first, then reassess.** This is a labelled default. No trial compared starting a drug now with
starting it after such a trial. At this baseline a few months' delay gives up very little expected benefit
and avoids the drug's tolerability cost
[inferred from @wang2025mildhtn; @bplttc2021].
Wang reports guidance split on it: «Some guidelines suggest a three- to six-month trial of
non-pharmacologic intervention prior to initiation of pharmacotherapy [18], while others suggest non-
pharmacotherapeutic intervention only [19, 20].»
[@wang2025mildhtn]. Other guidance Wang cites
sets a low threshold for starting a drug
[@wang2025mildhtn].

**In this group neither a drug nor lifestyle change prevents many heart attacks and strokes over the years
the trials ran, and that is itself a result.** At this low baseline risk the drug benefit carried over from
the pooled trials is small (the drug comparison above), and a fall from lifestyle change needs a second
carry-over. Starting a drug after the trial, or instead of it, is a
reasonable individual choice; Wang's review suggests deciding on an individual basis
[@wang2025mildhtn].

**Intermediate risk, no CVD, SBP around 130-145: do the list, and decide on the drug by absolute risk.** Use
the two anchors in the drug comparison: little for the drug at the low end of that risk range, reasonable
to consider toward the high end. HOPE-3's SBP-third gradient is an unconfirmed hypothesis, and its thirds
cannot place someone in the 130s or low 140s reliably, so the case rests on absolute risk
[inferred from @lonn2016hope3; @bplttc2021].

**Grade 1 with diabetes: here the trials measured the benefit, rather than only carrying it over.**
The closer match is the mostly diabetic half of the BPLTTC grade-1 pool, where the benefit was
measured, though its authors call the point estimates unreliable (figures in the drug comparison). For an
already-treated person with diabetes, the question is adding a drug, not starting one
[inferred from @sundstrom2015mildhtn; @wang2025mildhtn].

**Aged 85 and over, trial-eligible: starting a drug is reasonable to consider, though less firmly than at 55-84.** The band's own estimate can neither confirm nor exclude the benefit. **Frail, multimorbid or
living in care: starting is an individual trade-off.** The trials under-represented these people, and the
age analysis pooled no harm outcomes. For someone already on a drug that suits them, the authors' default
is to continue it while preventing cardiovascular events still matters
[@rahimi2021bpage]
[inferred from @rahimi2021bpage; @bplttc2021]
[[Frailty]].

**Normal BP, drifting up: do the list, and expect small BP gains.** At a normal reading, lowering BP offers a medium-to-small gain [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]. Exercise lowers
SBP less than in people with hypertension (the exercise, weight and alcohol section). Extra potassium did
not lower an already-normal BP in relatively short trials, which did not test whether it prevents a later
rise [@aburto2013]. For this group, the stronger reasons to move more and keep weight down lie outside BP [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

### Supplements and mind-body methods are optional extras

**Garlic, nitrate, magnesium, mindfulness, yoga and slow breathing are optional adjuncts, never
substitutes for a drug that a person's risk warrants.** Chen's review frames
mindfulness as a supplement to BP drugs, not a replacement
[inferred from @chen2024mbi]
[[Stress Management and Cardiometabolic Health]]. For someone already on a drug, garlic adds little BP lowering; the case for it is stronger only for
someone on no drug [[Garlic and Blood Pressure]].

### The weighting is yours

This list says what each change does and how firmly the evidence supports it. **How much to value a
stroke avoided against a dementia risk, a side effect, a daily pill or a change of habit is the person's
judgement** ([[Rating Outcome Importance]]). No figure here supplies it. Several of these answers stop where the evidence stops; the last
section names where.

## The trials leave questions open, and that limits how firm this guide can be

For each gap below, the trials this guide draws on did not measure the thing, so nothing here says which way it would come out. **A gap is not a finding of
no effect, and not a hint of one.** Each entry names the study that would close it
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

### Whether a lifestyle BP fall prevents heart attacks and strokes

**Event trials of lifestyle changes, and salt-substitute mortality outside China.** Exercise, a DASH-style diet and cutting heavy
drinking each lower BP in trials, but no trial of them in this guide counts events
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).
An event-powered trial of any of these changes, or a salt-substitute trial reporting mortality outside
China, would close it.

**Weight loss per kilogram.** **This guide has no meta-analysis of how far SBP falls per kilogram lost**, only indirect figures from diet programmes, an obesity drug and a diabetes-remission diet
([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]], [[Named Diet Programs Compared]]). A dose-response
meta-analysis of SBP change on weight change would let weight loss be sized like the other options
.

**Exercise against a drug, head to head.** Naci's network meta-analysis placed exercise and BP drugs in
one network, but **no trial randomised people between them**; every comparison is indirect
[@naci2019exercise], and the authors call the linking
assumption tenuous [@naci2019exercise]. A trial
randomising hypertensive people to structured exercise or a first-line drug would settle the comparison
directly ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

**Ambulatory confirmation of large office effects.** The beetroot-juice BP effect was not confirmed on 24-hour
ambulatory monitoring (ABPM), including in older, overweight and obese people with type 2 diabetes
[@siervo2013nitrate].
Garlic has office readings only, with no ambulatory pool ([[Garlic and Blood Pressure]]). A long-term
nitrate trial with ambulatory BP in hypertensive or higher-risk people would close the first
([[Dietary Nitrate and Blood Pressure]]); a garlic trial with ABPM would close the second.

### Whether to start a drug at low or intermediate risk

**An adequately powered trial in uncomplicated grade 1.** The direct trials of starting a drug in
untreated, low-risk grade-1 hypertension are few, old and too small to confirm or exclude the benefit carried over from the pooled drug trials ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Sundström
named the trial that would settle it, and doubted it would happen:

> «A deﬁnitive, adequately powered, large-scale trial among patients with uncomplicated grade 1 hyperten- sion would be an important addition to the evidence base, but the likelihood that such a trial will be done seems small.»

[@sundstrom2015mildhtn]

**The pooled effect below 130 mmHg without heart disease.** BPLTTC's per-stratum hazard ratios for people
without CVD at baseline SBP under 130 mmHg exist only as an image this guide has not transcribed. **Without
them, HOPE-3's lower SBP thirds cannot be compared with the pooled trials stratum by stratum**, so that group stays contested ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Extracting those
per-stratum figures would test it.

### Who the trials left out

**Hyperkalaemia where the salt substitute is contraindicated.** SSaSS excluded people with these conditions, and the trials in this guide do not measure the harm in
them (the callout in the diet and salt section);
a study measuring serum potassium in them would close it ([[Sodium]]).

**Drug harms by age, and the frail.** Rahimi's age analysis of the drug trials does not pool falls,
syncope or tolerability by age. **For a frail person the benefit is carried over from other groups, and the harm is unmeasured.** The authors name ATEMPT, a trial in multimorbid,
polymedicated people, as the test; this guide has no result from it ([[Frailty]]).

### Thresholds, lifetime numbers and the shape of decline

**The guideline threshold.** This guide has no guideline's own reasoning for diagnosing hypertension at 130/80 rather than 140/90, so
it cannot say which objective drove either choice. A method for reading that reasoning already exists: an evidence-to-decision table names each non-evidence consideration a body weighed
([[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]]). The gap would close if someone read the threshold-setting bodies' decision tables with that method.

**Lifetime benefit of a lower SBP.** ESC publishes a grid of CVD-free years gained from a 10 mmHg SBP
reduction, by age, sex, smoking, SBP and non-HDL cholesterol. This guide has not transcribed its cells,
because a misprint in the published 45-49 systolic row labels leaves row alignment uncertain; the grids need a coordinate-based extraction [@esc2021]. Until
then, a younger adult's lifetime number for BP lowering is available only in words
([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

**Function, quality of life and cognitive trajectory.** The drug trials count events. Wang's and
Sundström's reviews report no quality-of-life outcome
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Peters' trials cut dementia diagnoses but
showed no effect on cognitive decline
[@peters2022bp]. The authors qualify that null: the
MMSE is insensitive to mild impairment, though SPRINT-MIND's fuller neuropsychological scores also showed
no difference, and they call for more sensitive measures
[@peters2022bp]
([[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]]).

**The trials this guide draws on do not measure how BP lowering shapes the years of decline, as opposed to
their end-point.** Rahimi cites other age-stratified analyses reporting no worsening of function or
quality of life in older people, which this guide does not have [@rahimi2021bpage]. Trials reporting function, quality of life and
cognitive trajectories beside events would close it.
