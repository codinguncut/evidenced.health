**Verdict up front: mostly no.** Time-restricted eating (TRE), one-meal-a-day (OMAD),
intermittent fasting and skipping breakfast are, for the most part, **a convenient delivery
system for eating less** — not a metabolic trick that works through the clock. When the honest
test is run — the one small *calorie-matched* trial (eTRF, n=8) shows only surrogate effects, and the weight null
comes from the free-living TREAT trial — the timing advantage does not survive as a weight lever. There is **one** narrow exception worth
separating out: eating **earlier** in the day may improve glucose handling and blood pressure a
little, independent of weight. Everything else that "works" about meal timing works because a
shorter eating window makes you eat less and is easy to remember.

This is a general appraisal of the population evidence, not personal advice, and the evidence
base held here is **thin** — the two anchor trials are small (one has just 8 participants). Treat
the confidence as **low** throughout.

## The one question that decides it: beyond the deficit, or the deficit in a costume?

Meal timing almost always causes some weight loss, because a shorter window cuts intake. So the
only test that means anything is: **when calories are held equal, does timing still win?** Ask it
of every claim below. This is the same pattern the wiki finds across nutrition — energy is the
headline; the clever protocol is usually the small print ([[Free Sugars Intake]]).

## Sub-question 1 — Weight: does TRE beat a calorie-matched control?

**Verdict: no advantage from the window itself, and a hidden cost.** In the one
adequately-powered free-living trial — TREAT, n=116, a self-selected 16:8 window (eat noon-8pm,
skip breakfast) with *nothing else changed* — TRE produced **no weight advantage** over normal
three-meal eating: a between-group difference of «-0.26 kg» with «P = .63»
[@lowe2020]. Adherence was good; the timing
simply did not do anything on its own.

Worse, the modest weight it did shed was **disproportionately muscle**: «approximately 65% ...
lean mass» versus the normal 20-30% for weight loss, with a significant between-group deficit in
appendicular lean mass [@lowe2020]. The
likely mechanism is that a short, unplanned window quietly cuts **protein**, which is eaten mostly
at meals. So a late/short window can trade the fat you wanted to lose for muscle you did not.

## Sub-question 2 — Glycaemic control: the one place timing may be real

**Verdict: a genuine but tiny weight-independent signal — from eating EARLY, not from the window
length.** eTRF (Sutton 2018) is the only trial here that isolates timing from calories. It fed 8
overweight men with prediabetes enough to hold their weight steady, meal-by-meal matched, and
compared a 6-hour **early** window (dinner before 3pm) against a 12-hour window over 5 weeks each.
With **zero weight change** («body weight ... approximately stable») it found:

- mean insulin **-26 mU/L** lower, and insulin resistance **-36 U/mg** (p=.005)
- systolic/diastolic blood pressure **-11 / -10 mm Hg** (p=.03)
- improved beta-cell responsiveness and lower oxidative stress
- fasting **glucose itself was null** — the primary endpoint did not move

[@sutton2018]

If real, this is a true circadian effect: metabolism is better aligned to food earlier in the
day, and eating late is the worse pattern. But the caveats are severe. It is **n=8**, men only,
prediabetic, 5 weeks, and confounded — the early arm fasted 18 hours before testing versus 12 in
the control, so some of the "benefit" may be the longer overnight fast, not the clock position.
And **every outcome moved is a surrogate** — insulin, blood pressure and oxidative stress are
markers, with no hard outcome and no weight change attached ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).

## Sub-questions 3 and 4 — Appetite and sleep: named gaps, not findings

**Verdict: insufficient evidence held.** The wiki does not hold a trial that cleanly answers
either. On **appetite**, reports are mixed — some people feel less hunger on a short window, some
more — and any effect is tangled with adherence. On **sleep**, the plausible story is that large
or late meals near bedtime disrupt sleep, so a window that shifts eating earlier might help, but
this is a mechanism, not a measured outcome here. Both are recorded as **insufficient-evidence
gaps**: unstudied in this corpus, which is not the same as "no effect." Do not credit meal timing
with an appetite or sleep benefit on this evidence.

## Sub-question 5 — Is skipping breakfast a "free lunch"?

**Verdict: not free, not magic — just calorie-timing that works only if you do not make it up
later.** No dedicated breakfast-skipping RCT is held; the closest evidence is TREAT, whose 16:8
window *is* breakfast-skipping, and it bought no weight advantage. Skipping breakfast is a fine
adherence tool for **some** people — one simple rule, fewer eating occasions — but its benefit
rides entirely on **eating less overall**. If you compensate at lunch and dinner, the deficit
closes and nothing is gained. The folk claim that "breakfast is the most important meal" is itself
weakly evidenced; skipping it is neither a metabolic trick nor a guaranteed win.

## Why the deficit keeps escaping — the compensation parallel

Meal timing "works" by creating a calorie deficit, and the body is good at quietly refilling
deficits. The clearest worked case is exercise ([[Exercise Energy Compensation]]): across 61
studies (928 subjects), «the overall mean energy compensation was 18% +/- 93%»
[@riou2015] — and it **grows over time**, so
that «for studies of longer duration (about 80 weeks), the energy compensation approached 84%»
[@riou2015]. A doubly-labelled-water study of
1,754 adults found compensation «averages 28% due to reduced BEE» — a measurable drop in basal
metabolism — «so ... only 72% of the extra calories we burn ... translates into extra calories
burned that day», rising from «27.7%» at the leanest to «49.2%» at the heaviest
[@careau2021].

The lesson transfers: a naive intervention that opens an energy gap is partly offset by
behaviour and metabolism, and **the offset is largest in the person who most wants the effect**.
A meal-timing protocol is no more immune to compensation than exercise is.

## The bigger frame — weight loss is the lever, and even it is bounded

If meal timing's value is that it delivers weight loss, it is worth knowing what weight loss
itself buys. The honest picture:

- **On hard cardiovascular events, lifestyle weight loss underdelivers.** Look AHEAD (5,145
  adults with type 2 diabetes) lost more weight in the intensive arm yet hit its CV endpoint at
  «hazard ratio 0.95; 95% CI 0.83 to 1.09» — no reduction
  [@look2013]. A 54-trial
  meta-analysis (30,206 adults) confirms it: CV events «risk ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.04»
  (null), while all-cause mortality did fall — «risk ratio 0.82, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.95» — a benefit
  **not routed through the heart** [@ma2017].
- **But for the outcomes weight loss does move, the effect is large.** DiRECT put **46%** of
  short-duration type 2 diabetics into remission (vs 4% control, «OR 19.7»), and remission scaled
  monotonically with kilograms lost — from **0%** in those who gained weight to **86%** in those
  losing >=15 kg [@lean2018, Results]. Notably DiRECT did this on
  a **59%-carbohydrate** formula diet, showing the operative variable is the weight lost, not the
  protocol's gimmick.

So the chain is: meal timing -> (maybe) a calorie deficit -> (maybe) weight loss -> real benefits
for some outcomes (diabetes remission, all-cause mortality), unproven benefit for others (heart
attacks). Meal timing sits at the *front* of that chain, and it is the weakest link — because on a
calorie-matched test the window alone moves nothing.

## Where meal timing actually earns its place: adherence

The one legitimate case for TRE is not mechanistic — it is that **a protocol you will actually
follow beats a better one you abandon** (CLAUDE.md, Recommending). "Don't eat after 8pm" is one
rule, easy to remember, and for some people it reliably cuts intake without calorie-counting. That
is a real, if unglamorous, value: TRE as a **simple deficit-delivery tool**, chosen for fit, not
sold as a metabolic hack. And if you use a window, the evidence points one way: **put it early and
guard protein** — early is the only weight-independent benefit signal, and lean-mass loss is the
only clear harm.

## Where this leaves the ranking

Meal timing is **heavily discussed relative to a small, surrogate-only, weight-independent
effect** — a textbook case of the wiki's rule that attention runs inversely to effect size in a
mature area. It ranks **low** as a health lever. For someone whose big levers (smoking, heavy
alcohol, obesity, inactivity) are unaddressed, the clock position of dinner is not the question.
For someone already lean, active and non-smoking, an early eating window is a plausible small
refinement — but a refinement, on markers, held with low confidence
([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]: a small effect is smallest exactly where
baseline risk is lowest).

## Confidence, gaps, and mandatory caveats

- **** The synthesis that TRE is "a deficit in a costume, with a narrow early-eating
  exception" is the wiki's reading across TREAT, eTRF and the compensation evidence; no single
  source states it.
- **The evidence base is thin.** The two anchor trials are **TREAT (n=116)** and **eTRF (n=8)** —
  one adequately-powered free-living RCT and one tiny confounded crossover. A large intermittent-
  fasting network meta-analysis is staged but **not yet ingested**, so it is not cited here; it may
  shift the weight and appetite readings in a later refresh.
- **Named gaps:** a calorie-matched TRE-vs-continuous-restriction meta-analysis; an early-vs-late
  head-to-head at the same window length; any appetite, sleep, or breakfast-skipping RCT; and any
  **hard-outcome or trajectory** data — everything above rests on surrogates and short trials.
- **Open loop (R1):** this appraisal grades coherence and fidelity to sources, never real-world
  validity. Nothing here was tested against a realized health outcome.
- **Appraise, do not prescribe.** This is a population reading; selecting, dosing, and screening
  for a given person is a prescriber's job.
- **Applied per person.** Adherence and schedule fit are individual — a "worse" protocol you keep
  beats a "better" one you drop.
- **Versus the popular framing (guidance-null):** the wiki diverges from the widespread "TRE / IF
  as a metabolic hack" story. The calorie-matched-trial reading is that it is **mostly an
  intake-reduction tool**, with a small, real, early-eating circadian exception on surrogates.
