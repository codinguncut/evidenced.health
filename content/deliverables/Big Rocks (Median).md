## Two changes help nearly everyone in this group; three others come first if they apply

> [!warning] Applies to a specific group
> This appraisal is written for **a Western adult in their late 40s with central weight gain, high
> triglycerides and borderline blood pressure or glucose, not diagnosed with a disease**. If you are not
> in it, the recommendations here may not transfer — the population-level picture can differ. Other groups
> rank the same levers differently; for adults past about 70, see [[Big Rocks (Elderly)]] as further reading.

For this group, the two changes with the largest held benefit are **moving more, at any intensity, and losing
weight by eating less**. If you smoke, drink heavily or sleep about five hours a night, those come first.
Blood pressure and cholesterol matter in proportion to your overall risk. The diet arguments that get the most
attention — sugar, carbohydrates, saturated fat, salt — move outcomes less than these. ->
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

### The two levers that apply to nearly everyone

**Movement's benefit is large, but it comes from cohorts, not trials.** Adults who wore activity monitors and
moved more, at any intensity, died less often, with the biggest gain among the least active
[@ekelundacc2019]. That includes
light, incidental movement, not only exercise sessions, and reverse causation cannot be ruled out by design.
Activity also tracks lower rates of Parkinson's disease, several cancers and dementia, though the dementia
link beyond ten years is contested [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]]. **One
habit reaches several outcomes at once, and no drug does that job.** ->
[[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]], [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]

**Weight loss is proven in trials, for specific outcomes.** A lifestyle programme built on modest weight loss
sharply cut new type 2 diabetes in adults with prediabetes
[@knowler2002]. Liver fat and liver damage fall step by
step with the share of starting weight lost
[@easl2024], and weight-loss programmes modestly
lowered deaths from any cause in adults with obesity
[@ma2017]. **They have not shown fewer heart
attacks and strokes**, and in a low-risk, non-diabetic group that question is unanswered, not settled ->
[[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].

**The lever is the energy deficit, whatever diet delivers it, and a normal BMI does not rule a reader
out.** In pooled cohorts, metabolically unhealthy normal-weight adults had RR 3.14 (2.36-3.93) for death or
cardiovascular events against metabolically healthy normal-weight adults, and metabolically healthy obesity
was not durably benign either, RR 1.24 (1.02-1.55) in studies with 10 or more years of follow-up. Kramer's
advice is to «consider both BMI and metabolic status»
[@kramer2013mho].

The wiki reads the target of the deficit as fat in the liver, pancreas and around the organs, which tracks
waist and metabolic profile more closely than BMI -> [[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]].
Taylor reports that reversing type 2 diabetes by losing liver and pancreas fat «was found to be achievable
equally readily by people with lower initial BMI»
[@taylor2015pft]. This depot reading
is mechanism-grade, not a trial of weight loss at normal BMI. Where neither waist nor metabolic profile is
raised, the wiki holds no measured benefit or harm.

### The levers sized by your risk

**Blood pressure and apoB are worth acting on in proportion to your absolute risk.** A treatment removes
roughly the same share of risk in everyone, so its absolute benefit scales with the risk you start from: low
over ten years at this age, larger over a lifetime [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]],
[[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]. **ApoB counts the particles that carry cholesterol
into artery walls.** Read non-HDL cholesterol, its free stand-in, off a standard panel, and measure apoB once
LDL-C and non-HDL-C goals are met [[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]].

Both have mature drugs, and a drug shrinks a lifestyle lever only on the outcome the drug covers. A
blood-pressure pill does nothing for liver fat or diabetes risk, so weight loss keeps its value there;
movement has no drug substitute at all. The same rule applies to every drug section below. ->
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]], [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]],
[[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]

### What sits below

**The composition questions are smaller than the attention they get.** Sugar swaps, low- versus
balanced-carbohydrate diets and salt at normal blood pressure each move weight or blood pressure by small
amounts. Replacing saturated fat lowers non-fatal heart attacks and strokes, events that can disable, without
moving death rates, and a healthy plant-rich pattern has a firm direction but a small proven causal core.
Supplements in replete adults, meal timing, coffee, red meat and the ultra-processed category sit at the
ceiling. **Once the larger levers are handled, what remains is small, and that is a reason to stop
optimizing**, not a failure to find something. -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

### Where each finding stands

**Overall confidence: medium.** The order holds whichever of the conditional levers you have. The table keeps
benefit, harm, no effect and insufficient evidence apart: unmeasured is not the same as no effect. The
absolute size of the top two levers at this group's own baseline risk is not held; the last section says why
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

| Lever | Applies to | Size, in words | Evidence behind it | Robust or conditional |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stop smoking | smokers | dominant where present | very large cohorts | conditional on smoking |
| Cut heavy drinking | heavy drinkers | big where present | cohorts and genetic studies | conditional on dose |
| Sleep more | chronic short sleepers (about five hours) | medium where present | self-report cohorts; standard advice, not trial-tested | conditional |
| Move more, from the least active band up | nearly everyone | big | device-measured cohorts | robust in direction |
| Sustained weight loss through eating less | anyone carrying central or ectopic excess fat | big on metabolic outcomes | trials | conditional on central fat and metabolic profile |
| Lower blood pressure | everyone, sized by absolute risk | small over a decade, larger over a lifetime | drug trials; lifestyle effects carried over | conditional on baseline risk |
| Lower apoB | everyone, sized by absolute risk | small over a decade, larger over a lifetime | drug trials; diet reaches markers only | conditional on baseline risk |
| Healthy pattern, plant foods | everyone | medium | strong guidance grade; mostly observational | robust direction, small causal core |
| Sugar, carbohydrate, saturated-fat and salt swaps | everyone | small | trials and cohorts, parts contested | small or contested |

## If you smoke, drink heavily or sleep five hours a night, start there

Three exposures outrank everything else in this guide for the people who have them: smoking, heavy drinking
and chronic short sleep. **If any of the three is present, it comes first.** They are not equal. Smoking
carries the largest and best-established effect, alcohol a dose-dependent one, and short sleep the thinnest
evidence of the three. -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

### Stopping smoking is the largest lever a smoker has, at any age

In 216,917 US adults followed through national death records, **current smokers died at about three times the
rate of people who had never smoked**: HR 3.0 (99% CI 2.7-3.3) in women and 2.8 (2.4-3.1) in men, adjusted for
age, education, alcohol and adiposity. Smokers lost more than 10 years of life
[@jha2013].

**Quitting wins most of those years back, and earlier wins back more.** People who quit at 35-44 gained about
9 years against those who kept smoking; people who quit at 45-54, this guide's age band, still gained about 6.
Quitting before about 40 still left a hazard of about 1.2 against never-smokers
[@jha2013]. The data are observational, but Jha
reports that adjustment barely moved the hazard ratios and that other large cohorts agree
[@jha2013]; an effect this size is hard to explain
by confounding alone -> [[Smoking and Mortality]].

### Alcohol works as a dose: less is better, and moderate drinking does not protect

Observational studies long showed light drinkers outliving abstainers. **That advantage disappears once the
comparison group is cleaned up.** Many "abstainers" in those studies are former drinkers who stopped because
they were ill. Across 87 studies, correcting for that bias and for study quality left low-volume drinking at
RR 0.97 (0.88-1.07) against abstainers, with no protection, and the J-shaped curve was no longer seen
[@stockwell2016].

A genetic analysis in China reached the same answer by a different route. In 161,498 genotyped adults, gene
variants that make carriers drink less showed stroke risk rising steadily with genotype-predicted intake in
men, total stroke RR 1.38 (1.26-1.51) per 280 g a week, with no protective low end; women, few of whom drink,
served as the contrast [@millwood2019].
The causal estimate is therefore a men's estimate. Myocardial infarction behaves differently in that study ->
[[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]], [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

The largest update, 107 studies and 4.8 million people, sets out where the all-cause death signal turns
statistically significant. Pooled across both sexes, risk was raised at 45-64 g a day, RR 1.19 (1.07-1.32); in
women alone it was already raised at 25-44 g a day, RR 1.21 (1.08-1.36)
[@zhao2023]. **These are points where the signal
becomes clear, not a permitted ceiling.** At 25-44 g a day the pooled estimate was RR 1.05 (0.96-1.14), P=.28:
above 1, not significant, and no evidence of safety
[@zhao2023]. A burden-weighted analysis found the
lowest-risk intake did not differ by sex
[@gbd2022alcohol], so read neither number as a
sex-specific safe level.

All-cause mortality is also the outcome most favourable to alcohol. **For cancer, risk rises from low
intake.** The World Cancer Research Fund finds no level of drinking below which the risk of at least some
cancers does not rise [@wcrf2018ter]. In 572 studies,
cancers of the mouth and pharynx, oesophagus (squamous cell) and breast were raised even at about one drink a
day or less; breast RR 1.04 (1.01-1.07)
[@bagnardi2014]. A heavy
drinker gains most by cutting down; for anyone else, less is still better -> [[Alcohol and Cancer Risk]].

### Chronic short sleep, not six hours, is the problem to fix

Pooled across 16 studies and 1.38 million people, short sleepers had RR 1.12 (1.06-1.18) for death against
each study's reference sleep length, most often 7 hours. Cappuccio frames the attributable deaths as an
estimate that holds only if the link is causal
[@cappuccio2010]. **The excess sits at chronic short
sleep.** Yin's hour-by-hour curve puts 6 h at RR 1.01 (1.00-1.01) against 7 h and 5 h at 1.04, steepening
below that [@yin2017]. For type 2 diabetes, the
shortest sleepers (5 h or less) had RR 1.37 (1.18-1.59)
[@shan2015]. Every one of these cohorts
relied on self-reported sleep.

Genetic studies add causal support for heart disease: genetically predicted short sleep raised coronary artery
disease risk, OR 1.24 (1.15-1.34), with no causal effect found on stroke
[@wang2022sleep]. Gene variants fix a tendency
from birth, so these estimates describe lifelong short sleep, not what happens when an adult starts sleeping
longer.

**A habitual five-hour sleeper should aim to get well past six hours, toward the 7-hour reference most of
these cohorts used.** The basis is the observational curve plus mechanism: standard advice, not trial-tested
on diabetes or death. Long sleep is a separate question and not a target in either direction ->
[[Sleep Duration and Mortality]], [[Sleep and Metabolic Health]].

## Move more at any intensity; the first step up from the least-active band is the biggest

**Add movement of any kind, starting with the least active, and keep adding until roughly 20-40 minutes a day
of brisk walking or the equivalent; past that, the mortality curve flattens.** The action is to move more and
sit less. It is not a prescription for formal aerobic training: light, incidental movement counts toward the
total. -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

### The first step off the floor carries most of the gain

The best-measured evidence comes from devices, not questionnaires. A harmonised meta-analysis of eight
accelerometer cohorts (36,383 adults aged 40 and over, mean age 62.6, 72.8% women, median follow-up 5.8 years)
split people into quarters of activity. **Adults in the second quarter of total activity had about half the
death rate of the least active quarter: HR 0.48 (0.43-0.54).** The third and fourth quarters reached 0.34 and
0.27 (0.23-0.32) [@ekelundacc2019]. That 0.48
ranks people on all movement the hip device registers; moderate-to-vigorous activity (MVPA), run as a separate
analysis, gave a second-quarter HR of 0.64 (0.55-0.74), so the halving does not split into minutes of exercise
[@ekelundacc2019]. The cohorts handled reverse
causation only by dropping early deaths [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

Unadjusted, 1187 of 9096 people in the least active quarter died against 483 of 9105 in the second
[@ekelundacc2019].

That is 13.0% against 5.3%, about 77 fewer deaths per 1000 people over a median 5.8 years
[inferred from @ekelundacc2019].
**That gap is unadjusted.** The least active people are older and sicker, so it overstates what moving more
would buy; the adjusted HRs are the better estimate of the effect.

### The smallest increments pay most at the bottom

A 2026 analysis from the same research group fitted the MVPA curve continuously, and the gain from an extra 5
minutes a day depended on where a person started. Going from 1 to 6 minutes a day of MVPA was associated with
about 30% lower mortality, going from 9 to 14 with about 18% lower, and beyond a little over 20 minutes a
day no clear further reduction was evident
[@ekelund2026small]. This refines the 2019
quarter result rather than confirming it independently: it draws on the same consortium
[[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

**The labelled default for someone near-inactive is about 5-10 extra minutes a day of walking at roughly 4-5
km/h, or about an hour a day more light activity.** That is a standing-practice default built on these curves,
not a trial-tested dose [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

### Where the curve flattens: a ceiling, not a target

In the 2019 analysis the MVPA hazard reached its low point, HR 0.39 (0.26-0.59), at a little over 20 minutes
a day, which the authors call «congruent with the minimum recommended level» of at least 150 minutes of
moderate activity a week, and higher MVPA brought no further risk reduction. Few people did
more than that, so the intervals at the high end are wide
[@ekelundacc2019]. **Read the flat stretch as a
plateau. Its low point is not a hard threshold**: the weekly total is what matters, and the wiki reads the
curve as flat across roughly 20-40 minutes a day. The 2026 curve still drifts
down slightly within that zone, so further minutes are a small, uncertain gain, not a proven zero.

Steps show the same shape: risk stopped falling at about 6000-8000 steps a day in adults 60 and over and at about
8000-10,000 in younger adults [@paluch2022]
[[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]. In absolute terms, the second step quartile (median 5801 steps a day)
had 65 fewer deaths per 1000 people (72 to 58 fewer) than the first (3553) over a median 7.1 years. That figure
is unadjusted [@paluch2022].

### Sitting less is the same action, not a second one

On device data, cutting sitting time and adding movement are one change. The 2026 analysis states that
«associations for lower sedentary time reflect associations for increases in both light physical activity and
MVPA.» [@ekelund2026small] Less sitting
showed no association with mortality below about 8 hours of sitting a day
[@ekelund2026small]; above 8 hours,
self-reported mortality rose 4% per extra hour, RR 1.04 (1.03-1.05), after adjustment for activity
[@patterson2018sedentary].
**For someone sitting more than about 8 hours a day, the default is to turn 30-60 of those minutes into
walking** [[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]].

### Add strength training at modest volume

Strength training adds to aerobic activity rather than replacing it. In the WHO evidence profiles, adults
meeting the strength guideline had lower mortality than those who did not, HR 0.80 (0.70-0.91), and adults
meeting both the aerobic and the strength guidelines did better than those meeting neither, HR 0.71
(0.57-0.87), both rated moderate certainty; a second analysis, in smokers, gave the same 0.71 (0.62-0.81) for
both against neither [@who2020]. Each
figure sits on its own reference group, and figures with different reference groups do not stack.

**The default is strength work on two or more days a week, about 30-60 minutes a week in total, where Momma's
pooled curve shows its largest reduction**
[@momma2022]. The same meta-analysis gave
all-cause RR 0.85 (0.79-0.93) at very low GRADE certainty, and type 2 diabetes RR 0.83 (0.77-0.89) at low
certainty [@momma2022]. **For this group
strength work is also a glucose lever**: muscle is the main site of insulin-driven glucose uptake, so more
muscle gives glucose somewhere to go. That is accepted physiology, not a trial in this group
[[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]. How the sessions are programmed matters far less than doing
them: across prescriptions, load, sets and frequency barely separate, while training beats not training
[[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].

### Beyond about two hours a week, more strength work is not shown to add benefit

In three large US cohorts (147,374 people), the top category of 120 or more minutes a week still had lower
mortality than no strength work, HR 0.92 (0.86-0.98), with no additional benefit over lower amounts
[@zhang2026rtmort]; that category averaged 204
minutes, read from Zhang's Table 2 on the linked page.

Zhang's cancer-mortality curve crosses 1.0 near 150 minutes a week with an interval that includes 1, and no cancer category estimate is above
1.0; Momma's pooled all-cause curve returns to 1.0 near 140 minutes, and its lower 95% band sits above 1.0
from about 165 minutes to the end of its plotted range near 195
[inferred from @momma2022]
[[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]. Part of Zhang's association is shared with aerobic
activity: adjusting for it moved the 90-119 minute estimate from 0.76 (0.70-0.82) to 0.87 (0.81-0.95)
[@zhang2026rtmort].

**In these cohorts more than 2 hours a week still beat none but was not shown to buy more, and the far upper
tail is not shown safe**: a ceiling, not a warning.

### Track fitness; act on activity

Cardiorespiratory fitness is the measurable milestone: each extra MET goes with about 13% lower all-cause
mortality, RR 0.87 (0.84-0.90), in observational data
[@kodama2009]. It predicts outcomes but is not
shown to be a lever separate from the activity that raises it, and intensity moves it more than duration
[[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]]. The AHA statement prefers a maximal exercise test where
feasible. Equations built on routine clinical measures can estimate fitness, and two of them predicted
long-term mortality about as well as measured fitness, but in most clinical patients they «should not be
viewed as a replacement» for a test [@ross2016].

### Start gradually if you are starting from nothing

The rare cardiac harm of exercise falls on the people who gain most: exercise-related sudden cardiac death
concentrates in habitually sedentary people with known or hidden heart disease doing unaccustomed strenuous
work [@franklin2020acute]. In absolute terms Franklin
calls it very rare, 0.31 to 2.1 sudden cardiac deaths per 100,000 person-years
[@franklin2020acute], a population rate,
not a per-session one. Under ACSM screening
advice, an inactive adult without symptoms or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease can start
light-to-moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up over about 2-3 months
[@riebe2015screen]; anyone with known disease or
symptoms gets clearance first. That is expert consensus, not a trial of screening
[[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]].

### Movement barely moves the scale, but it does target visceral fat

Across exercise-only trials in adults with obesity, weight loss ranged from 0.05 to 1.01 kg
[@odonoghue2020]. Visceral fat is different. In 40
trials (2190 participants), eating less produced the larger raw reduction (ES -0.53 against -0.28 for
exercise), but once the size of the weekly energy deficit was taken into account, exercise removed more
visceral fat per unit of deficit (ES -0.18, -0.33 to -0.04)
[@recchia2023]. **If visceral fat is
the target, weight the deficit toward activity and combine the two**: activity for the visceral edge, eating
less for the size of the deficit. That is a labelled default from a between-trial meta-regression with small
standardized effects -> [[Exercise vs Caloric Restriction for Visceral Fat]].

## Lose weight by eating less, for diabetes, liver and blood pressure, not for a promised heart-attack reduction

**For weight, the lever is a sustained energy deficit.** In 32 controlled feeding studies that
swapped carbohydrate for fat at equal calories, across carbohydrate intakes from 1% to 83% of energy, the
lower-fat diets burned 26 kcal/day more and lost 16 g/day more fat. Hall calls that physiologically
meaningless for body fat, then adds that such diets «may have overall health effects unrelated to total body
fat or energy expenditure», for example refined carbohydrate possibly shifting fat toward visceral and liver
stores [@hallguo2017]. So for the scale, the diet that
works is the one you can keep to; for liver and visceral fat, composition is an open question
([[What Drives Fat Gain - Energy Balance vs the Carbohydrate-Insulin Model]],
[[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]]).

### Diabetes prevention is the best-tested benefit

In the Diabetes Prevention Program, adults with prediabetes (mean age 51, mean BMI 34) followed an intensive
lifestyle programme, took metformin or took placebo. The lifestyle group lost 5.6 kg on average and **cut new
diabetes by 58% (95% CI 48-66%)** against placebo over an average 2.8 years; metformin cut it by 31% (17-43%).
About seven people (6.9, 5.4-9.5) had to join the programme for three years to prevent one case, against 13.9
on metformin [@knowler2002].

Within the lifestyle arm, each 5 kg lost went with a 58% lower hazard of diabetes (HR 0.42, 0.35-0.51)
[@hamman2006], a dose-response inside the same trial, not a
second trial. DPP did not test whether preventing a diagnosis averts heart attacks or extends life
([[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]).

### Liver fat, triglycerides and blood pressure fall as weight comes off

Anyone in this stratum who has liver fat meets the definition of fatty liver disease (MASLD), because the
second criterion is a single cardiometabolic factor, such as a waist of at least 94 cm in European men or 80
cm in women, or a BMI of 25 or more [@easl2024].
**For most people with MASLD the larger danger is to the heart and metabolism, not the liver.** In the general
population, liver fat itself is «not associated with a clinically meaningful increase in the risk of liver-related outcomes»,
while EASL reports a higher risk of non-fatal cardiovascular disease (HR 1.40)
[@easl2024] and more than double the risk of a new
type 2 diabetes diagnosis [@easl2024]
([[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]).

EASL sets its targets as a **share of starting weight**: at least 5% to reduce liver fat, 7-10% to improve
liver inflammation (MASH) and at least 10% to improve fibrosis (strong recommendation, level of evidence 2)
[@easl2024]. The ladder traces to a single trial
with liver biopsies, so read the cut-offs as where the trials sampled, not as precise thresholds. Few people
reach 5%, and liver fat partly returns as the weight does ([[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]).

The 2013 US obesity guideline grades the risk-factor doses High. About 3 kg lost lowers triglycerides by at
least 15 mg/dL; a 5% loss lowers blood pressure by about 3/2 mmHg; 5-8 kg lowers LDL-C by only about 5 mg/dL
[@jensen2014obesity]. The guideline prints these as point
estimates without intervals. **The big lipid change is in triglycerides, not LDL-C.**

### The same loss eases knee pain when exercise goes with it

In the IDEA trial (454 overweight or obese adults aged 55 or over with knee osteoarthritis, 18 months),
diet-induced weight loss plus exercise reduced pain more than either alone; in a post hoc analysis 38% of the
combined group reported little or no pain, against 20% on diet alone and 22% on exercise alone
[@messier2013idea]. The trial sits above
this group's age band, but pain and function count as outcomes in their own right ->
[[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]].

### Heart attacks and strokes: not shown for the lifestyle route

Look AHEAD randomised 5,145 overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes to an intensive lifestyle
programme or diabetes education. It stopped for futility after a median 9.6 years: CV death, non-fatal heart
attack, non-fatal stroke or hospitalised angina, HR 0.95 (0.83-1.09)
[@look2013]. The weight gap between arms had
shrunk from 7.9 to 2.5 percentage points, so it tested a modest sustained difference. Ma's
meta-analysis of 54 RCTs in 30,206 obese adults found CV events RR 0.93 (0.83-1.04)
[@ma2017], a pool Look AHEAD may dominate, so the
two nulls are not independent ([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]).

**All-cause mortality did fall in Ma's pool: RR 0.82 (0.71-0.95)**, high-quality evidence, about six fewer
deaths per 1000 (2 to 10) over a median two years, on diets mostly low in fat and saturated fat
[@ma2017]. That benefit does not seem to run
through the heart, and the trials cannot separate the fat reduction from the weight lost. For a low-risk
person without diabetes, the effect of lifestyle weight loss on heart attacks and strokes is **insufficient
evidence, not a shown null**.

Semaglutide did cut CV events in SELECT, HR 0.80 (0.72-0.90), but in people who already had cardiovascular
disease [@lincoff2023], so it says
nothing about primary prevention and does not rank a drug route above lifestyle
([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]).

### Measure the waist alongside BMI

Jayedi's dose-response meta-analysis of 72 cohorts found waist measures predict death independently of BMI:
per 10 cm of waist HR 1.11 (1.08-1.13), rising to 1.17 (1.13-1.22) in studies that adjusted for BMI, with high
heterogeneity. Risk began to rise from a waist-to-height ratio of 0.50 and was significantly higher by 0.55
[@jayedi2020central]. **A waist under half
your height** is a usable marker for placing risk. These are associations measured at baseline, not a measured
benefit of shrinking a waist ([[Central Adiposity and All-Cause Mortality]], [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]]).

### Expect regain, and plan the maintenance before you start

**Regain is the usual course.** Across studies, groups regained about 0.50 kg for each extra kg lost
(0.25-0.74; between-study meta-regression, median follow-up 44 weeks)
[@turicchi2019regain], which still leaves a bigger
loss with more kept. Losing fast did not mean regaining more in the one held RCT: among adults who
reached a 12.5% loss, three-year regain was the same after a 12-week very-low-calorie diet as after a 36-week
diet, and more of the fast arm reached that target, 78% against 51% by intention to treat
[@purcell2014rate]
([[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]).

The US guideline's Grade A recommendation is **a maintenance programme for at least a year**, with monthly or
more frequent contact, 200-300 min/week of activity and weighing weekly or more
[@jensen2014obesity]
([[Lifestyle Weight-Loss Programme Intensity and Delivery]], [[Self-Weighing for Weight Loss]]). Fear of
weight cycling is not a reason to stay heavy: in obese people harm is not shown, which is not the same as
shown safe [@zou2019fluctuation]
([[Weight Cycling and Cardiometabolic Risk]]).

### Keep protein up during the deficit

In 24 energy-restricted trials of overweight and obese adults without structured exercise, raising protein
from about 0.7 to about 1.25 g/kg/day kept 0.43 kg more fat-free mass (0.09-0.78)
[@wycherley2012]. Treat that intake as
**a floor and a low-cost adjunct**, not a large lever. Fat-free mass is a surrogate, and the higher targets
come from lean, resistance-trained samples. How much protein an obese adult in a deficit needs is a named gap ([[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]],
[[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]).

### Drugs are the realistic comparator, not a one-off fix

In STEP-1 (no diabetes, mean age 46), semaglutide 2.4 mg gave 12.4 percentage points more weight loss than
placebo at 68 weeks (95% CI 11.5-13.4)
[@wilding2021], and tirzepatide 15 mg gave 17.8
points in SURMOUNT-1 [@jastreboff2022]. When drug
and lifestyle support both stopped, **participants regained two-thirds of the lost weight within a year**, and
most cardiometabolic gains reverted [@wilding2022]. The
drug works while it is taken; its effect on events in people without cardiovascular disease is untested
 ([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]).

### If you already have type 2 diabetes: remission

This part applies only where type 2 diabetes is already diagnosed; applying it to someone without diabetes is
an assumption. In DiRECT (UK primary care, diagnosed within 6 years, BMI 27-45, not on insulin), a formula
diet of 825-853 kcal/day for 3-5 months, then food reintroduction and structured maintenance, **put 46% into
remission at 12 months against 4% on usual care** [@lean2018];
at 24 months the figures were 35.6% against 3.4%
[@lean2019]. The programme began by withdrawing
antidiabetic and antihypertensive drugs [@lean2018], so anyone
on them needs a prescriber's adjustment first
([[Total Diet Replacement and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]]).

Two corrections bound it. Remission rose with weight lost, from 0% in those who gained to 86% in those who
lost 15 kg or more, but that gradient pools both arms and every loss of 15 kg or more came from the formula
arm [@lean2018]. And the formula was 59% carbohydrate by energy at
825-853 kcal/day [@lean2018], about 122-126 g/day
[inferred from @lean2018], under Goldenberg's 130 g/day
low-carbohydrate cut-off [@goldenberg2021], so
**DiRECT does not show remission without carbohydrate restriction in grams.**

Churuangsuk's umbrella review, co-written by DiRECT's lead author, cautions that residual confounding may
remain and concludes: «The main contributor to HbA1c reduction and remission appears to be weight loss,
irrespective of diet type.» [@churuangsuk2022] Its
diet-independence point rests on one relayed cohort, ADDITION-Cambridge, with no diet comparison described,
so whether carbohydrate restriction adds anything beyond the weight it takes off is insufficient evidence
.

## Measure apoB when it adds, and lower it by a route that moves the particles

Losing liver and visceral fat lowers triglycerides, but the wiki holds no figure for what it does to apoB, the
particle count that drives arterial disease -> [[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]].

### Insulin resistance hides extra particles behind a normal LDL-C

Fat in the liver and around the organs drives insulin resistance, which shifts blood lipids toward high
triglycerides, low HDL and small, dense LDL -> [[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]]. Each
atherogenic particle carries one apoB molecule, so apoB counts the particles; LDL-C measures only the
cholesterol inside LDL. **The European Atherosclerosis Society consensus judges that LDL and the other
apoB-carrying particles cause atherosclerotic disease**, and that risk falls in proportion to the absolute
reduction and how long it is held, provided the cholesterol drop matches a drop in particle number
[@ference2017]. A modest reduction kept for decades
counts for more than a larger one started late.

The same consensus names the catch for this group: in metabolic syndrome, diabetes and high triglycerides,
**LDL-C can under-state the particle burden**
[@ference2017]. In UK Biobank, with apoB, non-HDL-C and
triglycerides entered together, only apoB stayed associated with heart attack; the same paper names non-HDL-C
as the substitute where apoB is not measured
[@marston2022]. A Mendelian randomization from a
group with no shared authors found instead that coronary risk follows non-HDL-C, not apoB
[@helgadottir2022] ->
[[ApoB Particle Number vs Cholesterol Content]]. Both camps agree that apoB and non-HDL-C beat LDL-C.


### Once LDL-C goals are met, high triglycerides are the prompt to measure apoB

The 2026 US guideline says apoB testing can be useful once LDL-C and non-HDL-C goals are met, particularly
with triglycerides of 150 mg/dL or more, diabetes, or a low achieved LDL-C
[@accaha2026]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index predicts
coronary disease in mostly Asian cohorts but did not improve on the Framingham score, and its authors call for
studies in non-Asians
[@liu2022tyg]
[@liu2022tyg]. TyG
flags risk; it is not a treatment target, and the US guideline above does not use it as a trigger for testing
 ->
[[Insulin Resistance Surrogates and Cardiovascular Risk]].

### Each route moves a different marker, by a different amount

The held routes do not move the same quantity, so the table names the marker each one moves and does not add
them together.

**On low-carbohydrate eating, triglycerides fall clearly while LDL-C may rise**: the 12-month interval for
LDL-C reaches zero. Anyone who chooses it should check apoB or non-HDL-C rather than infer the net from
triglycerides. These figures are in people with diabetes; transfer to this non-diabetic group is an
assumption.

### A diet's LDL drop does not inherit the statin's event reduction

The statin trials counted heart attacks and strokes; the diet trials measured blood lipids. A cholesterol fall
transmits only if the particle count falls with it and nothing else harms
[@ference2017]. In the Minnesota Coronary Experiment,
replacing saturated fat with corn oil lowered serum cholesterol with no mortality benefit, measuring total
cholesterol only [@ramsden2016]. **So no held trial shows
any diet lowering hard cardiovascular events through apoB; that leg is insufficient evidence, not a null.**
The Portfolio pattern's apoB fall is real, but its seven comparisons come from the diet's originators and none
counted events [@chiavaroli2018portfolio]; a plaque-imaging trial
is pending.

### The statin is the comparator, and its size depends on absolute risk

For adults aged 40-75 with at least one risk factor, USPSTF recommends a statin at a 10-year risk of 10% or
more, offers it selectively from 7.5%, and makes no recommendation below that
[@uspstf2022] ->
[[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]. The wiki holds no 10-year baseline for a
late-40s adult with this profile, so where a given reader sits is a named gap.

Over a lifetime the arithmetic changes. ESC's model puts **1 mmol/L of LDL-C lowering, started at 45-49, at
roughly 0.6 to 1.6 years free of heart attack or stroke for a non-smoking man**, and 0.9 to 2.0 years for a
non-smoking woman. Those years are modelled from a lifetime risk times the trial effect, not measured in
anyone [@esc2021] ->
[[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

The statin's harms carry the same grain. Over 5 years of 1 mmol/L LDL-C lowering, CTT estimates about 11
episodes of muscle pain or weakness per 1000 people, generally mild, against 25 major vascular events
prevented in primary prevention [@reith2022muscle]. **New diabetes is
a real drug effect**: RR 1.10 (1.04-1.16) on low or moderate intensity, about 0.12% a year, and RR 1.36
(1.25-1.48) on high intensity, with about 62% of the excess in people already in the top quarter of baseline
glycaemia [@reith2024diabetes], where a reader with borderline
glucose sits -> [[Statins and New-Onset Diabetes]].

### What to do

- **Read non-HDL-C off a standard lipid panel. Once LDL-C and non-HDL-C goals are met, measure apoB if
  triglycerides are 150 mg/dL or more, you have diabetes, or LDL-C is already low.** That is the guideline's
  suggestion; no trial has tested a measure-apoB strategy against outcomes
  ([[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]).
- **Lower it first by diet**: swap saturated fat for polyunsaturated oils and add Portfolio foods (nuts, soy
  and pulses, viscous fibre, plant sterols). These cost little and carry no drug harms, but their event
  benefit is borrowed, not shown ([[Portfolio Dietary Pattern and LDL Cholesterol]]).
- **Weigh a statin by absolute risk**, over 10 years and over a lifetime, with its muscle and diabetes harms
  counted at the same scale ([[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]).

## Size blood pressure by absolute risk; try the non-drug levers first

**Lowering blood pressure cuts heart attacks and strokes even in people
without heart disease**, but at this stratum's age the absolute gain from a drug is small, and **a few months
of non-drug change comes first** ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

### A proven relative effect, a small absolute one at this age

The largest pooled analysis of blood-pressure trials found that **each 5 mmHg fall in systolic pressure
lowered major cardiovascular events by about 9% in people without prior cardiovascular disease**, HR 0.91
(0.89-0.94), with no baseline-pressure subgroup in which the hazard ratio rose above 1. Those participants
averaged 65 years old, and the comparator group had 31.9 major events per 1000 person-years. The authors call
for treatment in anyone whose absolute cardiovascular risk is high enough, not for treating a blood-pressure
number [@bplttc2021].

A Cochrane review asked the narrower question a younger person faces: does starting a drug help in untreated
stage-1 hypertension (140-159/90-99 mmHg) without cardiovascular disease? Its five trials enrolled 9124
people, weighted mean age 50. **Both legs are low certainty.** On benefit, total cardiovascular events came
out at RR 0.93 (0.69-1.24), 25 vs 23 per 1000 over 2-5 years (4 trials, 7292 people), while stroke fell, RR
0.41 (0.20-0.84), 7 vs 3 per 1000, from only 35 events, most of them in a trial of people with
microalbuminuria [@wang2025mildhtn]. The events
result is **insufficient evidence, not a null**: trials this small could not detect a benefit of the size the
pooled analysis predicts.

On harm, withdrawals for adverse effects rose from 23 to 110 per 1000, RR 4.80 (4.14-5.57), from one
single-blind 1980s trial [@wang2025mildhtn]. On a
current drug pair, HOPE-3 found 3.4% vs 2.0% stopping for low blood pressure or dizziness over 5.6 years, in
older people who had passed an active run-in [@lonn2016hope3], about 14
more per 1000 [inferred from @lonn2016hope3]. A
withdrawal is reversible and a stroke is not, so this cut does not net one against the other.

Carried over to this lower baseline, **a 5 mmHg fall buys about 2 fewer cardiovascular events per 1000 over
about 4.4 years** (roughly 1.5 to 2.8). The Cochrane authors would not make that step: they want
decisions on starting a drug in mild hypertension to rest on direct trials
[@wang2025mildhtn]. This cut carries the pooled
relative effect over as a labelled default, because no trial shows the effect is smaller here
([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]). In the 130s, below Wang's band, the gain is smaller still
.

The ten-year view understates the case at 45-49: ESC notes that under 50 the 10-year risk is low on average
even with high risk-factor levels, while the lifetime risk can be very high
[@esc2021]. So **get an absolute-risk estimate, over a
lifetime as well as ten years, before deciding on a drug**
([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

### Run a three-to-six-month non-drug trial first

Some guidelines suggest a three-to-six-month trial of non-drug measures before starting a drug, and others
suggest non-drug measures only, in this group
[@wang2025mildhtn]. This is a **labelled default:
guidance and practice, not trial-tested**. At this baseline a few months' delay gives up very little expected
benefit and avoids the drug's tolerability cost. The candidate levers:

- **Structured exercise.** In trials of people at 140 mmHg or above it lowered systolic pressure by 8.96 mmHg
  (-10.27 to -7.64), indirectly no different from drugs, 0.18 (-1.35 to 1.68). No trial compared the two
  directly, and the exercise trials were mostly unblinded
  [@naci2019exercise].
- **A potassium-enriched salt substitute for home-added salt.** A 25-40% potassium chloride substitute lowered
  systolic pressure by 4.64 mmHg (-6.05 to -3.24) across 18 trials
  [@lai2026salt]. In SSaSS it cut stroke, rate ratio
  0.86 (0.77-0.96), in 20,995 rural Chinese adults, mean age 65.4, of whom 72.6% had already had a stroke
  [@neal2021], so the hard-outcome result is
  largely secondary prevention.
- **Less alcohol, for anyone above two drinks a day.** People drinking two a day or fewer saw no significant
  fall when they cut back; at six or more a day the fall was 5.50 mmHg (-6.70 to -4.30)
  [@roerecke2017alcohol]
  [@roerecke2017alcohol]. Of the 2865 trial participants,
  2464 were men [@roerecke2017alcohol], and with only three
  trials reporting women the authors have less confidence in the pooled estimates
  [@roerecke2017alcohol].
- **Weight loss where overweight** (dose above) and **less salt where intake is high**: in most Western
  countries salt intake runs at 9-10 g a day against a recommended maximum of 5 g
  [@esc2021].

**Screen before switching the household's salt.** SSaSS excluded households in which anyone used a
potassium-sparing diuretic or a potassium supplement or had known serious kidney disease
[@neal2021]. Lai tells clinicians to rule out
kidney problems and hyperkalaemia risk first
[@lai2026salt]. For those groups the substitute is
contraindicated, and its safety there is untested, not shown ([[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]).

Each of these levers lowers blood pressure, a surrogate. Whether a fall from lifestyle change cuts events at
the drug trials' rate has not been tested; the salt substitute is the one non-drug route with measured hard
outcomes ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]). Reassess
after the trial period; if pressure stays raised, the drug decision follows absolute risk and is the person's
to make.

## The composition levers are smaller than their attention

**Saturated fat, carbohydrate, sugar and salt each have a measured effect, and each is smaller than
movement, weight loss or the risk-scaled levers above.** None is zero, and a few cost almost nothing to act
on. -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

### Saturated fat: fewer heart attacks and strokes, no measured change in deaths

**In trials, lower saturated-fat intake against higher reduced cardiovascular events**: RR 0.83 (0.70-0.98),
15 fewer events per 1000 (25 to 2 fewer) at an 8.5% control rate, moderate certainty, 13 RCTs
[@who2023saturated]. In primary prevention, 56
people would need to cut saturated fat for about four years for one to avoid an event
[@hooper2020]. **All-cause mortality
did not move**: RR 0.96 (0.90-1.03), moderate certainty
[@who2023saturated]. The events that fell include
non-fatal heart attacks and strokes, often disabling, so the gain is real even though it does not lengthen
life -> [[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]].

**WHO recommends replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat**, strong recommendation, moderate certainty
[@who2023saturated]. Which replacement does most is not
settled: in Hooper's subgroups, polyunsaturated replacement suggested a 21% fall in cardiovascular events and
carbohydrate a 16% fall, with no statistically significant difference between them
[@hooper2020]. Three US cohorts
associate swapping 10 g/day of butter for plant oil with lower total mortality, HR 0.83 (0.79-0.86), an
observational, modelled estimate whose CVD-mortality arm is null
[@zhang2025butter]. For someone eating less to lose weight, WHO
allows saturated fat to be cut without replacement
[@who2023saturated].

The 10% figure marks where WHO's trials thin out: WHO finds only limited evidence for going lower
[@who2023saturated]. The trial base is also contested, on
a post hoc re-reading of a narrower trial pool ->
[[Does Reducing Saturated Fat Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].

### Low-carbohydrate diets: about a kilogram, much of it possibly water

In adults without diabetes, **low-carbohydrate diets lost about 1 kg more than balanced-carbohydrate diets**:
1.07 kg (0.59-1.55) at 3 to under 12 months and 0.93 kg (0.04-1.81) at 12 months or more, moderate certainty
[@naude2022]; with similar energy
prescriptions the gap was 0.48 kg (0.11-0.85)
[@naude2022]. No included trial
measured death, heart attack, stroke or new diabetes, and **the review advises caution for people with lipid
disorders whose atherogenic lipoproteins rise on a high-fat diet**
[@naude2022]. Anyone who switches can recheck
non-HDL-C or apoB after a few months, standard practice, not trial-tested ->
[[Low-Carbohydrate vs Balanced-Carbohydrate Diets]].

### Sugar: the scale responds to calories, and sugary drinks are the target

Advice to eat less free sugar lowered weight by 0.80 kg (0.39-1.21; 5 RCTs, moderate certainty). But
**swapping sugar for other carbohydrate at equal energy changed weight by 0.04 kg (-0.04 to 0.13)**
[@who2015]. Sugar moves weight through the calories it adds.
Sugar-sweetened drinks carry a separate signal: each extra 250 mL/day tracks a higher type 2 diabetes risk, RR
1.19 (1.13-1.25), across 19 cohorts, observational only
[@qin2020]. **Cutting sugary drinks
is cheap and reversible**, so it earns a plain recommendation on an observational signal ->
[[Free Sugars Intake]].

### Salt: small at normal blood pressure, larger as pressure rises

Cutting sodium lowered systolic pressure by 1.38 mmHg (0.02-2.74; 6 studies, moderate certainty) with normal
blood pressure and by 4.06 mmHg (2.96-5.15; 24 studies, high certainty) with hypertension
[@who2012]. **A borderline reading should expect a
fall between the two**. On hard outcomes, two RCTs on cardiovascular disease gave RR 0.84
(0.57-1.23), inconclusive; in cohorts, higher sodium tracked fatal stroke at RR 1.63 (1.27-2.10), low
certainty, and most other rows are very low [@who2012]. Whether everyone
or only people with hypertension should cut salt is disputed -> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]],
[[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]].

### The healthy pattern: a strong grade with no number attached

The US dietary-guidelines review graded the evidence **Strong** that patterns rich in vegetables, fruit,
legumes, nuts, whole grains, unsaturated oils and fish are associated with lower all-cause mortality. It
pooled no effect size, so the grade carries no magnitude
[@boushey2020]. The one pattern RCT with hard
outcomes, PREDIMED, cut heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death to HR 0.70 (0.55-0.89), 3.8% against
5.7% over five years, mostly through stroke. All-cause mortality was 0.98 (0.77-1.24). **Its participants were
high-risk Spanish adults**, about half with type 2 diabetes, and the authors leave transfer to lower-risk
people open [@estruch2018] ->
[[Dietary Patterns and All-Cause Mortality]], [[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]].

Fruit and vegetables track lower all-cause mortality, RR 0.90 (0.87-0.93) per 200 g/day, in cohorts. **About
500 g/day reaches most of the reduction seen at 800 g/day** (27% against 31% lower, compared with 0-40 g/day);
for coronary heart disease the gradient keeps going (16% against 24%), and 800 g/day is the top of the studied
range [@aune2017fv]. **Cohorts agree on the direction, but
trials have shown only a small causal share**, for fibre mainly through LDL ->
[[Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Health]], [[Dietary Fibre and Health]],
[[Whole Grains Refined Grains and Pulses]], [[Food Groups and Health Outcomes - A Dose-Response Matrix]],
[[Named Diet Programs Compared]].

## What can be left alone, and what is not known

Below the composition levers, the levers that remain are small, null, or untested in this stratum.

### Levers you can leave alone

**Vitamin D and fish oil did not lower major cardiovascular events in adults who are not deficient.** VITAL
randomized 25,871 US adults (men 50 and over, women 55 and over, mostly replete) for a median 5.3 years:
vitamin D3 HR 0.97 (0.85-1.12) [@manson2019vitd], marine omega-3 HR 0.92
(0.80-1.06). Omega-3's lower total myocardial infarction, HR 0.72 (0.59-0.90), was a secondary endpoint
without correction for multiple testing, to be read with caution, its authors say
[@manson2019n3]. Beta-carotene raised lung cancer in smokers and
asbestos-exposed workers (RR 1.18, 1.03-1.36)
[@uspstf2022supp]. Correcting a measured
deficiency is a different question ([[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]],
[[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]]).

**In prediabetes, vitamin D's small benefit showed up in leaner people only.** A pooled analysis of three
diabetes-prevention trials (4190 adults with prediabetes, mean BMI 30) found 15% fewer new cases (HR 0.85,
0.75-0.96). In the cholecalciferol trials it worked below the median BMI of 31.3 (HR 0.74, 0.60-0.90) but not
above it (HR 1.01, 0.84-1.22), and the authors name a mechanism: obesity blunts vitamin D activation
[@pittas2023vitamindipd]. The paper sets its number needed to treat
of 30 beside 7 for intensive lifestyle change in the separate DPP trial; that is a comparison across trials,
not a head-to-head test ([[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]).

**A shorter eating window adds nothing to the same calorie deficit.** Across 99 trials, mostly short and
measuring weight rather than disease, time-restricted eating beat eating freely by 1.72 kg (-2.21 to -1.22).
That is below the 2.0 kg the review treats as the smallest important difference, and the window did not beat
ordinary calorie restriction
[@semnaniazad2025]. One trial
(TREAT) found a lean-mass cost (appendicular, -0.47 kg, -0.82 to -0.12)
[@lowe2020]; a 12-month trial of different design, with
calorie restriction in both arms, did not
[@liu2022]. Neither trial tested why
([[Time-Restricted Eating]]).

**Coffee gives no health reason to start or to quit, outside pregnancy and raised fracture risk.** Poole's
umbrella review finds coffee safe within usual patterns «except during pregnancy and in women at increased
risk of fracture». Risk is lowest at three to four cups a day, with a smaller benefit beyond that; the
evidence is graded mostly low or very low, and Mendelian randomization finds no genetic support for a causal
effect [@poole2017]. One cheap switch holds: **someone
with high LDL who drinks a lot of unfiltered (boiled) coffee can switch to filtered**, because filtering
removes most of the cholesterol-raising effect
[@poole2017] ([[Coffee Consumption and Health]]).

**Ultra-processed food is an observational signal with candidate mechanisms, not a tested lever.** Its links
with disease and death are mostly low or very low certainty, type 2 diabetes moderate
[@lane2024upf]. In the one feeding trial, 20 adults ate 508
kcal/day more and gained 0.9 kg over 2 weeks on the ultra-processed diet; Hall names energy density and eating
rate as likely contributors for future study, and states that «no causal relationship between ultra-processed
food consumption and human obesity has yet been established»
[@hall2019]
([[Ultra-Processed Food and Health Outcomes]]).

**Red meat is a small lever, and processed meat is the better-evidenced half of it.** WCRF grades processed
meat as a convincing cause of higher colorectal cancer risk (RR 1.16, 1.08-1.26, per 50 g/day) and red meat as
a probable one, with a pooled estimate whose interval touches 1 (RR 1.12, 1.00-1.25, per 100 g/day)
[@wcrf2018]. Three fewer servings a week works
out at about 7 fewer cancer deaths per 1000 people over a lifetime, at low certainty
[@nutrirecs2019]. One cohort saw the red-meat
and diabetes link reverse between normal-weight and obese women
[@schwingshackl2017t2d]; that is a hypothesis,
not a rule by body weight ([[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]],
[[Red and Processed Meat and Cancer]]).

**Social isolation predicts earlier death, but no trial shows that reducing it changes the outcome.** Isolated
adults had higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.32, 1.26-1.39) in pooled cohorts
[@wang2023isolation]. Treat it as a marker
that flags other risks, not as a proven lever ([[Social Connection and Mortality]]).

**Women through menopause should watch the waist, not the scale.** Postmenopausal women had a 4.63 cm
(3.90-5.35) larger waist than premenopausal women in a pooled cross-sectional comparison. Bone and muscle loss
blur what BMI shows [@ambikairajah2019].
Hormone therapy treats symptoms and protects bone while it is taken. WHI's investigators concluded that
estrogen plus progestin should not be started or continued to prevent heart disease
[@rossouw2002]
([[Menopause and the Shifting Levers]], [[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]]).

### What the evidence cannot yet say

**This stratum's own numbers are missing.** The wiki holds no baseline cardiovascular risk for a late-40s
adult, because SCORE2's under-50 band has no held risk grid, so the absolute benefit of more movement or of
weight loss at this baseline is not held either ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]])
.

**Several outcomes are unmeasured.** Lifestyle weight loss is untested on heart attacks and strokes in
low-risk people without diabetes, and the wiki holds no apoB response to weight loss. For most levers,
trajectory and quality of life go unmeasured. Who benefits at a normal BMI with a large waist rests on the
personal fat threshold, the hypothesis that each person has a fat level above which fat spills into liver and
pancreas; no trial has tested weight loss on outcomes in that group
([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]], [[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]],
[[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]]).

**Harms and the long run are thin.** No study quantifies harm from these levers in the leaner minority of
this stratum; caution there is reasoned, not measured ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).
Long-run harm from intentional weight cycling has been neither detected
nor ruled out, and beyond the regain itself the health course after a drug or programme stops is unmeasured
([[Weight Cycling and Cardiometabolic Risk]], [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]])
.

**Some specifics await sources.** Waist and resting heart rate have no outcome-validated cutoffs here
([[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]], [[Central Adiposity and All-Cause Mortality]]). Protein
needs for obese adults in a deficit await a held review, and so does the survival gain from raising fitness
within a person
([[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]). DIAAS, the protein-quality score, is a surrogate with no held
outcome evidence, and scoring a realistic plant food set on it needs a food-composition database the wiki
does not hold ([[Protein Quality and the DIAAS Score]]).

**Fitness may keep paying where activity flattens.** In one referral cohort the elite-fitness group still did
better than the high-fitness group, HR 0.77 (0.63-0.95)
[@mandsager2018]. That is one cohort,
measuring fitness rather than minutes, so it does not reopen the activity ceiling.

## The direction matches mainstream guidance; the order and the certainty labels differ

On direction this cut agrees with the guidance bodies it draws on: move more, lose excess fat, do not smoke,
keep alcohol low, replace saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat, and cut salt where blood pressure is high.
It differs in three places. It ranks levers by effect size and certainty, so the composition questions sit
below movement and weight loss. It labels the lifestyle route's heart-attack benefit as untested in low-risk
adults. And it reports saturated fat's benefit on non-fatal events, with deaths unchanged. Population
guidance also weighs goals beyond one person's health; this cut does not ->
[[The Weighting Problem - Why Population Guidance Is Ill-Posed and Individual Advice Is Not]],
[[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]].

## Pull the large levers first, then stop

The numbers behind each step are in the sections above and on the linked pages.

- **Stop smoking**, if you smoke; it comes before everything else here ([[Smoking and Mortality]]).
- **Drink little.** The alcohol significance points are not a permitted ceiling, and cancer risk rises from
  low intake ([[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]], [[Alcohol and Cancer Risk]]).
- **Sleep longer if you now sleep about 5 hours**, toward about 7; standard advice, not trial-tested
  ([[Sleep and Metabolic Health]]).
- **Get off the floor of activity**: start with 5-10 extra minutes of walking a day, build toward about 20-40
  minutes of brisk activity, sit less, and add strength work on two or more days a week. The first step up
  from the least active band counts most ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]],
  [[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]], [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]).
- **Lose weight by eating less if your waist or metabolic profile is raised**, at least 5% of starting weight,
  and plan a year of maintenance from the start; some regain is normal
  ([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]], [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]).
- **Measure non-HDL-C and blood pressure, and act on your absolute risk**; with raised pressure, try three to
  six months of non-drug change first ([[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]],
  [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).
- **Replace butter with plant oils and cut sugary drinks** ([[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]],
  [[Free Sugars Intake]]).
- **Then stop optimizing.** Supplements, meal timing, coffee and the processed-food label add little on
  current evidence ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]).

Two findings would most change this list: a held baseline risk for adults in their late 40s, and a trial of
lifestyle weight loss on heart attacks and strokes in people without diabetes.

### Caveats

- This page grades how well its claims fit their sources, not whether they are true; no step here has been
  checked against what happened to anyone who followed it.
- It appraises evidence and does not prescribe: the drug sections select no drug and no dose.

- It is written for a stratum, and applying it to one person means weighing their own risks, goals and
  constraints.
