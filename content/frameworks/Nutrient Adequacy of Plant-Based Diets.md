**The decision.** For someone eating (or transitioning to) a vegetarian or vegan diet, *which* nutrients
actually need attention — through food pairing, fortification, or a supplement — and *for whom*? This is
the multi-nutrient generalization of the B12 question -> [[Vitamin B12 Status in Vegetarian and Vegan Diets]]: B12 is the sharpest single case, but a plant-based diet shifts the deficiency-risk profile of a
whole panel of nutrients at once. The single source held is a gold-tier systematic review of nutrient
**intake and status** in adults on plant-based vs meat-containing diets — 141 studies, mostly Europe /
South-East Asia / North America [@neufingerl2021plant]. It
reports **prevalence and biomarker facts, not effect estimates on any patient-important outcome** — the
same surrogate discipline the B12 page carries applies to every nutrient here -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]].

## Source COI — read the data, discount the framing (symmetric standards)

**Both authors are Unilever employees** (Unilever Foods Innovation Centre), and Unilever sells plant-based
products [@neufingerl2021plant]. The specific bias risk is an
*adequacy-favorable* tilt. Held to the same bar as a meat-industry-funded SR reaching the opposite
conclusion, the split is:

- **The empirical layer is the core and is even-handed.** The prevalence/status data cut against plant-based
  diets exactly where the biology says they should (B12, D, iron, zinc, iodine, calcium, EPA/DHA all lower),
  *and* honestly report meat-eaters at risk of fiber, PUFA, ALA, folate, vitamin E, D, calcium and
  magnesium: «There were nutrient inadequacies across all dietary patterns, including vegan, vegetarian and
  meat-based diets» [@neufingerl2021plant]. A tilted review
  would have buried the plant-diet deficits; this one tabulates them. Credit the data.
- **The interpretive layer runs ahead of this review's own evidence.** The abstract's framing sentence —
  «As plant-based diets are generally better for health and the environment, public health strategies
  should facilitate the transition...» [@neufingerl2021plant]
  — imports a health-and-environment verdict this review **did not measure**: it assessed nutrient
  biomarkers, not disease outcomes, and holds no environmental data at all. The "generally better for
  health" claim is borrowed from cited EAT-Lancet/GBD references, not produced here. Do **not** launder it:
  this review's *own* finding is that a plant-based diet creates several real deficiency risks that require
  active management, which is a narrower and more useful claim than the framing headline
.

The COI conditions the interpretive claims; it does not disqualify gold-design prevalence counts. The
non-health axis (environment) is **named and dropped**, never priced in here — the wiki holds no
environmental data (the *one axis, named* rule).

## The reframe — intake, bioavailability, and status are THREE different objects

The most decision-relevant move is a unit-of-analysis shift: **what you eat (intake) is not what you absorb
(bioavailability) is not what your blood shows (status)**, and on a plant-based diet these three can point
in *opposite* directions [@neufingerl2021plant]. Iron and
zinc are the clean demonstrations:

- **Iron — intake is HIGHER in plant diets, status is LOWER.** Vegans out-eat meat-eaters on iron (21.0 vs
  13.9 mg/d), yet serum ferritin runs the other way (meat 55.5 vs vegetarian 33.8 vs vegan 31.3 ug/L;
  vegetarian *women* particularly low at 24.3), with iron-deficiency prevalence 7% / 11% / 15% and anemia
  5% / 11% / 17% (meat / vegetarian / vegan) [@neufingerl2021plant]. The gap is bioavailability: «iron bioavailability in a plant-based diet is substantially lower
  (i.e., \~10%) compared to a diet with meat and fish containing heme iron (18%)... Heme iron is more
  efficiently absorbed (15-40%) than non-heme iron (1-15%) which is inhibited by phytates, polyphenols and
  proteins» [@neufingerl2021plant] -> [[Antinutrients in Plant Foods]]. IOM sets the plant-diet iron requirement **1.8x** higher for this reason.
- **Zinc — intake SIMILAR, status LOWER.** Mean zinc intake is comparable across patterns, but against the
  bioavailability-adjusted EAR (phytate inflates the requirement up to +50%) vegetarians and vegans fall
  short for men, status is lower (0.79-0.81 vs 0.90 mg/L), and deficiency reaches 30% in vegans vs 13-14%
  otherwise [@neufingerl2021plant].

**Decision consequence:** a plant-diet intake number that "meets the EAR" can still leave a real status
shortfall, so **an unadjusted intake comparison is the wrong instrument for iron and zinc** — use the
bioavailability-adjusted requirement, and prefer *status* (biomarker) over *intake* (self-report) wherever
both are reported, since self-reported intake carries the larger error -> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]. The zinc case also carries a counter-caveat the authors state plainly: «there are no reported
adverse health consequences in adult vegetarians with lower zinc status, suggesting that the efficiency of
zinc utilization may be increased in vegetarians on the longer term» [@neufingerl2021plant] — lower zinc status here is a surrogate whose transmission to harm is
itself unestablished, exactly the [[Surrogate Outcomes]] discipline.

## The at-risk panel, by stratum

Risk is **stratum-graded: vegan > vegetarian > meat-eater** for the animal-sourced nutrients, and reversed
for the plant-abundant ones. Magnitudes below are means benchmarked to the EAR (or AI/AMDR where noted),
plus deficiency-prevalence where a biomarker cutoff existed
[@neufingerl2021plant].

| Nutrient | Highest-risk stratum | Signal (intake vs requirement · status · deficiency %) | Why plant diets fall short |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin B12** | vegan >> vegetarian | intake vegan 1.5 / veg 2.1 / meat 5.6 ug/d (EAR 2.0, food-only vegan well below); deficiency \~44% vegan, \~32% vegetarian | essentially no active plant source |
| **EPA/DHA** | vegan, vegetarian | intake vegan 27/4, veg 16/31, meat 94/172 mg/d — below 250 mg AMDR; status consistently lower | fish/seafood absent; ALA->EPA/DHA conversion <10% |
| **Iodine** | vegan | intake vegan 111 / veg 146 / meat 170 ug/d (EAR 95); deficiency (UIC<100) **92% vegan** vs 37% veg vs 51% meat | few plant sources; only 5 studies (thin) |
| **Iron** | vegetarian/vegan **women** | intake higher but ferritin lower; deficiency 15% vegan / 11% veg / 7% meat; anemia up to 17% | non-heme, phytate-inhibited (see reframe) |
| **Zinc** | vegan | intake similar but below adjusted EAR; deficiency 30% vegan | phytate-inhibited absorption |
| **Vitamin D** | vegan (but ALL patterns) | intake below EAR (10 ug) in **every** pattern (vegan 1.52 -> pesco 5.25); deficiency 67% vegan / 33% veg in one small Finnish study | few natural sources; a whole-population gap |
| **Calcium / bone** | vegan | intake vegan lowest (838 mg, EAR 800); higher PTH + bone-turnover markers in veg/vegan, lower lumbar-spine BMD in vegans | lower intake + phytate/oxalate absorption drag |
| **Protein** | (older) vegans | lower %E (veg 13.4 / vegan 12.9 vs meat 16.0) but **all above the 10%E AMDR**; quality \~80-90% | lower digestibility; a concern mainly for older adults |

**The mirror finding (symmetric standards).** Meat-eaters are the at-risk stratum for the plant-abundant
nutrients — fiber, PUFA, ALA, folate, vitamin E, magnesium — and, alongside everyone else, vitamin D and
calcium [@neufingerl2021plant]. "Nutrient inadequacy" is not
a property of plant-based diets; it is a property of *any* non-diverse diet, differently distributed.

## What each risk actually licenses (the levers, not a prescription)

Mapping onto [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]]: these are **repletion-territory** cases (correcting a
real dietary shortfall in an identified stratum), not enhancement of the replete — so the lever can matter,
but the review holds no outcome effect, only the shortfall. The source's own tiering of remedies:

- **Fortification / supplementation (structural gap, no good food fix):** B12 (absent from plant food),
  vitamin D and iodine (few natural sources) — these need fortified foods, iodized salt, or supplements
  regardless of dietary pattern [@neufingerl2021plant]. B12 is
  the non-optional one -> [[Vitamin B12 Status in Vegetarian and Vegan Diets]]. Iodine cross-links the
  *deficiency* arm here, distinct from the *excess* arm -> [[Excess Iodine Intake and Thyroid Disease]] and
  the pregnancy stratum -> [[Iodine Supplementation in Pregnancy]].
- **Bioavailability / pairing (food is adequate, absorption is not):** iron and zinc — pair with vitamin-C
  foods, use soaking/fermentation to cut phytate, and count the adjusted requirement, not the raw intake.
- **Diet diversity (a real food fix exists):** calcium (leafy greens, pulses, fortified plant milks),
  EPA/DHA (algae oil the only direct plant source), fiber/vitamin E in the vegetarians who eat too few whole
  plant foods.

The *choice* among these stays the person's (layer 3); supplement selection/dosing needs this person's labs
and is a prescriber act out of scope.

## Relationship to Pawlak (B12) — type-F broadening, NOT independent corroboration

Neufingerl broadens the held B12 finding (Pawlak 2013) from one nutrient to a whole panel, and on B12
specifically it **adds the omnivore comparator Pawlak lacked** (Pawlak reported one-arm vegetarian
prevalences only). That is **type-F refinement/broadening, not type-E independent backing** — a wider
review filling a gap, not a second independent witness to the same quantity. The B12 parameter comparison
is built on the B12 page. No `[E-independent]` is claimed here
.

## Confidence and gaps

**Confidence `medium`** — gold-design SR, but a single source, all endpoints are surrogates (intake/status,
no patient-important outcome), and the industry COI conditions the framing (not the counts).

Key limitations and gaps (G):

- **No patient-important outcome anywhere.** Every finding is intake or biomarker status; whether correcting
  any of these shortfalls changes a hard endpoint is unheld. — outcome effect of correcting
  plant-diet nutrient shortfalls (iron/zinc/iodine/D/omega-3) on patient-important endpoints`
- **True inadequacy prevalence could not be estimated.** The authors compared *means* to the EAR rather than
  the proportion of the population below it: «A major limitation of our review was that we could not provide
  reliable estimations on the adequacy of dietary nutrient intakes» [@neufingerl2021plant]. Read the prevalence numbers as indicative, not exact — and this cuts
  *against* over-reading the deficits.
- **Iodine and EPA/DHA rest on very few studies** (iodine 5, EPA/DHA 8) — insufficient-evidence, not a firm
  parameter, despite the dramatic 92% vegan iodine-deficiency figure.
- **Western populations only**; results may not transport to other food systems.
- **Cutoff and measurement heterogeneity** drive part of every deficiency band (the same caveat that governs
  the B12 prevalence range).
