Cutting sodium lowers blood pressure. That is the best-established part of the whole
picture -- reliably true, and larger the higher a person's starting pressure. Sodium is
also one of the few nutrients that can be measured against a target rather than guessed at,
which makes the advice unusually auditable. But blood pressure is a means, not the end. It
matters because lowering it is a proven route to fewer strokes and heart attacks, so for a
person with elevated blood pressure or high cardiovascular risk, reducing sodium is a real
lever with a real payoff.

For a lean, normotensive, low-risk person the same cut moves blood pressure only a little,
so its absolute benefit is small -- and raising potassium does not lower an already-normal
pressure at all. The one strong trial that reached hard outcomes -- a potassium-enriched
salt substitute -- cut strokes and deaths, but it raised potassium as well as cutting
sodium, so the benefit belongs to the combined swap, not to sodium alone, and it ran in a
very high-risk group where absolute benefits are largest.

The recurring concern that very low intakes carry *higher* event rates in some cohorts has
never faced the study that would settle it, so it is unresolved rather than disproven;
meanwhile every reduction pays on blood pressure, and overshooting a hidden floor would fail
to help rather than harm. Raising potassium and cutting sodium are complementary, not
interchangeable -- but potassium is a harm, not a help, for anyone with impaired kidney
function or on potassium-raising drugs.

## 1. Sodium and blood pressure -- the high-certainty core

Reducing sodium lowers systolic blood pressure (SBP), and this is where the evidence is
strongest. WHO's pooled estimate from 36 randomised trials (49 comparisons, n=6736) is a
**3.39 mmHg** fall in resting SBP (95% CI 2.46 to 4.31), rated **HIGH** certainty -- with a
1.54 mmHg fall in diastolic pressure, also high certainty
[@who2012]. The published,
gold-tier RCT meta-analysis behind the same effect -- He, Li & MacGregor 2013, a Cochrane
review of 34 trials (n=3230) at a modest reduction sustained four or more weeks -- puts the
all-participants fall slightly higher at **-4.18 mmHg** SBP (95% CI -5.18 to -3.18)
[@he2013]. The two figures
differ because they pool different trial sets; both are large, precise, and point the same
way.

> «A modest reduction in salt intake for four or more weeks causes significant and, from a
> population viewpoint, important falls in blood pressure in both hypertensive and
> normotensive individuals, irrespective of sex and ethnic group.»
[@he2013]

The effect is not uniform: it is far larger the higher the starting pressure. He's stratified
figures are **-5.39 mmHg** in hypertensives (95% CI -6.62 to -4.15) against **-2.42 mmHg** in
normotensives (95% CI -3.56 to -1.29) -- a roughly 2.2:1 gap
[@he2013]. WHO's own
subgroups run steeper still: **4.06 mmHg** (95% CI 2.96 to 5.15, high certainty) in
hypertensives versus **1.38 mmHg** (95% CI 0.02 to 2.74, moderate certainty) in
normotensives -- close to a 3:1 ratio
[@who2012].

The coarse
hypertensive/normotensive split is a summary of what is really a **continuous** gradient in
baseline SBP, but the direction is unambiguous and holds even in people with normal pressure:
sodium reduction still lowers it, just by less, so the absolute payoff shrinks as starting
pressure falls. For scale, the public-health target is a cut to 5 g/day salt = 2 g/day sodium
(\~=87 mmol/24h; 1 g sodium \~= 43 mmol), and the pooled trials achieved reductions in that
neighbourhood.

What sets sodium apart from nearly every other dietary exposure in this evidence base is how
it is measured. 24-hour urinary sodium is a **recovery biomarker** -- a known fraction of
intake is excreted over a defined period, so it yields *absolute* intake rather than a mere
ranking [@willett]. Almost everything else
here rests on food-frequency questionnaires, which Willett positions as adequate for ranking
people but not for checking anyone against an absolute cutpoint. Sodium escapes that limit:
WHO's «<2 g/day» is one of the few guidance numbers in this whole corpus a person could
actually be measured against, which makes both the exposure and the target unusually
auditable [inferred from @willett; @who2012].

Two
caveats keep this honest -- the recovery biomarker fixes *calibration*, not confounding,
outcome measurement, or day-to-day variation (a single day's urine is still a poor estimate
of habitual intake), and it does nothing to raise the certainty on hard outcomes discussed in
the next section.

One thing the high certainty on the blood-pressure effect does **not** buy is independence.
WHO's guideline and the He/MacGregor meta-analyses share one research lineage: He and
MacGregor's own trials sit inside WHO's cited evidence base, and both authors are principals
of the salt-reduction advocacy charities CASH and WASH. The pooled blood-pressure effect is
hard RCT data and is taken at face value here, but the surface agreement between these
sources is not the corroboration of independent witnesses -- it is one body of primary work
reaching two committees, so it does not multiply the confidence
[inferred from @who2012; @he2013].

A fall in blood pressure is only worth having if it actually transmits into fewer strokes and
heart attacks -- so does it?

## 2. From blood pressure to events -- the chain, and where it holds

Blood pressure is not an outcome anyone cares about; it is a **surrogate** for the events that follow --
stroke, heart attack, cardiovascular death. GRADE restricts surrogate use precisely because a marker can
move while the outcome does not, so the load-bearing question is never *does sodium lower BP?* (it does)
but *does that BP fall transmit to fewer events?* [@grade] The sodium case
turns entirely on this step, and the step is stronger for the drug route than for the diet route.

### The transmission is proven -- for pharmacological BP-lowering

The largest randomised evidence base for the surrogate-to-event link is BPLTTC (344,716 people, 48
trials). A fixed **5 mmHg systolic reduction cut major cardiovascular events by about 10%** -- and,
decisively, it did so «irrespective of previous diagnoses of cardiovascular disease, and even at normal
or high-normal blood pressure values» [@bplttc2021]. The
hazard ratio per 5 mmHg was 0.91 (0.89-0.94) in people **without** prior CVD and 0.89 (0.86-0.92) in
those with it; across seven baseline-SBP strata «in no subgroup was the HR above 1.0», with stroke down
13%, heart failure 13%, ischaemic heart disease 8%, CV death 5%
[@bplttc2021].

So a randomised BP fall does buy fewer hard events, and the benefit **reaches primary prevention** -- it
does not require pre-existing disease. But BPLTTC's own message is not *lower everyone's BP*: physicians
should «emphasise its importance on reducing cardiovascular risk rather than focusing on blood pressure
reduction itself» [@bplttc2021]. A constant \~10%-per-5-mmHg
*relative* effect buys little absolute benefit at low risk and much at high risk, so the rule is **treat
on absolute cardiovascular risk, not to a blood-pressure number** -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

### But that warrant is the drug's, and sodium borrows it

The direct evidence that cutting *sodium* reduces hard outcomes is far thinner. In WHO's own profile every
sodium-to-outcome row is **cohort-only and rated VERY LOW** -- stroke RR 1.24 (1.08-1.43), all-cause
mortality RR 1.06 (0.94-1.20, inconclusive), CVD and CHD both null
[@who2012]. The recommendation does not rest on those
rows. It rests on a **disclosed surrogate transfer**: WHO takes its high-certainty BP evidence, downgrades
it one level, and treats it as «also considered moderate-quality evidence that reduced sodium is
beneficial for reducing risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke and coronary heart disease»
[@who2012].

The BPLTTC-style transmission model is doing the work -- but applied to a **sodium-induced** BP fall,
which is a different intervention than the drugs BPLTTC pooled. That the surrogate transmits under a drug
does not, on its own, prove it transmits under a dietary change; it is a licensed inference, not the same
evidence -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]].

### The certainty inversion is a feature of what is measurable, not a grading flaw

Note the shape: **BP graded High, hard outcomes graded Very low**, in the same document, on the same
exposure. This is not WHO grading badly. A blood-pressure fall can be randomised, blinded to the analyst,
and read off in weeks in a few hundred people; a stroke-or-death outcome needs years of follow-up in tens
of thousands, and rarely admits a clean dietary trial at all. **Certainty tracks measurability, not
importance** -- the best-known quantity is the one nobody chooses on directly, and the outcomes a person
would actually weight are known least well.

The same inversion appears on saturated fat (LDL graded High, every disease outcome Low or Very low) ->
[[Surrogate Outcomes]]. Two exposures in two WHO guidelines is not yet a law -- it is a **hypothesis about
this literature**: wherever a nutrient acts through a measurable intermediate, the intermediate will be
better graded than the endpoint, structurally, because the designs that grade well are the ones the
intermediate admits. A third body grading a third exposure the same way would promote it from hypothesis
to property.

### A forward pointer on the J-curve

The blood-pressure J-curve itself has been tested and refuted interventionally -- but that is a
different object from the low-sodium-intake question, taken up in §5 -> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

The one place the corpus tests a dietary salt route end-to-end, on hard outcomes rather than on the
surrogate, did so by moving sodium and potassium *together* -- which is exactly where the electrolyte
tangle begins.

## 3. The salt-substitute trial -- hard outcomes, and the confound that defines it

Everything above rests on blood pressure -- a surrogate. SSaSS (Neal 2021, NEJM) is the corpus's
cleanest hard-outcome RCT of a dietary salt route: an open-label cluster trial in 600 rural-Chinese
villages, 20,995 adults with prior stroke or age >=60 with high BP, followed 4.74 years, events
adjudicated blind to assignment. It replaced regular salt with a **potassium-enriched salt substitute
(75% NaCl / 25% KCl)** and cut the outcomes that matter:

| Outcome | Rate ratio (95% CI) | Absolute |
|---|---|---|
| **Stroke** (primary) | **0.86 (0.77-0.96)**, P=0.006 | -4.5 / 1000 py |
| **Major adverse CV events** | **0.87 (0.80-0.94)**, P<0.001 | -7.2 / 1000 py |
| **All-cause death** | **0.88 (0.82-0.95)**, P<0.001 | -5.3 / 1000 py |

[@neal2021]

### The confound that defines the trial: this is a joint Na-down/K-up lever

The substitute moved **two electrolytes at once**, and it raised potassium *more* than it cut sodium.
The achieved 24-hour urinary contrast was sodium **-15.2 mmol** (-350 mg), potassium **+20.6 mmol**
(+803 mg), SBP **-3.34 mmHg**. [@neal2021]

So SSaSS is strong evidence that a **salt substitute** reduces stroke, cardiovascular events and death
in a high-risk stratum -- it is **not** clean evidence that *sodium reduction on its own* delivers that
benefit. Sodium and potassium each lower BP and act together, so the \~12-14% relative reductions are a
**joint effect**. SSaSS adjudicates the salt-substitution **lever**, not sodium alone and not potassium
alone. The per-electrolyte split, and which Na:K ratio is optimal, stay open (sections 4 and 7): the
sodium-BP channel and the potassium channel must not be summed, and neither electrolyte can claim this
benefit for itself.
[inferred from @neal2021]

### The large absolute effect rides on an extreme baseline risk (route a)

The absolute reductions are large (-4.5 to -7.2 events per 1000 person-years) because the population is
extreme: **72.6% had a prior stroke**, mean BP 154/89, mean age 65. The relative reductions may
transport to a lower-risk person; the **absolute** benefit will not. Absolute benefit is the relative
reduction times baseline risk, so a lean, normotensive, low-stroke-risk person switching salts gets a
far smaller absolute effect from the same intervention -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].
SSaSS earns a strong recommendation *for its own high-risk stratum*, not a universal one.
[inferred from @neal2021]

The two electrolytes therefore have to be pulled apart -- and potassium, it turns out, behaves
differently from sodium.

## 4. Potassium -- a different lever with a different shape

Cutting sodium is one arm of the BP/stroke story; raising potassium is the other -- but it is not a mirror image. Aburto's WHO-commissioned SR+MA (22 RCTs for BP, measured by 24-hour urinary excretion; 11 cohorts for hard outcomes) grades the BP effect **HIGH**: raising potassium lowers resting SBP by 3.49 mmHg (95% CI 1.82 to 5.15). But the effect is not a weak average -- it is a strong effect in one stratum diluted by a flat zero in the other [@aburto2013].

### The lever only fires in hypertensives -- the asymmetry with sodium

Stratified, the split is unusually clean: SBP falls 5.32 mmHg (3.43 to 7.20) in the 16 hypertensive-targeted trials, but only 0.09 mmHg (-0.77 to 0.95) in the normotensive trials -- **essentially zero, not merely smaller** (a route-b effect-modification finding) [@aburto2013]. This is the asymmetry with sodium: sodium reduction moves BP in **both** strata (larger in hypertensives, but \~-1.4 to -2.4 mmHg and significant in normotensives too), whereas potassium moves only the hypertensive. So *cut sodium* and *raise potassium* are not symmetric advice -- for a normotensive person the sodium lever still works and the potassium lever, on current evidence, does not. (Aburto flags the three normotensive trials as too short to rule out long-run *prevention* of a future rise.)

Food is a sufficient vehicle -- fruit, vegetables, and pulses. This is **potassium repletion**, not *supplementation*; no upper limit is set for dietary intake [@aburto2013].

### The hard outcome and the dose

Stroke is the one hard cohort endpoint that reaches significance: **RR 0.76** (0.66 to 0.89), graded **LOW** (cohort floor, direct estimate). CVD, CHD, and mortality all stay null and underpowered [@aburto2013]. There is no absolute risk or NNT, so the RR cannot yet be ranked against another exposure; absolute benefit scales with baseline stroke risk -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

On dose, the largest BP fall sits at **90-120 mmol/day, with intake above 120 buying no additional benefit** and no clean monotone gradient below it [@aburto2013]. Read with the dose-response caution, the defensible statement is a minimum effective **region** around 90 mmol/day with no demonstrated benefit above 120 -- the low figure a knee, the high figure where the data thin, not a proven ceiling -> [[Dose-Response]].

### Safety -- null in the healthy, but a real contraindication

Aburto found potassium null on lipids, catecholamines, and serum creatinine, all graded **HIGH** -- no adverse effect in people with normal potassium handling [@aburto2013]. But the benefit is **scoped to normal renal handling**: in chronic kidney disease, in others at hyperkalaemia risk, or on potassium-sparing diuretics, the same exposure becomes a harm (a route-c contraindication). SSaSS supplies an RCT safety datum -- no excess serious hyperkalaemia -- but only in a population screened to exclude known kidney disease, so the contraindication is **confirmed, not lifted**. Flagged here, developed fully in [Section 6](#6).

### The Na:K ratio -- asserted, not graded

Raising potassium to >=90 mmol/day alongside sodium <2 g/day yields a molar Na:K ratio near **1:1**, «considered beneficial» -- but that figure is sourced to a 2003 WHO/FAO consultation, **not graded in this review**, and the joint-intervention evidence is a handful of studies, not a meta-analysis. The two levers do reinforce (potassium works best at higher sodium intake), and SSaSS is now a hard-outcome RCT of *raising K and cutting Na at once* -- but SSaSS used a single 75% NaCl / 25% KCl formulation, so **it cannot credit the stroke/CV benefit to potassium alone, and it says nothing about which ratio is optimal**. The ratio as a dose-optimised target stays open [@aburto2013].

### DASH -- a whole-pattern lever, not a sodium lever

The DASH diet lowers SBP \~**-5.2 mmHg** vs control (Siervo, 20 RCTs) -- but its BP effect is **not** driven by its sodium content: the between-arm sodium difference did not predict the BP change (SBP meta-regression P=0.67) [@siervo2015]. So **do not double-count**: DASH's -5.2 mmHg is a whole-pattern effect and must never be added to a sodium-reduction number. The two stack only when deliberately combined -- the DASH-Sodium factorial design, where a sodium cut *on top of* DASH adds further benefit.

Granting that both levers work, the next question is the shape of the sodium curve itself: is there a dose below which cutting further stops paying -- and could cutting too far do harm?

## 5. Dose-response shape -- every reduction pays, no demonstrated plateau, and the low-sodium concern

Two questions hide behind one curve, and they are different objects. **Does more sodium reduction
keep lowering blood pressure (the dose-response shape)?** And **does cutting sodium too far raise
hard-outcome risk (the low-sodium J-curve)?** The first is answered; the second is not. Keeping them
apart is the whole discipline of this section.

### Part A -- the blood-pressure dose-response is monotone, but visible only after adjustment

Over the studied range, larger sodium reductions buy larger blood-pressure falls -- with a catch that
matters for how much to trust the shape. In Huang's 133-RCT pool the raw contrast is **null**: neither
the by-quintile trend nor the univariable meta-regression found any link between the size of the sodium
cut and the size of the blood-pressure fall.

> «The meta-analysis of trials by group of achieved sodium reduction (fig 2) identified no clear
> association between the magnitude of sodium reduction and magnitude of either the systolic blood
> pressure reduction (P trend=0.09) or the diastolic blood pressure reduction (P trend=0.63). Likewise,
> the univariable meta-regression analyses including all studies (table 1) showed no association of
> blood pressure effect with magnitude of sodium reduction.»
[@huang2020]

The positive slope appears **only after** adjusting for trial duration, age, sex, ethnicity and
baseline blood pressure (each 50 mmol/day cut then buys about -1.1 mmHg systolic), and the effect
persists down to baseline systolic below 120 mmHg -- so no plateau is located in the studied range.
[@huang2020]

**The asymmetry is what makes the honest claim conservative.** Dietary measurement error and the
flat-slope compression it produces can *hide* a knee -- they pull extreme intakes toward the mean and
shorten the exposure axis -- but they **cannot manufacture** one. So a measured-monotone curve is only
weak evidence of a truly monotone one; the safe reading is *no plateau has been shown*, with the burden
on whoever asserts a knee to locate it -> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]].

A guideline cutpoint carries the same caution. WHO's «<2 g/day sodium» is first a hypothesis that the
number marks the **edge of the evidence**, not a feature of the curve -- an absolute intake a person
can be measured against, but not an established optimum. The operative default therefore holds: **every
reduction pays on blood pressure**, and misjudging the exact shape costs little where the curve is flat.
[@who2012]

### Part B -- the low-sodium J-curve for HARD outcomes: named, never adjudicated

The separate worry is a J-shaped curve on *hard outcomes* -- that cutting sodium below roughly 2 g/day
might *raise* cardiovascular or mortality risk. The corpus holds three postures on this arm, and **none
of them runs the strong, confounder-immune check** the question requires.

- **WHO names it, then cannot answer it.** A J-shaped hypothesis (harm below \~2 g/day) was cited as a
  reason to commission the 2012 review, but WHO never returned to those studies, and excluded *a priori*
  the strata where a lower-arm harm is most plausible (heart failure, type-1 diabetes, anyone prone to
  hyponatraemia). So the guideline is structurally unable to resolve it.
  [@who2012]
- **He and MacGregor rebut it -- from advocacy, without the check.** They dismiss the J-curve cohort
  papers as flawed by measurement error, uncontrolled confounding and reverse causation -- exactly the
  recipe [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]] names. But this is a *critique* from CASH/WASH
  salt-reduction advocates, not an adjudication: no referent-correction and no genetic instrument on the
  low-intake arm. [@he2013]
- **GBD encodes the split as uncertainty rather than resolving it.** Needing an optimal intake to
  compute burden, the consortium -- with no salt-advocacy stake -- widened the band instead of picking a
  point:

> «To reflect the uncertainty in existing evidence on optimal level of intake for sodium, 1--5 g per day
> was considered as the uncertainty range for the optimal level of sodium where less than 2.3 g per day
> is the intake level of sodium associated with the lowest level of blood pressure in randomised
> controlled trials and 4--5 g per day is the level of sodium intake associated with the lowest risk of
> cardiovascular disease in observational studies.»
[@afshin2019]

The blood-pressure-minimising intake (RCTs, <2.3 g) and the event-minimising intake (observational,
4-5 g) point to *different* optima -- which is what a hard-outcome J-curve would produce. **The
strong check has not been run.** So the low-sodium hard-outcome arm is **unadjudicated: neither
confirmed nor disproved**, and it is the wiki's largest open question on sodium.

### The contrast that must NOT be transferred -- two J-curves, two verdicts

There is a cleanly *refuted* J-curve here, but it is a **different object**. The *blood-pressure*
J-curve -- registries showing lowest event risk near 130/75, read as a reason not to lower pressure far
-- was overturned **interventionally**: across 48 trials and 344,716 people, a fixed 5 mmHg systolic
reduction cut major cardiovascular events by about 10% «even at normal or high-normal blood pressure
values», with no rising hazard in any low-pressure stratum.
[@bplttc2021]
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]

That refutation concerns pharmacological lowering to a low blood-pressure **value** -- it says nothing
about dietary sodium reduction to a low **intake**. The two must not be conflated: **the BP J-curve
verdict does not transfer to the sodium hard-outcome arm**, which stays unadjudicated. The decision
default is unchanged and rests on the Part-A asymmetry: **every reduction pays on blood pressure**, and
because measurement can only hide a floor rather than create harm, overshooting a hidden low-sodium
floor would most likely **fail to help, not harm**.

If the size of the lever varies this much -- substantial where blood pressure and baseline risk are
high, near-flat for someone already low on both -- the operative question is not *how far to cut in
general* but **for whom the cut actually changes an outcome**.

## 6. For whom -- baseline blood pressure, absolute vs relative benefit, and the potassium contraindication

### The relative BP effect is larger in hypertensives -- but the real modifier is continuous

Three estimates from one lineage put the SBP fall from sodium reduction at roughly 3:1 in hypertensives versus normotensives: He 2013 -5.39 vs -2.42 mmHg (ratio \~2.2) [@he2013], WHO 2012 4.06 vs 1.38 (\~2.9) [@who2012], ESC 2021 5.8 vs 1.9 (\~3.0) [@esc2021]. But the two-box split is a coarsening. Huang 2020 finds the hypertensive/normotensive dichotomy non-significant (P trend=0.08) while the continuous baseline-SBP gradient is significant (P trend=0.01), with a real effect even below SBP 120 [@huang2020]. So the modifier is a graded baseline pressure, not two categories; the 3:1 ratio is a summary of a slope.

### Absolute benefit tracks baseline CV risk -- route (a), no subgroup claim needed

The relative effect is only half of what a person weighs. Absolute benefit = relative reduction x baseline risk, so a constant relative effect delivers a large absolute benefit to a high-risk person and a small one to a lean normotensive -- route (a) baseline-risk stratification, which needs no effect-modification claim [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. SSaSS shows this concretely: its large absolute reductions ride on an extreme stratum. The \~12-14% *relative* reductions may transport to a lower-risk person, but the *absolute* benefit shrinks with baseline risk. Where a personal baseline CV risk is read off: [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].

### Salt sensitivity -- a stratifier in principle, absent from the held evidence

*Salt sensitivity* (*hyperresponders*) would be a natural person-level stratifier for who benefits most from cutting sodium. But the term does not appear in the held guidelines -- WHO 2012's own absence audit records that it does not occur, and the hypertensive/normotensive split is the closest the document comes [inferred from @who2012]. So this is a named GAP, not a lever the fabric can apply: no held source measures salt sensitivity or assigns it a direction. Do not infer one.

### The contraindication stratum -- where a salt substitute or potassium load is a harm (route (c))

Cutting sodium via a potassium-enriched salt substitute -- or raising potassium generally -- is a *harm* in one stratum: impaired potassium excretion. Aburto scopes potassium's safety to normal renal handling; in chronic kidney disease, on potassium-sparing diuretics, or with potassium supplements, the same exposure risks hyperkalaemia [@aburto2013] -> [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]. SSaSS's reassuring hyperkalaemia null (no excess; RR 1.04, 0.80-1.37) holds only for its *screened* population: it excluded known serious kidney disease, potassium-sparing diuretics and K-supplements by history, did no biochemical renal prescreening, and did not measure serum potassium serially [@neal2021]. So the null does not transfer to unscreened CKD -- the contraindication is confirmed, not lifted.

Given this much variation -- by baseline blood pressure, by baseline CV risk, by renal stratum -- what do the guidelines actually put a number on, and can a person act on it?

## 7. The guideline target -- WHO <2 g/day, what moved it, and what it can't tell you

**WHO recommends reducing sodium to <2 g/day (5 g/day salt) in adults, a STRONG recommendation.**
The word *conditional* does not appear in the 2012 document.

> «WHO recommends a reduction in sodium intake to reduce blood pressure and risk of cardiovascular
> disease, stroke and coronary heart disease in adults (strong recommendation). WHO recommends a
> reduction to <2 g/day sodium (5 g/day salt) in adults (strong recommendation).»
> [@who2012]

**But the strength does not rest on a directly-assessed threshold-vs-hard-outcome trial.** For the
specific <2 g/day question, WHO's evidence table carries four rows reading *"no studies... addressed
this question"* on cardiovascular disease, stroke, coronary heart disease and all-cause mortality --
each labelled **(directly assessed)**. The recommendation is built instead from **high-certainty
blood-pressure evidence at that threshold, transferred one certainty level down to hard outcomes,
plus 5 indirect cohort comparisons** (a significant stroke effect, RR 1.30, the others null)
[@who2012].

**So read <2 g/day first as the EDGE of the evidence, and carry its uncertainty.** The number is a
surrogate-transfer plus indirect-cohort construction, not a measured optimum, and WHO itself flags
that these recommendations *"should be reviewed when more evidence... becomes available"*
[@who2012]. The hard-outcome certainty behind the threshold
sits at **very low / moderate-by-transfer**, never high -> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**WHO measured a \~3-fold weaker BP response in normotensives (roughly 4.06 vs 1.38 mmHg) and then
refused to soften the target for them.** The stated reason is the high global prevalence of
hypertension and the breadth of benefit -- **a public-health-standpoint reason, not an evidentiary
one** [@who2012]. A population strategy is a
legitimate different-standpoint divergence (class 1), **not a process defect**: name the axis and
stop -> [[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]]. The wiki records *that* a non-health objective
set the uniform target; it does not price it against the health finding.

**Population intake sits at roughly twice the target.** ESC records Western salt intake at *"9-10
g/day, whereas the recommended maximum intake is 5 g/day"* -- **a population-wide halving**
[@esc2021]. This is an **achievability / adherence**
fact, not an efficacy one, and it belongs in any realistic reading of what the recommendation asks --
but it changes nothing about whether the reduction works.

**One caveat on how much authority stands behind the number.** WHO, ESC, He, Huang and Aburto largely
share **one He/MacGregor (CASH/WASH) research lineage** -- their agreement is *not* independent
corroboration, and their interpretive extensions (a 3 g/day target, the J-curve dismissal) are
advocacy-shaped and read at face value only for the data, never as multiplied authority
-> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**The one genuinely useful operational point: <2 g/day is auditable in a way most guidance numbers
are not.** Because sodium is a recovery-biomarker exposure (Section 1), <2 g/day is one of the few
guidance numbers in this corpus a person can actually be measured against -- via 24-hour urinary sodium
-> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]].

### What the evidence does not settle

State these plainly as **absence of evidence**, not as evidence of a direction:

1. **Sodium alone -> hard outcomes.** No held meta-analysis isolates sodium reduction on
   cardiovascular events apart from the Na+K confound: the corpus's cleanest hard-outcome RCT (SSaSS)
   moved both electrolytes at once, so its benefit cannot be attributed to sodium reduction per se.
2. **The low-sodium J-curve.** No Mendelian-randomization or bias-corrected analysis is held that
   resolves whether the low-intake hard-outcome arm is causal or artifact. WHO names it and cannot
   answer; He rebuts it from advocacy without the strong check; GBD encodes it as a 1-5 g uncertainty
   band. None runs the confounder-immune adjudication -> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].
3. **Salt-sensitivity as a usable stratum.** The terms do not occur in the held guidelines; there is
   no evidenced way to identify responders in advance.
4. **The optimal Na/K ratio.** The 1:1 figure is an ungraded 2003 expert consultation, and SSaSS
   tested a single 75/25 formulation -- so no ratio optimum is evidenced against a hard outcome.
5. **Transportability.** The RCT base is largely Western; SSaSS is rural-China, high-risk. Transport
   to LMIC settings or to a low-risk stratum is not directly evidenced.

### Scope

**OUT of scope:** the clinical management of diagnosed hypertension; antihypertensive drug selection
or titration; and setting a specific per-person gram target -- a prescriber act requiring this
person's labs, medications and history, which the wiki does not hold. Diagnosis and acute care are
also out. This section is a **population-level appraisal of a modifiable exposure**, not a treatment
protocol: it says which outcomes reducing sodium moves, in whom, and with what certainty, and leaves
the individualized target to a clinician with the missing materials.
