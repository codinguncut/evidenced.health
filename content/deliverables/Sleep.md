## §1 — Verdict

The one thing worth acting on is chronic short sleep. Sleeping habitually under about
7 hours — and the signal concentrates below 5-6 — tracks a real, mechanism-backed rise in
mortality (\~12%), incident type 2 diabetes, and a small dementia risk. Pushing a
chronically short sleeper back toward the 7-8 hour reference is the actionable move.


Do not read the other half of the curve the same way. The duration-mortality curve is
U-shaped, but its two arms are not the same finding. Sleeping *long* is a marker of
occult illness, not a cause of death — no mechanism, higher heterogeneity, and a risk that
climbs with age and illness. A habitual long sleeper is a work-up candidate, not a
sleep-reduction target. "Sleep less to live longer" has no support.

And the 7-8 hour figure is a referent, not a validated target with a ceiling. There is no
evidenced harm at the upper end, so no cap; the short arm is pooled and flat across
cutoffs, so the floor is arguably \~6+ hours, not a hard 7; and the curve is shallow near
the nadir. Precision about the exact hour carries no decision.

A second knob may matter more than the first. How *regular* your sleep timing is out-
predicted how many hours you slept for mortality in the one cohort that measured both
objectively — on thinner, single-cohort evidence. For someone already sleeping enough at
chaotic times, stabilising the schedule is a lever the hours-based advice misses.

Once insomnia is present, the treatment order inverts the usual reach: CBT-I first,
melatonin as a benign adjunct, prescription hypnotics short-course and last. The ordering
follows evidence type, durability and harm — not the effect number — and every aid
endpoint is a surrogate: no trial shows treating insomnia lowers mortality.

As a lever, sleep is moderate — below the big rocks (smoking, weight, blood pressure,
activity), above most refinements. It matters most once those are handled, or for the
badly sleep-deprived. What sits *upstream* of sleep — light, schedule, late meals,
caffeine timing — and whether quality at equal hours matters are candidate levers the
evidence cannot yet size. Named, not guessed.

Confidence is medium overall, and uneven across the legs:

| Leg | Evidence state | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Short-sleep harm (mortality / T2D / dementia) | benefit-of-correction, directional | **medium** |
| Long-sleep arm = artifact, not a target | negative verdict, well-adjudicated | **high** |
| "7-8 h" is a referent, no upper cap | negative verdict on the cap | **high** |
| Regularity as a distinct / stronger lever | benefit-association | **low** (one cohort) |
| Aid ordering (CBT-I > melatonin > hypnotics) | benefit + harm-signal, on surrogates | **medium** |
| Upstream levers; continuity / architecture | insufficient evidence | named gaps |

One caveat binds the whole page: sleep-duration exposure is self-reported and measured
once, which flattens real gradients, and the loop is open — no study here grades whether
changing someone's sleep improved their outcomes. Read the numbers as calibrated
associations.

## §2 — The U-curve: two arms, one causal, one a marker of illness

Sleep and mortality trace a U. Risk is lowest around a 7-8 h nadir and rises at
both ends. But the two arms of that U are not the same kind of finding, and reading
them as one — *any deviation from 7-8 h shortens life* — is the error this section
exists to prevent. Cappuccio's pooled meta-analysis (16 studies, 27 cohorts,
1.38 million people, 112,163 deaths) sets the reference at 7-8 h and separates the
arms cleanly.

| Arm (vs 7-8 h reference) | Pooled RR (95% CI) | Heterogeneity |
|---|---|---|
| **Short sleep** (commonly <7 h, often <5 h) | 1.12 (1.06-1.18) | I2 = 39% |
| **Long sleep** (commonly >8-9 h) | 1.30 (1.22-1.38) | I2 = 71% |

[@cappuccio2010]

The long-sleep arm is the *larger* association and the *weaker* claim. It is the
reverse-causation case of [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]] — a marker of occult
ill-health, not a dose of sleep that shortens life. Three tells give it away, each one
the diagnostic that concept says to look for:

- it is the larger association (1.30 vs 1.12) yet the one with **no mechanism**;
- it carries much **higher heterogeneity** (I2 71% vs 39%);
- it **strengthens with age and with a longer cutoff** — >=60 y RR 1.46 vs <60 y 1.22,
  rising to RR 1.54 at >=10 h. That is a frailty gradient, not a physiological
  dose-response.

[@cappuccio2010]

Cappuccio's own reading is explicit: no study has shown a mechanism by which long
sleep *causes* death, and «long duration of sleep may be a powerful additional marker
of ill-health».
[@cappuccio2010]
The dementia literature reproduces the split on a fresh outcome. In a Swedish cohort
the long-sleep/dementia link «was completely attenuated after cases occurring in the
first 5 years of follow-up were excluded» — the fingerprint of reverse causation — and
the 2024 Lancet Commission concludes «prolonged sleep is not a risk factor for
dementia, although dementia and its prodrome may cause prolonged sleep».
[@livingston2024]

The short arm sits on firmer ground. It carries a named mechanism (curtailed sleep
degrades glycemic control and raises cardiovascular risk) and a direction check:
in Whitehall II, people who *curtailed* their sleep died predominantly of
cardiovascular causes (2.04), while those who *lengthened* it died mainly of
non-cardiovascular causes (2.06) — exactly the pattern if short sleep is causal and
long sleep tracks illness.
[@cappuccio2010]

So the rule is *adjudicate the arm, not the curve* -> [[Sleep Duration and Mortality]].
The consequence for the long arm is fixed and it runs opposite to the folk reading:
a habitual long sleeper is a **work-up candidate for occult illness, not a
sleep-reduction target**. Shortening their sleep treats a symptom and leaves the cause.


## §3 — "7-8 h" is a referent, not a target with an upper bound

The 7-8 h figure is a reference point the cohorts anchor on, not a validated optimum
with a ceiling. Three honest limits keep it from hardening into a bare cutpoint.

**The short arm is pooled and flat across cutoffs, so 6-7 h is not resolved from
chronic <5 h.** Cappuccio's short-sleep category bundles 6-7 h together with chronic
<5 h, and the short-arm RR is flat across every cutoff definition (heterogeneity
P=0.89). The 1.12 is therefore the *pooled* short-sleep risk, not evidence that 6-7 h
specifically carries it. The risk most plausibly concentrates at chronic short sleep
(<5-6 h), which puts the actionable lower bound at roughly **6+ h**, not a hard 7 h.
[@cappuccio2010]

**There is no evidenced harm at the upper end, so "up to 8 h" is not a ceiling.** The
long-sleep association is the reverse-causation artifact of §2 — it has no mechanism
and strengthens with age and illness — not evidence that 8+ h harms a healthy person.
Stating an upper cap would launder that artifact into a dose-response bound, the exact
error the arm-level rule forbids.

**Between roughly 7 and 8 h is most plausibly diminishing returns, not a knee or a
harm boundary.** The curve is shallow near the referent, so precision beyond "adequate,
not chronically short" is not warranted by this evidence — and neither is a stated upper
cap. Carry the confidence interval and the studied range; state no bare number.
[inferred from @cappuccio2010]

One measurement caveat travels with every duration figure here: exposure is
self-reported and single-timepoint, so gradients are attenuated and thresholds inherit
that error -> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]].

## §4 — What short sleep does: mortality, diabetes, and the mechanism that does not hold

Take the short arm outcome by outcome. The pattern is consistent — a real,
mechanism-backed signal of moderate size — and its causal reading does *not* rest on
the appetite-hormone story most often told about it.

**Mortality.** Chronic short sleep carries a \~12% higher all-cause mortality (RR 1.12,
1.06-1.18), small for an observational metric but believed over the long arm because it
carries a mechanism and the Whitehall direction check, not because 12% is itself
compelling.
[@cappuccio2010]

**Type 2 diabetes.** Shan pooled 10 prospective cohorts (18,443 incident cases among
482,502 people): «A U-shaped dose-response relationship was observed between sleep
duration and risk of type 2 diabetes, with the lowest risk observed at a sleep duration
category of 7-8 h per day», the short arm running RR 1.09 (1.04-1.15) per 1-h shorter
sleep.
[@shan2015]
Against a US incidence of 7.6/1,000/yr that is roughly **68 extra cases per 100,000 per
year** for each hour lost. The short arm shows no nonlinearity (P=0.22) and a
human-corroborated glucose/insulin mechanism; the long arm's mechanism is on Shan's own
account «currently considered more speculative».
[@shan2015]

**Dementia.** The short-sleep arm keeps a small, mechanism-backed risk — the
million-woman study reports RR 1.08 (1.04-1.12) below 7 h, and persistent <=6 h sleep
across midlife a \~30% higher dementia risk.
[@livingston2024]
But the 2024 Lancet Commission judged the evidence not yet clear enough and **declined
to make any sleep-duration recommendation** — an honest insufficient-evidence stop the
wiki mirrors, distinct from a finding of no effect.

**The RCT direction-fix — and its limit.** An observational U-curve cannot prove that
short sleep *causes* anything, since illness lowers sleep. Capers meta-analyzed RCTs
that *manipulated* sleep, fixing the direction by design: «sleep restriction increases
food intake and total energy expenditure with inconsistent effects on integrated
energy balance as operationalized by weight change».
[@capers2015]
Restricting sleep reliably raises food intake — the causal link the cohorts cannot
establish. But the weight ledger stays open: sleep-extension trials showed a modest
weight benefit (p=0.02, I2=35%), while restriction gave «no significant effect overall
(p=0.08)» because expenditure rose alongside intake.
[@capers2015]
So sleep is **not** a demonstrated weight-loss lever; its firmer metabolic signal is on
glucose, not the scale.

**The leptin/ghrelin refinement.** The popular explanation — short sleep lowers leptin
and raises ghrelin, so appetite climbs — is downgraded, not banked. Capers found no
pooled RCT effect on either hormone (leptin p=0.84, ghrelin p=0.83), citing Chaput and
St-Onge that «hormones such as ghrelin and leptin are not the main mechanism
contributing to increased food intake during sleep restriction ... hedonic factors are
likely stronger».
[@capers2015]
Intake rises through hedonic drive and simply more waking hours in which to eat — not
through the hormonal surrogate. Route the appetite-hormone axis as a discounted
mechanism; the short-arm causal reading rests on the glucose/insulin pathway and
Whitehall, never on it -> [[Sleep and Metabolic Health]].

These three sleep sources are not independent witnesses. Shan, Cappuccio and Livingston
draw on overlapping cohorts and a shared reverse-causation lineage, so each is a
**type-F** refinement on a new outcome, not independent backing that would raise
confidence on its own.


## §5 — Regularity is a distinct knob, and may beat duration

How many hours you sleep is only one dial. Sleep has at least three separable axes, and a person can score well on one and badly on another:

- **duration** — hours slept, the axis the guidelines and the U-curve above measure;
- **regularity** — the night-to-night consistency of your sleep and wake times, scored by the **Sleep Regularity Index (SRI)**;
- **continuity** — how consolidated a single night is, versus fragmentation into brief wakings.

Duration and regularity are not the same question, and until recently the second one had no large objective test. Windred supplied it -> [[Sleep Regularity and Mortality]].

### The head-to-head, measured objectively

Windred put both dials on the same scale in one cohort. Sleep was measured by **wrist accelerometry**, not self-report, in 60 977 UK Biobank participants, then duration and regularity were compared directly as predictors of death. [@windred2023]

The more regular sleepers lived longer. Across the top four SRI quintiles, all-cause mortality ran **20%-48% lower** than in the least-regular quintile. [@windred2023] Two features make this more than another duration result:

- **Regularity was the stronger predictor.** Fully-adjusted top-quintile hazard ratio: SRI **0.70 (0.59-0.83)** versus duration **0.76 (0.65-0.89)**. Adding duration to an SRI model did not improve the fit — «sleep duration does not explain significant additional variance in mortality risk beyond the variance explained by SRI scores». [@windred2023]
- **The SRI-mortality curve is monotonic** — more regular, lower risk, with no U-turn. Unlike duration, regularity carries no upper-arm artifact to adjudicate. [@windred2023]

### Why this does not overturn the short-sleep lever

Regularity beating duration in this one dataset is not a contradiction of the duration U-curve. The two dials were measured over **different ranges**, so the comparison is apples-to-apples on the SRI side but truncated on the duration side.

Windred's longest objective quintile tops out at only **>7.56 h**. The self-report long arm that drives the U-curve — the illness-marker range of >9-10 h — sits above that ceiling, so this cohort never reaches it: «we therefore would not necessarily expect to see a heightened risk of mortality in this upper quintile». [@windred2023] Windred's duration arm looks flatter here because it does not sample the range where the artifact lives, not because the short-sleep finding weakened.

So both dials are real, and both are actionable. Regularity is the newly-visible one — an axis the duration-centric guidelines omit, added on top of the short-sleep lever rather than replacing it (a type-F refinement). Windred confirms the short-sleep and cardiometabolic-duration associations even while showing that day-to-day consistency, «the central focus of current sleep health guidelines» notwithstanding, predicts death more sharply than average hours. [@windred2023]

### How firm this is, and what to do with it

Certainty is **low**, and the reasons are structural. This is a **single cohort**, correlational, resting on one roughly 7-day accelerometry snapshot per person; no trial has raised anyone's SRI and then measured survival. [@windred2023] Regularity may be a cause of longer life or merely a marker of the health that produces it — the data cannot separate the two.

The decision it changes is narrow but clear. For the person who already sleeps an adequate \~7 h but at chaotic times — shift-adjacent schedules, weekend-to-weekday social jetlag — regularity is a lever the hours-based advice misses entirely. The concrete target is to **fall asleep and wake within roughly 1-hour windows most days**; the least-regular fifth of people scatter across \~3-hour windows. [@windred2023] It may also be the easier dial to turn — «sleep regularity may also be an easier dimension to target through interventions» than extending sleep, which is psychosocially and biologically hard. [@windred2023]

The third axis, continuity — whether the night holds together or fragments — has no comparable outcome test here and stays a named gap.

## §6 — For established insomnia: CBT-I first, hypnotics last

Once someone *has* chronic insomnia, three treatments compete: **CBT-I** (cognitive behavioural
therapy for insomnia — the behavioural first-line), the OTC circadian agent **melatonin**, and
prescription **hypnotics** (Z-drugs, benzodiazepines, sedating antihistamines). The instinct is to
reach for a drug first and behaviour last. The evidence inverts that order.

They cannot be ranked by effect size, and not for want of trying. Each reports a different quantity
on a different design: a standardized mean difference for CBT-I, minutes of latency for melatonin, a
mortality hazard ratio for hypnotics. Line them up and every *same quantity?* cell reads NO — an SMD
is not a hazard ratio is not a minute. [@vanstraten2018; @kripke2012; @ferraciolioda2013] So the order follows what *is* comparable across them: **evidence type, durability, and harm**.

### CBT-I — the durable benefit, and the one exit from pills

van Straten pooled **87 RCTs** (3724 treated patients): large effects on insomnia severity
(ISI g=0.98), sleep efficiency (g=0.71), wake-after-sleep-onset (g=0.63) and onset latency (g=0.57).
[@vanstraten2018] This is gold-tier evidence,
and two properties earn it first place.

It works where drugs are the problem. Effects were «quite robust (similar for patients with or
without comorbid disease, younger or older patients, using or not using sleep medi- cation)»
[@vanstraten2018] — chronic hypnotic users
included, so it is the route back off the pills. And it is guideline first-line: «CBTI is at least as
effective as pharmacotherapy», and «the American College of Physicians recently recommended CBTI as
the initial treatment for all adults with insomnia». [@vanstraten2018]

One caveat sizes the benefit honestly: CBT-I's *smallest* effect is on total sleep time (g=0.16). It
consolidates the night and lifts efficiency; it does not add hours. It restores the sleep you have
rather than manufacturing more.

### Melatonin — a small real effect, benign, no tolerance

Ferracioli-Oda's 19-RCT meta-analysis is also gold-tier, and held to the same outcome bar as any
drug — the label *natural* buys no exemption. On that bar melatonin clears only a small surrogate: it
cut sleep-onset latency by **7.06 min** (4.37 to 9.75) and raised total sleep time by **8.25 min**
(subjective; null on objective measures), improving quality by SMD 0.22.
[@ferraciolioda2013] Its own benchmark
places that sub-7-minute latency gain *below* benzodiazepines' 10.0 to 19.6 minutes.
[@ferraciolioda2013]

Here the surrogate benefit and the hard outcome are two different questions, and both answers are
worth stating. The surrogate effect is small but real, and the safety profile is unusual: melatonin
shows **no tolerance** — the effect grew with trial duration and dose — and a benign side-effect
profile, so it «may have a role in the treatment of insomnia given its relatively benign side-effect
profile compared to these agents». [@ferraciolioda2013] But no trial follows it out to a patient-important endpoint. That places melatonin as a
**benign, low-stakes adjunct** — reasonable to try, never a substitute for CBT-I, and not a
mortality or disease-prevention intervention.

### Hypnotics — a harm signal, held loosely

Kripke's matched cohort found a strong dose-response **mortality** association: hazard ratios of
**3.60, 4.43 and 5.32** across ascending dose tiers, HR **3.60 even at fewer than 18 pills a year**,
plus a cancer signal (upper-third HR 1.35). [@kripke2012] Read naively, that inverts the whole ranking on its own. It should not be read naively.

This is a single **observational** cohort, and its live confounder is **confounding by indication**
— the illness that leads to the prescription also raises the risk. And the direction is not assumed;
it is checked. The distress, psychiatric load and occult illness that prompt a hypnotic
independently raise mortality, so the confounder **inflates** an apparent harm rather than masking a
real one. Kripke matched on 116 comorbidity combinations, which attenuates the bias but «residual
confounding could not be fully excluded». [@kripke2012] No chronic-hypnotic mortality RCT exists to settle it, and the lead author runs an anti-hypnotic
advocacy site — reason to hold the *magnitude* loosely.

So the hazard is a **decision-relevant harm signal, not a demonstrated causal death toll**. It does
not prove that hypnotics kill; it is enough to move them from a first reach to a short-course,
lowest-effective-dose, last-resort option. Kripke's own reading points the same way: «A consensus is
developing that cognitive-behavioural therapy of chronic insomnia may be more successful than
hypnotics.» [@kripke2012]

### Do sleep aids give real sleep?

The three are not interchangeable routes to one outcome — they change different things.

- **CBT-I** restores continuity and efficiency: the sleep you have, made whole.
- **Melatonin** nudges timing and latency: a slightly earlier, slightly longer night.
- **Hypnotics** produce drugged sedation whose long-run safety is unestablished.

One limit binds all of it. Every endpoint here — ISI, PSQI, latency, sleep-number, even the mortality
association — is either a **surrogate** or a single confounded observational signal. No hard-outcome
trial has shown that treating insomnia lowers mortality. The ordering rests on evidence type,
durability and harm asymmetry, held as coherent reasoning, not as validated proof that any of these
extends a life.

## §7 — How sleep ranks, and the upstream levers the fabric can't yet size

Layer 1 ranks any exposure by **effect size x certainty**, and pulls the big rocks first. On that
scale sleep is a **moderate-effect, moderate-certainty lever**: below the dominant exposures, above
most refinements. It is neither a big rock to lead with nor a detail to bury.

The distinction matters because sleep is *not* one of the levers that recur across the major
age-related diseases. Assemble the modifiable-risk lists for dementia, cardiovascular disease, cancer
and Parkinson's, and the same handful repeats in the same direction: adiposity, physical inactivity,
diabetes/glycaemia, blood pressure, LDL, smoking, excess alcohol. Sleep duration is absent from that
shared set. Pulling those big rocks once buys risk reduction on several outcomes at the same time;
sleep is not among them, so it does not inherit their multi-outcome leverage
-> [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

The most recent dementia body reached the same stop honestly. The 2024 Lancet dementia commission
discussed sleep but did **not** adopt sleep duration as one of its modifiable risk factors, judging
the association reverse-causation-suspect: «Two meta-analyses included studies using varying
definitions, from short being <7 hours and long being >8 hours and had a follow-up of <10 years until
incident dementia, and their findings of an inverted U-shaped association between sleep duration and
dementia risk remain subject to potential reverse causation bias.»
[@livingston2024] That is an insufficient-evidence stop,
not a null.

**Where sleep earns attention is a specific stratum:** the habitually sleep-deprived, or someone on a
chaotic, shifting schedule. For that person the lever is real and remediable. For someone whose big
rocks are not yet handled — a smoker, a person with untreated central adiposity or high blood
pressure — those dominant exposures still come first, and no attainable precision about sleep changes
what to do next. The ranking is stratum-dependent: sleep rises once the larger levers are already
pulled -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

**The upstream levers the fabric cannot yet size.** What actually produces short or irregular sleep —
morning and evening light exposure, a fixed schedule, protected sleep opportunity (time in bed vs
time actually asleep), daytime exertion, late meals, caffeine timing — are plausible *candidate*
levers, but the wiki holds **no measured magnitude** for any of them on a sleep or a health outcome.
They are named gaps, and no direction is assigned to them here. Two overlap exposures the fabric does
hold sit adjacent: late-evening eating -> [[Meal Timing and Fasting]], and caffeine as a sleep
disruptor -> [[Coffee Consumption and Health]].

A limit travels with every duration figure behind this ranking: the exposure is **self-reported and
single-timepoint**, so misclassification is likely and real gradients can be flattened
-> [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]. The moderate-certainty grade already carries that
discount.

## §8 — What the evidence does NOT settle (named gaps)

Four questions sit open, and the discipline is to keep *insufficient evidence* distinct from *no
effect*: an unstudied question returns silence, not a null. None of the gaps below licenses a
directional claim.

**Sleep quality, architecture and continuity -> hard outcomes, at equal total time.** The duration
literature measures hours in bed, not their structure. Whether **fragmentation** (WASO,
wake-after-sleep-onset — the time spent awake between falling asleep and final waking) or **sleep
architecture** (the slow-wave and REM staging of a night) predicts mortality, cardiometabolic or
cognitive outcomes *independently of total sleep time* is untested here: the wiki holds no
actigraphy- or polysomnography-based cohort or systematic review on that question. A mechanism can be
imagined — fragmented, shallow sleep plausibly impairs metabolic and vascular recovery —
but it is a hypothesis, not a finding, and no direction follows from it. Named gap.

**Upstream levers -> outcomes.** The candidate levers named above — light and circadian timing,
schedule regularity, protected sleep opportunity, exercise, meal and caffeine timing — are held as
plausible influences with no magnitude attached. The wiki holds no light/circadian systematic review
and no exercise-to-sleep meta-analysis, so their effect on either sleep or a downstream health
outcome is unquantified. Candidate levers, not sized levers.

**Chronotype genetics and social jetlag.** Whether an evening chronotype, or **social jetlag** (the
weekday-weekend mismatch in sleep timing), causally worsens health — as opposed to co-travelling with
worse diet, later meals and less activity — is unresolved. The wiki holds no chronotype
Mendelian-randomization study and no review of short-sleeper genetic variants, the two designs that
would separate a causal timing effect from confounding. Named gap.

**What is NOT an open gap: the long-sleep arm.** The causal status of habitual long sleep is settled,
not pending. Its excess risk is a **reverse-causation artifact / marker of occult ill-health** — no
mechanism, higher heterogeneity, a frailty gradient — so the habitual long sleeper is a work-up
candidate for hidden illness, not a sleep-reduction target. That is a resolved verdict and does not
belong on this list.

## §9 — The decision, for whom

- **Fix chronic short sleep.** For anyone habitually under \~7 h — especially under 5-6 — moving
  toward the 7-8 h reference lowers a real, mechanism-backed mortality and diabetes risk. Aim at a
  *floor*, not a precise hour: adequate, not chronically short.
- **Do not shorten a long sleeper.** Habitual long sleep marks possible occult illness; the response
  is a work-up, not sleep restriction. There is no evidenced harm at the upper end and no ceiling to
  steer to.
- **Stabilise chaotic timing.** For the person already sleeping enough but at scattered hours — shift
  work, social jetlag — hold sleep and wake within roughly 1-hour windows most days. This is the
  newly-visible lever, on single-cohort evidence, and often easier than adding hours.
- **For insomnia, behaviour before pills.** Try CBT-I first — the durable, harm-free first-line and
  the exit from chronic hypnotic use; melatonin is a benign small adjunct; sedative hypnotics carry a
  real (if confounded) harm signal and belong short-course and last.
- **Rank it honestly.** Sleep is a moderate lever — below smoking, weight, blood pressure and
  activity, above most refinements. It matters most once those are handled, or for the badly
  sleep-deprived. Where sleep is already adequate and regular, that the remaining gains are small is
  itself the useful result.

The honest ceiling: what changes sleep upstream, and whether quality at equal hours matters, are
candidate levers the evidence cannot yet size — named gaps, not nulls. Your baseline health, work
schedule, chronotype, and whether you have diagnosed insomnia or apnoea decide the individual
weighting, at layer 3.

### Mandatory caveats

- **Open loop.** This wiki grades internal coherence and fidelity to its sources — never whether a
  recommendation actually improves outcomes in the world. A clean appraisal is not a validated result.
- **Appraise, do not prescribe.** This is a general, population-level appraisal, not medical advice;
  selecting a sleep medication, dosing it, and screening for contraindications and interactions are
  prescriber acts requiring information this document does not hold.
- **A general appraisal, applied per person.** Your baseline health, shift/work schedule, chronotype,
  whether you have diagnosed insomnia or apnoea, and your realistic alternative decide the individual
  weighting.
- **Health axis only.** This cut weighs sleep on longevity, cardiometabolic and cognitive outcomes;
  daytime function, mood and quality of life are real and yours to weigh, entering here mainly through
  their physical intersection.
- **The target is not agreement or divergence with any guideline.** It is showing how good the sleep
  evidence is — largely observational and self-reported, with reverse causation the central threat — and
  where, despite that, a lever changes a decision.
