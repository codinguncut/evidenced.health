## A few shared exposures matter most, and in older age the firmest evidence is about function

**Only a few exposures change the risk of several diseases of ageing at once:** smoking, physical
activity, body fat, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and alcohol. Heart disease and stroke, type 2
diabetes, several cancers and dementia share most of them, in the same direction. **So one set of
changes lowers the risk of several diseases together**: not smoking, moving more, keeping body fat,
blood pressure and LDL down, and drinking little or nothing
([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]). In older age, balance and
muscle-strengthening work join the list. [[Big Rocks (Median)]] and [[Big Rocks (Elderly)]] appraise
those shared levers in full. The evidence comes disease by disease, and no study adds it up into one
combined effect.

**Most other candidates add little.** Some rest on insufficient evidence, and some help one disease
while raising the risk of another. Parkinson's disease breaks the shared pattern most clearly
([[Parkinsons Disease Modifiable Risk Factors]]). Dairy, hormone therapy after menopause and statins
each lower one risk while raising another; this guide lists those outcome by outcome without netting
them ([[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]], [[Statins and New-Onset Diabetes]]).

**For older adults, the firmest evidence bears on function more than on length of life.** Randomized
trials show, at high certainty, that exercise built on balance and functional training prevents falls in
older people living in the community. It cuts the rate of falls by about a quarter (rate ratio 0.76)
[@sherrington2019]. Exercise also lowers fracture risk,
on weaker evidence, though the trials do not show which part of the programme does it
([[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]). The link between activity and a longer life comes from
observational cohorts ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]). **None of the studies appraised
here measures whether a lever shortens the years spent ill or disabled before death**
([[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]).

**In the oldest age bands, drug trials are sparse rather than negative**: the evidence is insufficient,
not evidence of harm (age section below).

| Outcome | Lever | Evidence state | Certainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart attack and stroke | lowering blood pressure or LDL with drugs | benefit (randomized) | high |
| Falls, older adults living in the community | balance and functional exercise | benefit (randomized) | high |
| Type 2 diabetes, people with prediabetes | lifestyle programme | benefit (randomized) | high |
| Death, heart disease, cancer | smoking | harm | high |
| Death, cancer, Parkinson's | physical activity | benefit association; Parkinson's in men only | observational |
| Fractures | exercise | benefit | low to moderate |
| Dementia | the shared exposures | association | see [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]] |
| Years lived ill or disabled before death | any lever | insufficient evidence (not measured) | none |

**Overall confidence: medium.** It is high for the large shared exposures and for the blood-pressure
and LDL drug trials. It is lower for Parkinson's disease and the diet-and-cancer links, where
the evidence is observational and the reviews are few or of low methodological quality
([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]).

This guide is written for the general population. Dementia has its own appraisal in
[[Dementia Prevention]] and appears here as one column. Chronic kidney disease
([[Chronic Kidney Disease and Modifiable Exposures]]), treating an established disease and choosing or
dosing a drug are outside this guide.

## The same few exposures move most diseases of ageing

Line up the risk factors for heart disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer, dementia, Parkinson's and falls,
and **the same handful of exposures keeps reappearing**, with the overall diet pattern more weakly. No
single disease body says this; the overlap appears only when the lists sit side by side
-> [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

### What each shared lever asks, in rank order

This guide orders the levers by judging effect size, certainty and breadth together, not by
calculation. The cells measure different outcomes with different designs, so **no row can be added up
into one cross-disease effect**, and no held source computes one.

**Smoking: do not smoke, or stop. It has the largest effect and the widest reach.** Current smokers die
at about three times the rate of never-smokers (all-cause HR 3.0, 99% CI 2.7-3.3, in women; 2.8,
2.4-3.1, in men) [@jha2013]. Quitting pays at
every age the data cover (age section) -> [[Smoking and Mortality]].

**Physical activity: move more and sit less. It is the broadest lever, and no drug replicates it.** In
device-measured cohorts, all-cause mortality fell most, HR 0.34 (0.27-0.43), at about 300 counts per
minute of total activity, with no further drop above that
[@who2020]. Total activity here
counts movement at any intensity [@ekelundacc2019]. These are
cohorts, where illness that lowers activity can inflate the contrast, so read the flat top as where
the data level off, not as a proven dose ceiling
-> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

**Activity's link to dementia is contested** (table), and studies with at least 20 years of follow-up
still found RR 0.79 (0.71-0.87) [@isomarkku2022].
The linked page reads this as insufficient evidence for a direct effect, not proof of no effect
-> [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]].

**Body fat: cohorts link it broadly to disease, but the trials of losing it are thinner; for diabetes
the trial evidence is strong.** Weight-loss trials cut all-cause mortality (RR 0.82, 0.71-0.95) but did not measurably cut
cardiovascular events [@ma2017]
-> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]], [[Body Fatness and Cancer Risk]]. In the DPP
(adults with raised fasting and post-load glucose, BMI >=24) a lifestyle programme cut diabetes
incidence by 58% (48-66) against 31% (17-43) for metformin, and preventing one case over three years
took 6.9 people on the programme and 13.9 on metformin
[@knowler2002]
-> [[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]. For a frail person the advice on weight reverses
(age section).

**Blood pressure and LDL: keep both down. They carry the firmest randomized proof, and drugs supply most
of it.** Each 5 mmHg of systolic lowering cut major cardiovascular events by about 9% in people without
heart disease [@bplttc2021],
and each 1 mmol/L of LDL lowered by a statin cut them by 22% and all-cause mortality by 10% (RR 0.90,
0.87-0.93) [@ctt2010].

Because mature drugs lower these markers, **diet and exercise aimed only at blood pressure or LDL add
less on cardiovascular outcomes** than their raw effect suggests. No held evidence shows one drug doing
what activity does from falls to cancer, so activity keeps its rank
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], [[LDL Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]],
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]. Statins raise new-onset diabetes slightly
(opposite-sign table below).

**Alcohol: drink little or nothing. It has no protective arm once the comparisons are corrected.**
Adjusted for how abstainers were defined and for study quality, low-volume drinkers showed no survival
advantage: RR 0.97 (0.88-1.07) [@stockwell2016]
-> [[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]], [[Alcohol and Cancer Risk]].

**Diet pattern comes last: its hard-outcome evidence is narrow.** PREDIMED cut a cardiovascular
composite, mostly through fewer strokes, but **all-cause mortality did not change: HR 0.98 (0.77-1.24)**
[@estruch2018]
-> [[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]], [[Diet Physical Activity and Cancer Prevention]].

### What the dementia figures can and cannot say

The Lancet Commission credits its 14 factors with 45.3% of dementia cases, but that is a population
figure. The Commission states that «this risk modification affects the population but does not
guarantee any individual will avoid dementia»
[@livingston2024]. **Trials that bundled the shared levers
into one dementia programme have not prevented dementia**: pooled data from the MAPT and preDIVA trials
showed no effect on incident dementia (HR 0.98, 0.80-1.21). No subgroup responded, whether
pre-specified or found by a data-driven search
[@coley2025]. Blood-pressure treatment
on its own did lower incidence (table above)
-> [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]],
[[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]].

## Parkinson's and cancer add a few levers of their own

**Most disease-specific levers are weaker than the shared ones, and the strongest apply only to a
narrow group.** A few more lower one disease's risk while raising another's (last part of this section)
 -> [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

### Parkinson's disease has few credible levers

**The Parkinson's evidence is thin, and most of what looks like a lever may not be causal.** An
umbrella review of 46 systematic reviews rated none high or moderate quality on AMSTAR-2; 39 were
critically low. Activity went with lower risk in men but not clearly in women (table); the authors
still advise activity for women for its general benefits. Diabetes raised the risk, RR 1.31 (1.10-1.57;
4 cohort studies). Pesticide exposure (OR 1.76, 1.56-2.04) rests on case-control and mixed designs,
from which the review drew no reliable conclusion; coffee and dairy are in the opposite-sign table
[@chen2021pd].

**A prodrome lasting years can manufacture protective associations**: early, undiagnosed disease can cut
activity, coffee intake and smoking, so even cohorts cannot rule out reverse causation. On smoking's
inverse association the review leaves causality open and, because smoking causes many other diseases,
concludes it «does not impact public health strategies for reducing smoking among populations»
[@chen2021pd]. Activity is the one
association worth acting on, and it is already a shared lever
-> [[Parkinsons Disease Modifiable Risk Factors]], [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

### Cancer adds processed meat, wholegrains and one harmful supplement

The largest diet-adjacent cancer levers are the shared ones: body fat, alcohol and activity
 -> [[Diet Physical Activity and Cancer Prevention]]. **Below them, processed meat is the
best-graded single food**: colorectal cancer RR 1.16 (1.08-1.26) per 50 g a day, graded convincing; red
meat's RR 1.12 (1.00-1.25) per 100 g a day touches the null and is graded probable
[@wcrf2018]. NutriRECS estimates about 7
fewer cancer deaths per 1000 people over a lifetime for three fewer servings of unprocessed red meat a
week, at low certainty, and suggests that adults continue their current intake (a weak recommendation)
[@nutrirecs2019]. The two bodies read
largely overlapping cohorts in opposite directions
-> [[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]].

WCRF grades wholegrains and dietary fibre as probable protection against colorectal cancer. **One
supplement causes harm: WCRF judges high-dose beta-carotene a cause of lung cancer in current and former
smokers** [@wcrf2018ter]. Two trials in people who
smoked or were exposed to asbestos at work each found more lung cancer: RR 1.18 (1.03-1.36) with
beta-carotene, and adjusted RR 1.28 (1.04-1.57) with beta-carotene plus vitamin A
[@uspstf2022supp]
-> [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

### Some exposures lower one disease's risk and raise another's

**Four exposures move different diseases in opposite directions.** The table does not net them: the
outcomes differ in weight and certainty, and the reader weighs them.

| Exposure | Lowers risk of | Raises risk of | No benefit shown | How to read it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dairy | colorectal cancer, graded probable [@wcrf2018] | Parkinson's, RR 1.40 (1.20-1.63), observational [@chen2021pd] | hip fracture in cohorts, RR 0.90 (0.73-1.11) [@malmir2019] | not a bone lever -> [[Dairy and Bone Health]] |
| HRT (WHI, oral oestrogen + progestin, women 50-79) | hip fracture, HR 0.66 (0.45-0.98) [@rossouw2002] | CHD 1.29 (1.02-1.63), stroke 1.41 (1.07-1.85), breast cancer 1.26 (1.00-1.59) [@rossouw2002]; Parkinson's 1.24 (1.10-1.41), observational [@chen2021pd] | total mortality, HR 0.98 (0.82-1.18) [@rossouw2002] | a symptom and bone treatment, not a prevention drug -> [[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]] |

**HRT is not a drug for preventing disease.** WHI, after an average 5.2 years, concluded that «this
regimen should not be initiated or continued for primary prevention of CHD»
[@rossouw2002]. Formulation and timing
are appraised in [[Does Timing Modify HRT Cardiovascular and Mortality Effect]].

The statin's diabetes excess is about 0.12% a year at standard intensity, concentrated in people whose
glycaemia already sits near the diagnostic threshold, and the CTT investigators judge it greatly
outweighed by the drug's benefit on major vascular events
[@reith2024diabetes]. Dairy's colorectal grade did not
produce a WCRF recommendation: the panel held back because of a suggestive prostate-cancer signal
[@wcrf2018].

## In older age, function is where the firmest evidence sits

**Length of life and function are separate outcomes, and the trials measure them unevenly.** Randomized
trials cover falls, fractures and mobility; the mortality results for the same levers come mostly from
cohorts, and how the last decades unfold is not measured
[[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]].

### Balance and functional exercise prevents falls in community-dwelling older adults

**Older adults living in the community: do balance and functional exercise, about 3 hours a week as a
practice default** (where that default comes from is below).

A Cochrane review of 108 RCTs in community-dwelling people (mean age 76) found
exercise cut the rate of falls by 23% (rate ratio 0.77, 0.71-0.83; 59 RCTs; HIGH certainty): at 850
falls per 1000 people per year, 195 fewer falls (144-246 fewer). **Balance and functional exercise
carries the effect** (0.76, 0.70-0.81; 39 RCTs; HIGH). Resistance training alone (1.14, 0.67-1.97) and
walking (1.14, 0.66-1.97) are very-low-certainty estimates crossing 1: insufficient evidence, not a
shown null [@sherrington2019].

**The relative effect held across ages; the absolute gain grows with fall risk.** It did not differ
between people aged 75 and over and younger participants, or by selection for high fall
risk. The authors wrote:
«There is likely to be a greater absolute impact in people identified at increased risk of falling, but
there is benefit also for those who are at more general risk in the community.»
[@sherrington2019]

**The dose default comes from comparing trials**: an earlier meta-regression found larger effects at 3
or more hours a week (ratio of rate ratios 0.77, 0.65-0.91). The authors flag the comparison as potentially confounded but still
recommend that dose. In care homes, exercise alone gave 0.90 (0.72-1.12), insufficient evidence
[@sherrington2017falls]. Read 3 hours as a
labelled practice default, not a tested dose -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].

### Exercise cuts major osteoporotic fractures, without saying which channel does it

**Exercise programmes reduced major osteoporotic fractures by about a quarter**: incidence ratio 0.75
(95% CI 0.59-0.94) across 11 trials in cohorts aged over 50 on average. The trials mixed fall-prevention
and bone-loading programmes, so the review cannot say whether fewer falls, stronger bone or both did it
[@hoffmann2022]. The Cochrane
falls review points the same way: fall-related fractures RR 0.73 (0.56-0.95; LOW certainty)
[@sherrington2019].
**A bone-density gain is a surrogate, not a fracture result** -> [[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]],
[[Fluoride and Bone Health]], [[Exercise Safety in Osteoporosis]].

### Vitamin D with calcium has shown no fracture benefit in community trials

**Routine vitamin D with calcium has shown no fracture benefit in community trials, and one harm.** A US
evidence review covered adults aged 50 and over, excluding the known-deficient, people in institutions,
and those with osteoporosis or a prior fracture. Its total-fracture result rests on one trial in
postmenopausal women: risk difference -0.35% (-1.02 to 0.31), with the evidence for no benefit graded
low strength. Kidney stones rose (RR 1.18, 1.04-1.35; absolute +0.33%, 0.06-0.60),
on evidence graded moderate [@kahwati2018].
Calcium raised myocardial infarction in one meta-analysis (RR 1.24, 1.07-1.45)
[@bolland2011], a contested signal
-> [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]].

**A broader pool shows a modest hip-fracture benefit.** Vitamin D with calcium cut hip fracture by 16%
(RR 0.84, 0.72-0.97; 6 RCTs, including the community trial above); vitamin D alone did not (1.14,
0.98-1.32). The authors flag uncertain intervals and high risk of bias
[@yao2019]; no held trial tests a deficient group
[inferred from @yao2019]. For people in
institutions or with osteoporosis, a prior fracture or a known deficiency, a prescriber makes the
decision.

### In frailty, function moves; falls and survival are unshown

**For prefrail or frail adults, physical activity improves function.** A meta-analysis of 26 studies
(8022 adults aged 65 and over) found gains in mobility (SMD 0.60, 0.37-0.83, moderate certainty).
Frailty also became less common (RR 0.58, 0.36-0.93, moderate certainty), though only 4 of the 23 meta-analysed
trials reported it. Falls were RR 0.80 (0.51-1.26; very low certainty), insufficient evidence. The
programmes lasted 29 weeks on average [@racey2021frailtypa].

**So do not carry the community falls result into frailty.** Start lower and supervised; the
linked page holds a labelled low-volume floor for resistance work (two sets of three lower-limb
exercises, twice a week), not a tested dose [[Frailty]]. Mortality was not measured.

### Grip, muscle mass and fitness are markers; training is the lever

**Low grip strength and muscle mass predict death, but no trial shows raising them lowers it.** These
markers, like fitness, place a person in a risk stratum; they are not targets
[[Grip Strength and Mortality]], [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]].

**Act on the activities, not the markers: add muscle-strengthening work.** Muscle-strengthening activity goes
with lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.85, 0.79-0.93), at very low certainty, downgraded mainly for indirectness because most studies
were conducted in the USA [@momma2022]
-> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]], [[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]].

**Protein supports the training; it does not replace it.** PROT-AGE advises at least 1.0-1.2 g/kg a
day, 1.2 g/kg for active older adults [@bauer2013].
It advises 1.2-1.5 g/kg with acute or chronic disease, and up to 2.0 g/kg in severe illness, injury or
marked malnutrition, except in severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30, not on dialysis)
[@bauer2013]. PROT-AGE is a consensus built largely
on surrogate endpoints [inferred from @bauer2013]
[[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

**Raising a hormone is not a fracture lever.** In TRAVERSE (men 45-80 with low testosterone and
preexisting cardiovascular disease or a high risk of it), clinical fractures were numerically higher on
testosterone: 3.50% vs 2.46%, HR 1.43 (1.04-1.97), unadjusted for multiple comparisons
[@snyder2024] [[Testosterone Adiposity and Muscle]].

## The order of levers shifts with age

**No held source ranks levers by age.** The tables below line up age-specific results from separate
sources; this guide, not any source, reads a pattern across them. The cells mix designs and
units, so compare directions within a row, not sizes across rows
-> [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

### Lifestyle levers by age

| Lever | Younger bands | Older bands | Design and population | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quitting smoking (years of life gained vs continuing) | quit at 25-34: \~10; at 35-44: \~9 | quit at 45-54: \~6; at 55-64: \~4 | US national cohort (NHIS); data stop at 55-64 | [@jha2013] |
| Body fat: all-cause mortality per 5 BMI units above 25 | 35-49 y: HR 1.52 (1.47-1.56) | 50-69 y: 1.37 (1.35-1.39); 70-89 y: 1.21 (1.17-1.25) | pooled prospective cohorts, never-smokers without chronic disease | [@globalbmi2016] |
| Lifestyle programme: diabetes incidence vs placebo | 25-44: 48% lower (27-63); 45-59: 59% (44-70) | >=60: 71% lower (51-83) | RCT (DPP), adults with raised fasting and post-load glucose, BMI >=24 | [@knowler2002] |
| Daily steps: where mortality risk stops falling | under 60: \~8000-10 000 | 60 and over: \~6000-8000 | 15 cohorts, n=47 471, observational | [@paluch2022] |

**Quitting smoking pays at every age the data cover**, though the gain shrinks. People who quit by
about 39 still carry a hazard ratio of 1.2, and the data hold no quit-age band past 64
[@jha2013].

**The body-fat gradient flattens with age but stays above 1** at 70-89: a weaker association, not a
protective one. An older adult who loses weight should pair it with resistance training
and protein, because energy restriction costs more muscle as reserve falls
-> [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]], [[Protein Intake for Older Adults]].

**The diabetes programme worked at least as well in older participants.** Placebo incidence was about
11 per 100 person-years in every band; lifestyle brought it to 6.2 at 25-44 and 3.1 at 60 and over
[@knowler2002]. The subgroup tests were not
planned or powered, so the result shows a direction, not a proven age effect.

**Older adults reach the step plateau sooner** (age-by-steps interaction p=0.012). The top quartile's
median was 10 901 steps, so the data say nothing about counts far above that
[@paluch2022] -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]].

### Drugs and supplements by age

| Lever | Younger bands | Older bands | Design and population | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood pressure: major CV events per 5 mmHg lower SBP | <55: HR 0.82 (0.76-0.88) | 55-64, 65-74: 0.91 (0.88-0.95); 75-84: 0.91 (0.87-0.96); >=85: 0.99 (0.87-1.12) | IPD from 51 RCTs, 358 707 people, with and without prior CVD | [@rahimi2021bpage] |
| Statin: major vascular events per 1 mmol/L lower LDL, over 75 | not shown separately | with vascular disease: RR 0.85 (99% CI 0.73-0.98); without: 0.92 (99% CI 0.73-1.16) | 28 statin RCTs, 14 483 people over 75 | [@ctt2019] |
| Aspirin, starting for primary prevention | 40-59 at >=10% 10-year risk: individual decision, small net benefit (Grade C) | 60 and over: recommend against starting (Grade D) | guideline, moderate certainty | [@uspstf2022aspirin] |
| Metformin: diabetes incidence vs placebo | 25-44: 44% lower (21-60) | >=60: 11% (-33 to 41) | RCT (DPP), as above | [@knowler2002] |
| Vitamin D, empiric, in healthy adults | 50-74: nothing above the recommended allowance (600 IU/day to age 70, 800 IU/day from 71) | 75 and over: empiric supplementation suggested (conditional), daily lower doses preferred; trial doses averaged \~900 IU/day; mortality RR 0.96 (0.93-1.00) | guideline on pooled RCTs | [@demay2024vitamind] |

**Blood-pressure drugs keep their relative effect to 84; at 85 and over the evidence is insufficient,
not negative.** The authors advise continuing a well-tolerated drug. Frail, multimorbid and
institutionalized people were under-represented, so starting one in them is an individual trade-off
[@rahimi2021bpage]
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**Statins for people over 75 without vascular disease are unconfirmed, not refuted.** The authors say
there were too few such participants to assess the group alone
[@ctt2019]. The USPSTF calls the evidence for starting at 76 or older insufficient
[@uspstf2022]. The 2026 US cholesterol guideline
says starting may be reasonable after discussion, if life expectancy is at least 2.5 years
[@accaha2026]
-> [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]].

**For aspirin, age drives the harm**: the relative bleeding risk is constant, but the absolute rate
rises with age, more steeply from 60 [@uspstf2022aspirin]
-> [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]. **Metformin moves the other way from
lifestyle**: near-null in the oldest DPP band, where lifestyle's advantage was greater
[@knowler2002].

**Vitamin D appears only at the oldest age.** For healthy adults aged
50-74 it found high-certainty evidence of little or no effect on fractures, CV events, cancer and death.
At 75 and over the estimate is about 6 fewer deaths per 1000, with an upper bound touching 1.00, and the
panel calls its age bands somewhat arbitrary [@demay2024vitamind]
-> [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

### The young end: judge the lifetime, not the next ten years

**For younger adults a 10-year risk score understates the case for acting.** ESC notes that young
people with high risk factors usually score low over ten years while their lifetime risk is very high
[@esc2021]. Its modelled gain from lowering LDL by
1 mmol/L falls from roughly 0.6-1.6 CVD-free years for a man aged 45-49 to 0.0-0.3 at 90 and over
[@esc2021]
-> [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

### The old end: muscle, life expectancy and risk set the order, not a birthday

**No single age flips the order.** ESC raises treatment thresholds from 70, because almost everyone
over 70 exceeds the usual thresholds and treatment buys less CVD-free time
[@esc2021]. Three things re-rank the levers for an
older person: muscle, life expectancy and baseline risk
-> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

**For a frail person, deliberate weight loss can do harm.** Unintentional weight loss is one of the
five Fried criteria that define frailty, alongside exhaustion, low activity, slow walking and poor grip
[@who2020]. Someone who meets those
criteria has little reserve to spare, so the advice on weight reverses: keep muscle first, and treat
deliberate weight loss as contraindicated. A robust older person with excess weight can still lose it,
paired with training and protein
-> [[Frailty]], [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

**Read together, the tables suggest a pattern, not a finding:** the lifestyle levers keep paying into
old age, while one drug harm (aspirin bleeding) grows with age.

**For a person at low baseline risk the shared levers still matter, but the absolute gain is smaller**,
because absolute benefit scales with baseline risk
([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]).

## What the evidence cannot yet say

**The held sources do not answer the questions below, and none of the gaps points in a direction.**
Each is an open question, not a finding of no effect.

- **The shape of decline.** No held source measures disease-free life expectancy or compressed
  morbidity ([[Intrinsic Capacity and Multidimensional Healthspan]]).
- **Muscle, strength and connection as levers.** No trial shows that raising muscle mass, grip or
  fitness lowers mortality ([[Low Muscle Mass and Mortality]]), and none shows that more social
  connection does ([[Social Connection and Mortality]]). No falls-incidence trial tests resistance or
  power training on its own ([[Power Training and Physical Function in Older Adults]]).
- **Weight loss and a person's cancer risk.** The cancer grades rest on body fat in cohorts; no held
  source shows that losing weight lowers an individual's cancer risk ([[Body Fatness and Cancer Risk]]).
- **A combined effect across diseases.** No source pools one lever's effect over several diseases, and
  this appraisal cannot compute it ([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]).
- **Activity and Parkinson's.** No magnitude meta-analysis is held; the leg rests on an umbrella review,
  with no absolute risk ([[Parkinsons Disease Modifiable Risk Factors]]).
- **A shared biology of ageing.** No held source tests a common mechanism behind the shared levers; the
  pattern is epidemiological ([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]).
- **Bone drugs as a comparator.** No review of bisphosphonate or other antiresorptive efficacy is held,
  and no single page here appraises osteoporosis as one question ([[Exercise and Bone Mineral Density]]).

**This appraisal checks that each claim matches its sources and that the claims hang together.**
No one has tested any claim in it against what later happened to people who acted on it.

## What to do at each stage of life

**Anyone: do not smoke, or stop. Move more at any intensity and sit less**; in cohorts, mortality
stopped falling at about 8,000-10,000 steps a day under 60 and 6,000-8,000 at 60 and over. **Keep body
fat, blood pressure and LDL down, and drink little or nothing.** In younger adults, judge the levers on
lifetime risk, not 10-year risk ([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

**Older adults: add balance and functional exercise, about 3 hours a week as a practice default, plus
resistance work** ([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]],
[[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]). Eat at least 1.0-1.2 g of protein per kg a day
(1.2 g/kg if active), unless you have severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30) and are not on dialysis
([[Protein Intake for Older Adults]], [[Resistance Training for Sarcopenic Obesity]]). Routine vitamin
D with calcium has shown no fracture benefit in community trials; for people in institutions, or with
osteoporosis, a prior fracture or a known deficiency, a prescriber decides
([[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]]). Do not start aspirin at 60 or
over ([[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]).

**The frail: keep muscle first, and avoid deliberate weight loss** ([[Frailty]]). **The oldest:** trial
evidence for starting a drug runs thin; missing evidence is not shown harm, and the decision is individual. A
person already on a well-tolerated blood-pressure drug usually continues it.

**Past the large shared levers, stop optimizing.** Supplements, acrylamide, coffee and similar
refinements are small or unproven (see *What does not carry a large effect*).
