**Where your fat is stored predicts your health more faithfully than how much you weigh.** The
harm concentrates in fat that overflows into the liver, pancreas and viscera once the body's safe
storage runs out — and that overflow can begin at a normal weight and spare some heavy people, so
the scale number is the wrong instrument to steer by.

**Losing fat reliably reverses some conditions and moves nearly every risk marker — but the single
largest lifestyle trial did not show that it prevents heart attacks.** Weight loss earns its place
on the outcomes it demonstrably changes: type 2 diabetes remission, fatty-liver regression, glucose
control, function and independence. Treat a promised reduction in cardiac events, by the lifestyle
route, as unproven rather than assured.

**The method matters less than what you can sustain, and maintenance is a separate problem from
loss** — because after a diet the body appears to defend the higher weight through a persistent shift
in appetite hormones. And any deficit costs muscle, which becomes a front-line harm rather than a
cosmetic one once a person is older.

This deliverable appraises the evidence; it does not prescribe. The weighting of length of life
against its quality, and of health against every other consideration, stays with the person.

## Fat's location drives cardiometabolic risk more than its total amount

The organizing finding, induced across an individual-mechanism source and a population meta-analysis,
is that cardiometabolic risk tracks *where* fat is stored more faithfully than total fat mass or BMI
-> [[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]]. Three fat compartments are three different objects. **Subcutaneous**
fat is the safe, expandable store, and is not itself pathogenic; its capacity varies by person and by
ethnicity. **Intra-organ fat** — in the liver and pancreas — is the pathogenic depot, driving hepatic
insulin resistance and suppressing insulin secretion. **Visceral fat** is commonly cast as the villain, but
Taylor demotes it to a *marker*: «Extent of visceral fat accumulation is a surrogate marker for intra-organ
fat ex- cess, but is not pathophysiologically related to adverse metabolic consequences»
[@taylor2015pft]. Waist
circumference is therefore a marker of a marker — useful because it correlates with intra-organ fat, a step
removed from the depot doing the damage -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]].

### Storage capacity is personal, so risk begins at different weights

Chronic energy surplus fills subcutaneous fat until an individual's storage limit — a **personal fat
threshold** — is exceeded, after which lipid deposits ectopically. Taylor states it as a hypothesis:
«We hypothesize that each individual could have a personal fat threshold (PFT) which de- termines their
susceptibility to developing T2DM ... Gaining sufﬁcient weight to cross their PFT will trigger the
condition» — and the threshold is «independent of BMI»
[@taylor2015pft]. The clinical
consequence is real: 36% of newly-diagnosed type 2 diabetes in the UKPDS cohort occurred at BMI below 25,
and the equivalent-risk BMI shifts by ethnicity — «BMI greater than 30 kg/m2» in Caucasians is «expressed
in South Asians at 25.2 kg/m2 and at 27 kg/m2 in African/Caribbeans»
[@taylor2015pft]. So a
normal-weight person can be above their own threshold, and a population BMI cut-point misclassifies them.
The threshold construct is a mechanism under test, admitted directionally, not a per-person measurable
quantity [inferred from @taylor2015pft].

### "Metabolically healthy obesity" is not durably safe

If risk tracks depot and metabolic status rather than mass, then obesity is not made safe by currently-normal
markers, and normal-weight people with poor metabolic profiles carry high risk. Kramer's meta-analysis of
observational cohorts shows exactly this. Metabolically-healthy obesity is not benign over the long run — RR
1.24 (95% CI 1.02-1.55) versus metabolically-healthy normal weight in studies with >=10 years of follow-up
(the pooled absolute excess is small and slow, \~0.7% over 10-11 years, emerging only after \~10 years); and
metabolic status dominates BMI, with metabolically-*unhealthy* normal-weight carrying RR 3.14 (2.36-3.93),
roughly equal to unhealthy-obese RR 2.65. Kramer's conclusion: «there is no healthy pattern of increased
weight» [@kramer2013mho]. The honest
framing is a small, slow, non-zero excess — continued attention, not an emergency.

### What body-fat percentage can and cannot tell you

Body-fat percentage is a better proxy than BMI because it separates fat from lean mass. But it remains a
*total* measure: it does not say where the fat sits, which is the risk-bearing distinction above. The corpus
holds no source establishing a sex-specific body-fat-percentage threshold against all-cause mortality, so
that curve is a **named gap, not a number**. And a low-body-fat left arm would carry the same
reverse-causation and frailty artifact that inflates the BMI-underweight arm (next section), so it could not
be read as a clean protective floor even if it were held. Steer by depot and metabolic status — waist as a
marker of intra-organ fat, liver fat where it can be seen — not by a single total-adiposity number
[inferred from @taylor2015pft].

## Correcting for bias erases the "obesity paradox"

The largest analysis of the question — 239 prospective cohorts, 10.6 million participants — was built to
answer the artifact question directly, restricting its primary analysis to never-smokers, dropping anyone
with chronic disease at baseline, and excluding the first five years of follow-up
-> [[BMI and All-Cause Mortality]]. In that bias-controlled curve, mortality is lowest at BMI 22.5-25 and
rises monotonically above it — HR 1.31 (1.29-1.33) per 5 kg/m2 above BMI 25, with no protective or even
neutral overweight band once the biases are removed
[@globalbmi2016].

The apparent protection of overweight is a bias cascade: across the stepwise corrections the overweight arm
walks from HR 0.96 (raw, apparent protection) to 1.11 (never-smokers, clear harm) as smoking confounding,
reverse causation and prevalent disease are stripped out
[@globalbmi2016]. The flip is
starkest split by smoking status: in the overweight band the never-smoker HR is 1.07 (1.06-1.07) — real
harm — against 0.94 (0.94-0.95) in ex- and current smokers, whose illness-driven weight loss manufactures
the apparent protection (between-stratum heterogeneity P=0.0003)
[@globalbmi2016]. This is the
worked instance of the protective-arm artifact — adjudicate the arm, not the curve
-> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

The underweight arm behaves differently: it falls under correction but stays elevated at HR 1.47, so low
BMI carries residual real excess, distinct from the fully-artifactual overweight signal.

A genetic natural experiment confirms the direction. Wade's Mendelian randomization in UK Biobank (335,308
participants; a 77-SNP instrument) supports causal harm from higher BMI — all-cause HR 1.03 per 1 kg/m2
(0.99-1.07, directional but imprecise), reaching significance for cardiovascular mortality (1.10, 1.01-1.19)
— and shifts the nadir down toward the normal range (\~23 vs \~26 observationally), naming reverse causation as
the driver of the observational J-shape
[@wade2018]. The two analyses share
investigators, so this is a refinement of the same lineage rather than independent corroboration, and the
all-cause genetic estimate is imprecise — but the correction consistently *deflates* the low arm and
*inflates* the obesity arm.

**One boundary this section cannot cross.** A bias-corrected curve of where mortality is lowest *across people*
does not establish that *reducing* an individual's BMI moves them down it. That is the intervention question,
and it is the subject of the next sections [inferred from @globalbmi2016; @wade2018].

## Fat loss reverses conditions and moves markers, but has not been shown to prevent heart attacks by diet

That losing weight prevents heart attacks is intuitive and widely recommended, and for the lifestyle route it
was tested head-on. **Look AHEAD** randomized 5,145 overweight/obese adults with type 2 diabetes to intensive
lifestyle intervention or diabetes support, and *failed* on its primary cardiovascular endpoint — HR 0.95
(95% CI 0.83-1.09); the trial was stopped early on a futility analysis at a median 9.6 years
[@look2013]
-> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]. The null is narrower than it sounds: the between-arm
weight gap shrank sharply by study end, leaving only a modest sustained difference; background diabetes care
was good in both arms; LDL was the one risk factor the intervention did not improve; and the trial was powered
only for a large effect. This bounds the claim rather than voiding it.

Look AHEAD is also a clean worked case of the surrogate-versus-outcome gap. A substudy showed the intervention
drove C-reactive protein down by roughly 42-45%, on top of statins, alongside falls in weight, HbA1c and
blood pressure [@belalcazar2013]. Nearly every surrogate moved
the right way — and the events did not follow -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]].

### The mortality picture: no cardiac benefit, but all-cause deaths fall

The higher-tier evidence is the meta-analysis. **Ma 2017** pooled 54 RCTs of 30,206 mostly non-diabetic obese
adults and split cleanly: on the heart it *confirms* Look AHEAD — cardiovascular events RR 0.93 (0.83-1.04,
NS) and cardiovascular mortality RR 0.93 (0.67-1.31, NS) — while all-cause mortality *falls*, RR 0.82
(0.71-0.95, high certainty; \~6 fewer deaths per 1000)
[@ma2017]. Since cardiovascular events are null, that
benefit is not routed through the heart. Removing the dominant Look AHEAD trial leaves it intact and larger
(RR 0.78, 0.63-0.96; I2 0%), so it is not an artefact of one trial. One confound: the trials were «usually low
in fat and saturated fat», so part of the benefit could travel the fat-quality channel rather than weight loss
per se [inferred from @ma2017].

### Bigger doses and the drug route do reach hard outcomes

Two anchors show the lever is real at the ends of the dose range. **SOS**, a matched non-randomized cohort of
bariatric surgery in severe obesity (\~14-25% loss sustained at 10 years), found reduced all-cause mortality —
adjusted HR 0.71 (0.54-0.92) — converging with Ma on the one quantity they share
[@sjostrom2007]. It is not an RCT (patients
self-selected for surgery), and its own authors decline to attribute the benefit to weight loss per se rather
than to other effects of surgery, so it warrants the extreme-dose point directionally, not cleanly. And
**SELECT** showed the drug route *does* cut events: semaglutide reduced major cardiovascular events by 20% (HR
0.80, 0.72-0.90; absolute risk reduction \~1.5 points over 3.3 years, NNT \~67) on top of standard secondary-
prevention care [@lincoff2023]
-> [[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]. But SELECT enrolled *only* established-cardiovascular-
disease patients; the trialists state plainly that primary prevention «was not studied». So a hard-outcome
benefit of weight loss is unproven by the lifestyle route and proven only in secondary-prevention obesity by
the drug route — neither establishes that shedding weight, by itself, prevents events in a low-risk person.

That *what you eat* can carry the cardiac signal that *how much you weigh* did not is sharpened by PREDIMED, in
which an energy-unrestricted Mediterranean pattern with little weight change cut the cardiovascular composite
by \~30% (HR 0.70, 0.55-0.89) in high-risk primary prevention
[@estruch2018] — a reasoned cross-trial contrast, not a head-to-head,
and its own all-cause mortality was null [inferred from @estruch2018; @look2013].

**The defensible reading.** Pursue fat loss for the outcomes it demonstrably moves (below), and treat a
lifestyle-route reduction in cardiac events as unproven. Absolute benefit scales with baseline risk, so even a
true small cardiac effect is smallest exactly where risk is lowest
-> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

## Target diabetes remission by the kilograms lost, not the diet's name

**DiRECT** is the sharpest case of a patient-important outcome that fat loss plainly moves. An energy-restricted
total-diet-replacement programme delivered in routine primary care put **46% of short-duration type 2 diabetes
patients into remission** off all antidiabetic drugs at 12 months, versus 4% on standard care (OR 19.7,
7.8-49.8), with a mean loss of 10.0 kg and a measured quality-of-life gain
[@lean2018] -> [[Total Diet Replacement and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]].
The intervention was a low-*energy* target (an 825-853 kcal/day formula, 59% carbohydrate) with a structured
maintenance phase — notably not a low-carbohydrate one.

The jewel is a dose-response. Pooling both randomized groups, remission rises monotonically with the weight
maintained at 12 months: 0% among those who gained, 7% at 0-5 kg lost, 34% at 5-10 kg, 57% at 10-15 kg, and
86% at >=15 kg [@lean2018]. Read as a curve, it is monotone over the studied
range with no visible plateau below 15 kg — every increment of sustained loss buys more remission; whether it
plateaus above 15 kg is unresolved, and the gradient is a within-population association carrying responder
confounding, not the randomized contrast.

**Weight lost, not carbohydrate cut, is the operative variable.** The fabric also holds a low-carbohydrate
remission result (Goldenberg 2021), but its remission and weight advantages both attenuate to null by 12 months
as weight is regained (weight difference +0.29 kg at 12 months; medication-free remission RD -0.04, NS)
[@goldenberg2021]. A remission effect that decays in step
with the weight advantage is consistent with a *weight-loss* effect delivered by a low-carb route, not a
carbohydrate effect independent of weight — and DiRECT reaches remission at 59% carbohydrate, so carbohydrate
restriction is not necessary for it. The person should optimize for the weight they can sustain, by whichever
route they will adhere to [inferred from @lean2018; @goldenberg2021]
-> [[Carbohydrate Restriction and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]].

Two boundaries. First, remission is an intermediate outcome — a strong surrogate for microvascular risk that
can relapse, not a demonstrated reduction in hard events. Second, DiRECT excluded normal-weight patients (it
required BMI >=27), yet the personal-fat-threshold frame extends the lever to the \~third of type 2 diabetes
that is normal-weight, and at a *smaller* required loss (\~13% at normal BMI vs \~21% for the whole cohort to
reach normoglycaemia) — a decision-change DiRECT's own population cannot license but the mechanism supports
[@taylor2015pft].

## Fatty liver responds to the same weight lever, dosed to its stage

MASLD (fatty liver) is effectively present by definition in a person with obesity plus a metabolic risk
factor, and weight loss is its base-layer treatment with a dose-dependent, outcome-specific histological
benefit -> [[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]. EASL's ladder: **>=5%** sustained loss reduces liver fat,
**7-10%** improves inflammation, **>=10%** improves fibrosis — «There is a dose-dependent association between
the amount of weight loss and the extent of improvement in biomarkers of liver damage»
[@easl2024]. Three caveats travel with the ladder: it
rests on a single histology-endpoint trial (read it as name-the-curve guidance, not a precise dose); few
reach the threshold and most partly regain (maximal loss at 6 months, \~5% net by 12-24 months); and there is
no proven lifestyle effect on advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis — the lever works on the reversible stages.

Two reframes matter for the decision. Liver fat *is* the ectopic depot the previous sections put at the centre
of cardiometabolic risk, so the weight-loss ladder is depot-drawdown made measurable, and the levers are the
*same* ones already indicated for the metabolic patient — MASLD adds a reason and a target, not a separate
intervention.

And for most people with MASLD the dominant risk it carries is *not* hepatic: steatosis per se
is nearly benign for the liver (liver risk tracks fibrosis, not fat), while the extrahepatic load is
substantial — non-fatal cardiovascular disease HR 1.40 and a more-than-two-fold higher incidence of type 2
diabetes [@easl2024]. Steer by the cardiovascular
risk, and monitor fibrosis rather than fat. These extrahepatic figures are observational associations; the
corpus holds no Mendelian-randomization or umbrella evidence pinning the causal arrow from liver fat *to*
diabetes and cardiovascular disease rather than all three tracking the same ectopic-fat cause — so read
them as risk-marking, not as an independent second lever.

## Body fatness causes cancer at many sites; whether losing it reverses the risk is untested

Greater body fatness is a convincing or probable cause of a broad set of cancers — «12 of the 17 cancers
reviewed» by WCRF's Continuous Update Project are linked to it, with three further sites (thyroid, multiple
myeloma, meningioma) added independently by IARC [@wcrf2018ter].
WCRF presents the weight recommendation first because the evidence is «particularly strong», and states that
«Maintaining a healthy weight throughout life is one of the most important ways to protect against cancer»
[@wcrf2018ter] -> [[Body Fatness and Cancer Risk]]. Risk
rises even within the so-called healthy BMI range, though most of the avoidable burden sits in avoiding
overweight and obesity. Plausible mechanisms — hyperinsulinaemia, elevated sex hormones, chronic inflammation
— inform direction, not proof.

Two honesty notes. The grades are on body fatness *causing incidence* in cohorts; whether *losing* weight
reduces a given person's cancer risk is not established (the survivor evidence is explicitly limited) — the
reversal loop is open. And body fatness in *young adulthood* runs the opposite way for breast cancer, an
opposite-direction, only-probable-graded association with no stated mechanism and obvious confounders — a
candidate artifact, read as a unit-of-analysis warning rather than a lever
[inferred from @wcrf2018ter].

## Each method moves a different outcome, so choose for the target and for adherence

Diet, exercise, drugs and surgery all produce a negative energy balance, but they are different exposures with
different tissue effects, and judging them means naming the outcome and the realistic alternative, not
comparing them against an ideal.

### For the scale, diet leads; for the risk-bearing depot, exercise does

At a *matched* weekly energy deficit, exercise removes more visceral fat than caloric restriction (deficit-
controlled head-to-head effect size -0.18, 95% CI -0.33 to -0.04), and only exercise is dose-dependent
(-0.15 per 1000 kcal/week; caloric restriction's slope flat at 0.03, likely a power artifact on fewer studies)
[@recchia2023]
-> [[Exercise vs Caloric Restriction for Visceral Fat]]. The mechanism: diet spends part of its deficit on lean
tissue, exercise concentrates it on fat, and exercise's fat loss is partly weight-*independent*. These are
standardized effect sizes on a surrogate, not absolute volumes or demonstrated events, and the numbers are
small — a refinement lever for a stratum where the big rock is already being addressed
[inferred from @recchia2023].

### Exercise under-delivers on the scale because the deficit is partly refilled

A substantial fraction of exercise energy is offset. Riou's review of 61 studies found mean compensation \~18%
(with an enormous ±93% spread — the individual response ranges from over-compensation to none), rising toward
84% over long interventions [@riou2015]. Careau's
doubly-labelled-water landmark reaches the same conclusion by a different method — \~28% offset via reduced
basal energy expenditure — and adds an adiposity gradient: people at the 90th BMI percentile compensate \~49%
versus \~28% at the 10th [@careau2021]. So exercise "counts"
least toward the deficit exactly where the weight goal is greatest. Crucially, intensity is *not* what drives
compensation, so HIIT is not self-defeating — plan weight change around intake, and use exercise for the
fitness, function and mortality benefits that do not run through the compensated calories
-> [[Exercise Energy Compensation]].

### The drug and surgical routes reach the same lever from outside

Semaglutide is a pharmacological route to the weight lever (\~9-15% loss depending on population), and
tirzepatide takes off more still, approaching the surgical range on the weight surrogate; bariatric surgery
delivers the largest, most durable loss. These carry their own benefit-risk appraisals — cardiovascular
evidence, tolerability tax, muscle loss, reversibility — held on [[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]
and [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]]. This deliverable names them as methods that deliver the same fat-loss lever a
lifestyle change targets, and they are combinable, not either/or; the swing factors — adherence, cost,
tolerability, reversibility — are elicited per person
[inferred from @lincoff2023].

The rate-versus-regain magnitude of loss, the muscle lost per unit of speed, and the head-to-head body-
composition difference between a diet deficit and an exercise deficit are **named gaps** — the corpus holds no
magnitude for them, and none is invented here.

## Losing fat costs muscle; defend it, above all in older adults

Any energy deficit sheds lean mass alongside fat, so the *composition* of loss is a decision, not a detail.
For a young, muscular obese adult this is minor and recoverable. For anyone whose muscle is already the binding
constraint it is a patient-important harm, because low muscle mass predicts mortality: de Santana's meta-
analysis of 10,028 older adults found those who died carried \~0.18 SD less appendicular muscle (SMD -0.18,
-0.23 to -0.12), a small but tight signal not fully explained by strength, and *stronger* at higher BMI —
sarcopenic obesity [@santana2021]
-> [[Low Muscle Mass and Mortality]]. This is a predictor, not a proven target: no trial shows that raising
muscle mass lowers mortality, and lean mass is itself a surrogate for function, which was not measured.

The defense is the ordinary muscle-preservation machinery. Resistance training is the primary lever under any
deficit, with protein a modest adjunct (\~1.6 g/kg/day suffices at energy balance, where RT is the driver)
[@morton2018]
-> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]. A deficit *raises* the retention target above
that figure — Refalo's exploratory meta-regression puts the fat-free-mass-holding intake near \~1.9 g/kgBM (or
\~2.5 g/kgFFM), with leaner people benefiting more per gram [@refalo2025]
-> [[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]]. The numbers are a directional prior for the untrained obese
person, off Refalo's support, but the direction is robust and the collision is the point.

On a GLP-1 drug the collision sharpens. Across trials, GLP-1 loss is \~30% lean as a proportion — comparable to
diet-induced and bariatric loss, so the class is *not* special on average — but semaglutide is the outlier at
up to 45%, and in absolute terms muscle falls (semaglutide -5.44 kg, -7.07 to -3.81)
[@laverde2026] -> [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]]. The ratio
face governs for the young; the absolute face governs for the older or sarcopenia-risk adult — and there the
drug cuts appetite precisely when the muscle-sparing protein target has gone up, so deliberate protein and
resistance training matter most where they are hardest -> [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]].

## The body defends the higher weight, so maintenance is a separate problem

The folk model reads regain after a diet as a lapse of resolve. The mechanistic alternative is that the body
defends the weight it has lost. Sumithran's study found that after diet-induced loss, appetite-regulating
hormones shift toward eating and storage — leptin down, ghrelin up, satiety signals suppressed — and *those
shifts persist a full year later, after regain has already begun*
[@sumithran2011]
-> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]. The appetite channel is the larger driver: intake
rises \~100 kcal/day per kilogram lost, «more than 3-fold larger» than the parallel drop in energy expenditure
[@hallguo2017]. The evidence here is a single small
uncontrolled study plus a review, and the causal step to regain is associational, so this is held at low
confidence — but it reframes the decision: maintenance is not *weight loss, continued*; it is a distinct
problem against an active biological headwind, which is why a structural or pharmacological prop can beat
unaided willpower, and why the drug route rebounds when stopped [inferred from @sumithran2011].

This also answers a common objection — that yo-yo dieting is worse than staying heavy. On the best available
(narrative-review, low-certainty) evidence, the weight-cycling harm signal is confounded, inconsistent, and
concentrated in normal-weight body-image dieters, not in the obese or metabolically-ill person for whom loss
is indicated; the associations attenuate on adjustment for overall weight status
[@montani2015cycling]
-> [[Weight Cycling and Cardiometabolic Risk]]. Fear of cycling is not a reason to avoid trying; it is a reason
to build a maintenance phase.

## The bottom line

- **Ask where the fat sits, not only how much there is.** Waist circumference and liver fat mark the risk-
  bearing intra-organ depot; a normal weight does not rule risk out, and a high one does not make it
  inevitable. Body-fat percentage beats BMI but is still a total measure.
- **Pursue fat loss for the outcomes it demonstrably moves** — diabetes remission, fatty-liver regression,
  glucose control, function — and do not count on a lifestyle-route reduction in heart attacks, which the
  largest trial did not show.
- **For diabetes remission, aim at the kilograms you can sustain,** not a particular macronutrient; the
  remission rate climbs monotonically with sustained loss, reaching \~86% at >=15 kg.
- **Dose fat loss to the target where a threshold exists** — >=5% for liver fat, 7-10% for its inflammation,
  >=10% for fibrosis — reading these as guidance, not precision.
- **Match the method to the outcome and to what you can keep up:** diet for the scale, exercise for the
  visceral depot and for fitness, drugs or surgery as routes to the same lever; plan weight change around
  intake, since exercise energy is partly refilled.
- **Protect muscle while you lose fat** with resistance training and adequate protein, and treat this as
  front-line — not cosmetic — once you are older or at sarcopenia risk.
- **Plan maintenance as its own problem.** Regain has a physiological driver; a standing structural prop beats
  relying on willpower, and fear of regain is not a reason not to start.

---

**Four caveats bound everything above.**

- **The loop is open.** This grades coherence and fidelity to the sources, never validity. No operation here
  checks a recommendation against a realized outcome — an internally sound, source-faithful reading can still
  be wrong about the world.
- **It appraises, it does not prescribe.** Selecting, dosing, titrating and screening for contraindications
  are prescriber acts that need this person's labs, medications and history, which the wiki does not hold.
- **The estimates are population-level; stratify per person.** Absolute benefit scales with baseline risk, and
  where fat loss can harm (the frail elderly) or begins below the BMI cut-point (normal-weight diabetes) the
  answer changes. Place a person in the narrowest reference class the evidence supports, and reason about how
  they differ from it.
- **One health axis only.** This holds longevity, healthspan, function and metabolic health. It carries no
  cost, convenience, body-image or ethical axis, and does not price any method's non-health load against its
  health effect — where such a trade-off exists, it is named and left to the person.
