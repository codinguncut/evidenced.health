## Two changes help nearly everyone in this group; three others come first if they apply

> [!warning] Applies to a specific group
> This appraisal is written for **a Western adult in their late 40s with central weight gain, high
> triglycerides and borderline blood pressure or glucose, not diagnosed with a disease**. If you are not
> in it, the recommendations here may not transfer — the population-level picture can differ. Other groups
> rank the same levers differently; for adults past about 70, see [[Big Rocks (Elderly)]] as further reading.

For this group, **moving more, at any intensity, and losing
weight by eating less** carry the largest held benefit. If you smoke, drink heavily or sleep about five hours a night, those come first.
Blood pressure and cholesterol matter in proportion to your overall risk. Sugar, carbohydrates, saturated fat and
salt — the diet arguments that get the most attention — move outcomes less than these. ->
[[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

Blood pressure and apoB both have mature drugs, and a drug stands in for a lifestyle lever only on the outcome the
drug covers. A blood-pressure pill does nothing for liver fat or diabetes risk, so weight loss keeps its value
there; movement has no drug substitute at all. -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]

### Where each finding stands

**Overall confidence: medium.** The order holds whichever of the conditional levers you have. The
sources behind this guide do not give the absolute size of the top two levers at this group's own baseline risk;
the last section says why [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

| Lever | Applies to | Size, in words | Evidence behind it | Robust or conditional |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stop smoking | smokers | dominant where present | very large cohorts | conditional on smoking |
| Cut heavy drinking | heavy drinkers | big where present | cohorts and genetic studies | conditional on dose |
| Sleep more | chronic short sleepers (about five hours) | medium where present | self-report cohorts; standard advice, not trial-tested | conditional |
| Move more, from the least active band up | nearly everyone | big | device-measured cohorts | robust in direction |
| Sustained weight loss through eating less | anyone carrying central or ectopic excess fat | big on metabolic outcomes | trials | conditional on central fat and metabolic profile |
| Lower apoB | everyone, sized by absolute risk | small over a decade, larger over a lifetime | drug trials; diet reaches markers only | conditional on baseline risk |
| Lower blood pressure | everyone, sized by absolute risk | small over a decade, larger over a lifetime | drug trials; lifestyle effects carried over | conditional on baseline risk |
| Sugar, carbohydrate, saturated-fat and salt swaps | everyone | small | trials and cohorts, parts contested | small or contested |
| Healthy pattern, plant foods | everyone | medium | strong guidance grade; mostly observational | robust direction, small causal core |
| Supplements when replete, meal timing, coffee, red meat, ultra-processed category | everyone | at the ceiling | trial nulls; observational | small or null |

## If you smoke, drink heavily or sleep five hours a night, start there

**If any of the three is present, it comes first.** They are not equal: smoking carries the largest and
best-established effect, alcohol a dose-dependent one, and short sleep the thinnest evidence of the three.
 -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

**Stop smoking, at any age.** Current smokers died at about three times the rate of people who had never
smoked, HR 3.0 (99% CI 2.7-3.3) in women and 2.8 (2.4-3.1) in men. People who quit at 45-54, this guide's age
band, still gained about 6 years [@jha2013] ->
[[Smoking and Mortality]].

**Drink less; moderate drinking does not protect.** Stockwell's analysis corrected for ill former drinkers
counted as abstainers, and for study quality. Low-volume drinking then sat at RR 0.97 (0.88-1.07) against
abstainers, and the J-shaped curve was no longer seen
[@stockwell2016].

Pooled across both sexes, all-cause
risk was raised at 45-64 g a day, RR 1.19 (1.07-1.32). In women alone it was already raised at 25-44 g a day,
RR 1.21 (1.08-1.36). At 25-44 g a day the pooled estimate was RR 1.05 (0.96-1.14), P=.28: above 1, not
significant, and no evidence of safety [@zhao2023].
**These intakes mark where the signal becomes clear, not a permitted ceiling.**

For cancer, risk rises from low
intake: mouth and pharynx, oesophageal (squamous cell) and breast cancers were raised even at about one drink a
day or less, breast RR 1.04 (1.01-1.07)
[@bagnardi2014] ->
[[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]], [[Alcohol and Cancer Risk]].

**Fix chronic short sleep, not six hours.** Against 7 hours, 6 h sat at RR 1.01 (1.00-1.01) and 5 h at 1.04,
steepening below that [@yin2017]. The cohorts
relied on self-reported sleep. A habitual five-hour sleeper should aim well past six hours, toward 7: standard
advice, not trial-tested. Long sleep is not a target in either direction ->
[[Sleep Duration and Mortality]], [[Sleep and Metabolic Health]].

## Move more at any intensity; the first step up from the least-active band is the biggest

**Add movement of any kind, starting with the least active, and keep adding until roughly 20-40 minutes a day
of brisk walking or the equivalent. Past that, the mortality curve flattens.** Move more and
sit less. This does not mean formal aerobic training: light, incidental movement counts toward the
total. -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]

**Headline.** In a harmonised meta-analysis of eight accelerometer cohorts (adults aged 40 and over), adults in
the second quarter of total activity had about half the death rate of the least active quarter: **HR 0.48
(0.43-0.54)**. That 0.48 ranks people on all movement the hip device registers. Moderate-to-vigorous activity
(MVPA), run as a separate analysis, gave a second-quarter HR of 0.64 (0.55-0.74), so the halving does not split
into minutes of exercise [@ekelundacc2019]. These
are cohorts, not trials, and they handled reverse causation only by dropping early deaths. In absolute terms,
the second step quartile (median 5801 steps a day) had 65 fewer deaths per 1000 people (72 to 58 fewer) than the
first (3553) over a median 7.1 years. That figure is unadjusted
[@paluch2022].

**Who and how much.** The gain depends on where a person starts. Going from 1 to 6 minutes a day of MVPA was
associated with about 30% lower mortality, and going from 9 to 14 with about 18% lower; beyond a little over 20
minutes a day no clear further reduction was evident
[@ekelund2026small].

Someone near-inactive can start, as a labelled default, with about 5-10 extra minutes a day of walking, or about
an hour a day more light activity: standing practice, not a trial-tested dose. The curve flattens into a plateau,
and its low point is not a hard threshold; count the weekly total (at least 150 minutes of moderate activity)
instead. On device data, cutting
sitting time and adding movement are one change
[@ekelund2026small] ->
[[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]].

**Harm: start gradually if you are starting from nothing.** Exercise-related sudden cardiac death concentrates
in habitually sedentary people with known or hidden heart disease doing unaccustomed strenuous work
[@franklin2020acute]. Franklin calls it very rare: 0.31
to 2.1 sudden cardiac deaths per 100,000 person-years
[@franklin2020acute], a population rate, not
a per-session one. An inactive adult without symptoms or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease can
start light-to-moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up over about 2-3 months
[@riebe2015screen]. Anyone with known disease or
symptoms gets clearance first. That is expert consensus, not a trial of screening
[[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]].

**Add strength training at modest volume.** Default to strength work on two or more days a week, about
30-60 minutes a week in total, where Momma's pooled curve shows its largest reduction
[@momma2022]. The same meta-analysis gave
all-cause RR 0.85 (0.79-0.93) at very low GRADE certainty
[@momma2022].

In three large US cohorts
(147,374 people), the top category of 120 or more minutes a week still had lower mortality than no strength
work, HR 0.92 (0.86-0.98), with no additional benefit over lower amounts
[@zhang2026rtmort]. That category averaged 204 minutes.
Momma's pooled all-cause
curve returns to 1.0 near 140 minutes, and its lower 95% band sits above 1.0 from about 165 minutes to the end
of its plotted range near 195
[inferred from @momma2022]. In these cohorts more than 2 hours a week still beat none but was not shown to buy
more. The far upper tail is not shown safe: a ceiling, not a warning -> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]].

## Lose weight by eating less, for diabetes, liver and blood pressure, not for a promised heart-attack reduction

**For weight, the lever is a sustained energy deficit, and the diet that works is the one you can keep to.**
For liver and visceral fat, composition is an open question ->
[[What Drives Fat Gain - Energy Balance vs the Carbohydrate-Insulin Model]],
[[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]].

**Headline.** In the Diabetes Prevention Program, adults with prediabetes (mean age 51, mean BMI 34) on an
intensive lifestyle programme lost 5.6 kg on average and **cut new diabetes by 58% (95% CI 48-66%)** against
placebo over an average 2.8 years. Metformin cut it by 31% (17-43%). About seven people (6.9, 5.4-9.5) had to
join the programme for three years to prevent one case
[@knowler2002] ->
[[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]].

**Who.** Waist measures predict death independently of BMI: risk began to rise from a waist-to-height ratio of
0.50 and was significantly higher by 0.55
[@jayedi2020central]. These are baseline
associations, not a measured benefit of shrinking a waist. Where neither waist nor metabolic profile is raised,
no held study measures a benefit or a harm -> [[Central Adiposity and All-Cause Mortality]].

**Liver.** EASL sets its targets as a share of starting weight: at least 5% to reduce liver fat, 7-10% to
improve liver inflammation (MASH) and at least 10% to improve fibrosis
[@easl2024]. These three targets trace to a single trial with
liver biopsies, so read the cut-offs as where the trials sampled, not as precise thresholds
[[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]].

**Weight-loss trials cut deaths but have not shown fewer cardiovascular events.** In 54 RCTs of obese adults,
cardiovascular events came out at RR 0.93 (0.83-1.04). All-cause mortality fell, RR 0.82 (0.71-0.95),
high-quality evidence, about six fewer deaths per 1000 (2 to 10) over a median two years
[@ma2017]. The trials did not show fewer heart
attacks and strokes, and in a low-risk, non-diabetic group that question is unanswered, not
settled -> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].

**Expect regain, and plan the maintenance before you start.** The US guideline recommends, at Grade A,
**a maintenance programme for at least a year**: monthly or more frequent contact, 200-300 min/week of activity
and weighing weekly or more [@jensen2014obesity]. In 24
energy-restricted trials of overweight and obese adults without structured exercise, raising protein from about
0.7 to about 1.25 g/kg/day kept 0.43 kg more fat-free mass (0.09-0.78)
[@wycherley2012]. Treat that intake as
**a floor and a low-cost adjunct**, not a large lever. No held review says how much protein an obese adult in a
deficit needs, a named gap
 ->
[[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]], [[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]].

**Semaglutide, the drug comparator, works while it is taken.** In STEP-1 (no diabetes, mean age 46), semaglutide 2.4 mg gave
12.4 percentage points more weight loss than placebo at 68 weeks (95% CI 11.5-13.4)
[@wilding2021]. When drug and lifestyle support
both stopped, participants regained two-thirds of the lost weight within a year, and most cardiometabolic gains
reverted [@wilding2022]. No trial has tested semaglutide's effect on events in people
without cardiovascular disease -> [[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]].

People with an already-diagnosed type 2 diabetes fall outside this group, and their remission is a separate
question ->
[[Total Diet Replacement and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]].

## Measure apoB when it adds, and lower it by a route that moves the particles

**Read non-HDL-C off a standard lipid panel; lower it first by diet; weigh a statin by absolute risk, with its
harms counted at the same scale.** -> [[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]

**Why apoB in this group.** The European Atherosclerosis Society consensus judges that LDL and the other
apoB-carrying particles cause atherosclerotic disease. It also names the catch for this group: in metabolic
syndrome, diabetes and high triglycerides, LDL-C can under-state the particle burden
[@ference2017]. The 2026 US guideline says apoB testing can
be useful once LDL-C and non-HDL-C goals are met, particularly with triglycerides of 150 mg/dL or more,
diabetes, or a low achieved LDL-C [@accaha2026]. No trial has
tested a measure-apoB strategy against outcomes -> [[ApoB Particle Number vs Cholesterol Content]].

**Headline (the drug comparator).** Per 1 mmol/L of LDL-C lowering, statins cut major vascular events, RR 0.78
(0.76-0.80), across 26 RCTs [@ctt2010] ->
[[LDL Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**The diet route.** Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat changes LDL-C by -0.055 mmol/L per 1% of
energy (-0.061 to -0.050), High certainty
[@who2023saturated]. Portfolio foods (nuts, soy and pulses,
viscous fibre, plant sterols) are the other held route [[Portfolio Dietary Pattern and LDL Cholesterol]]. A
diet that lowers LDL does not inherit the statin's event reduction: a cholesterol fall transmits only if the particle
count falls with it and nothing else harms
[@ference2017]. **No held trial shows any diet lowering
hard cardiovascular events through apoB; that link is insufficient evidence, not a null.**

**Who gets a statin.** For adults aged 40-75 with at least one risk factor, USPSTF recommends a statin at a
10-year risk of 10% or more, offers it selectively from 7.5%, and makes no recommendation below that
[@uspstf2022]. Over a lifetime the arithmetic changes ->
[[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]], [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]].

**Statin harms.** Over 5 years of 1 mmol/L LDL-C lowering, CTT estimates about 11 episodes of muscle pain or
weakness per 1000 people, generally mild, against 25 major vascular events prevented in primary prevention
[@reith2022muscle]. New diabetes is a real drug effect: RR 1.10
(1.04-1.16) on low or moderate intensity, about 0.12% a year, and RR 1.36 (1.25-1.48) on high intensity. About
62% of the excess was in people already in the top quarter of baseline glycaemia
[@reith2024diabetes], where a reader with borderline glucose sits ->
[[Statins and New-Onset Diabetes]].

## Size blood pressure by absolute risk; try the non-drug levers first

**Get an absolute-risk estimate, over a lifetime as well as ten years, and run a three-to-six-month non-drug
trial before deciding on a drug.** Both steps are a labelled default: guidance and practice, not trial-tested
 -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]],
[[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

**Headline.** Each 5 mmHg fall in systolic pressure lowered major cardiovascular events by about 9% in people
without prior cardiovascular disease, HR 0.91 (0.89-0.94). Those participants averaged 65 years old, and the
comparator group had 31.9 major events per 1000 person-years
[@bplttc2021]. Carried over to
this lower baseline, a 5 mmHg fall buys about 2 fewer cardiovascular events per 1000 over about 4.4 years
(roughly 1.5 to 2.8) -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

**Benefit and harm of starting a drug at stage 1.** A Cochrane review of untreated stage-1 hypertension
(140-159/90-99 mmHg) without cardiovascular disease, five trials, weighted mean age 50, rated **both benefit and harm low
certainty**. Total cardiovascular events came out at RR 0.93 (0.69-1.24), while stroke fell, RR 0.41
(0.20-0.84), 7 vs 3 per 1000, from only 35 events, most of them in a trial of people with microalbuminuria. On
harm, withdrawals for adverse effects rose from 23 to 110 per 1000, RR 4.80 (4.14-5.57), from one single-blind
1980s trial [@wang2025mildhtn].

On a current drug
pair, HOPE-3 found 3.4% vs 2.0% stopping for low blood pressure or dizziness over 5.6 years, in older people who
had passed an active run-in [@lonn2016hope3]. On total cardiovascular events the Cochrane result is
insufficient evidence, not a null. A withdrawal is reversible and a stroke is not, so this guide does not net one
against the other.

**The non-drug levers.** Movement and weight loss (above), and:

- **A potassium-enriched salt substitute for home-added salt.** A 25-40% potassium chloride substitute lowered
  systolic pressure by 4.64 mmHg (-6.05 to -3.24) across 18 trials. **Contraindication:** Lai tells clinicians
  to rule out kidney problems and hyperkalaemia risk first
  [@lai2026salt]. SSaSS excluded households in
  which anyone used a potassium-sparing diuretic or a potassium supplement or had known serious kidney disease
  [@neal2021]. For those groups the substitute is
  contraindicated and its safety untested -> [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].
- **Less salt where intake is high.** Cutting sodium lowered systolic pressure by 1.38 mmHg (0.02-2.74; 6
  studies, moderate certainty) with normal blood pressure and by 4.06 mmHg (2.96-5.15; 24 studies, high
  certainty) with hypertension [@who2012]; someone with a
  borderline reading should expect a fall between the two. In most Western countries salt intake runs at 9-10 g a day against a recommended maximum of 5 g [@esc2021]. -> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

Each of these lowers blood pressure, a surrogate; no trial has tested whether a lifestyle fall cuts events at the
drug trials' rate -> [[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]].

## The composition levers are smaller than their attention

**Saturated fat, carbohydrate, sugar and salt each have a measured effect, and each is smaller than
movement, weight loss or the risk-scaled levers above.** None is zero, and a few cost almost nothing to act
on. -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

**Saturated fat: replace butter with plant oils.** In trials, lower saturated-fat intake reduced
cardiovascular events, RR 0.83 (0.70-0.98), 15 fewer events per 1000 (25 to 2 fewer) at an 8.5% control rate,
moderate certainty; all-cause mortality did not move, RR 0.96 (0.90-1.03)
[@who2023saturated]. The events that fell include
non-fatal heart attacks and strokes, often disabling, so the gain is real even though it does not lengthen life.
WHO recommends replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat, strong recommendation, moderate certainty; the
10% figure marks where WHO's trials thin out
[@who2023saturated]. The trial base is contested ->
[[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]], [[Does Reducing Saturated Fat Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].

**Low-carbohydrate: about a kilogram, and watch the lipids.** In adults without diabetes, low-carbohydrate diets
lost 1.07 kg (0.59-1.55) more than balanced-carbohydrate diets at 3 to under 12 months, moderate certainty, and
no included trial measured death, heart attack, stroke or new diabetes
[@naude2022]. **Harm:** the review advises
caution for people with lipid disorders whose atherogenic lipoproteins rise on a high-fat diet
[@naude2022]; in adults with type 2 diabetes,
LDL-C rose +0.14 mmol/L (-0.00 to 0.28) at 12 months while triglycerides fell -0.32 mmol/L (-0.51 to -0.12)
[@goldenberg2021]. Transfer to this non-diabetic
group is an assumption; anyone who switches can recheck non-HDL-C or apoB after a few months ->
[[Low-Carbohydrate vs Balanced-Carbohydrate Diets]].

**Sugar: cut sugary drinks.** Each extra 250 mL/day of sugar-sweetened drinks tracks a higher type 2 diabetes risk, RR 1.19
(1.13-1.25), across 19 cohorts, observational only
[@qin2020]. Cutting them is cheap and
reversible, so this guide recommends it plainly on an observational signal -> [[Free Sugars Intake]].

**The healthy pattern: right direction, small causal core.** The one pattern RCT with hard outcomes, PREDIMED,
cut heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death to HR 0.70 (0.55-0.89), mostly through stroke. Its
participants were high-risk Spanish adults, about half with type 2 diabetes, and the authors leave transfer to
lower-risk people open [@estruch2018].

Fruit and vegetables
track lower all-cause mortality, RR 0.90 (0.87-0.93) per 200 g/day, in cohorts. **About 500 g/day reaches most
of the reduction seen at 800 g/day** (27% against 31% lower, compared with 0-40 g/day). For coronary heart
disease the gradient keeps going (16% against 24%), and 800 g/day is the top of the studied range
[@aune2017fv]. Cohorts agree on the
direction of plant-rich patterns, but trials have shown only a small causal share ->
[[Dietary Patterns and All-Cause Mortality]], [[Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Health]].

## What can be left alone, and what is not known

### Levers you can leave alone

- **Supplements when not deficient.** Vitamin D and fish oil did not lower major cardiovascular events;
  beta-carotene raised lung cancer in smokers and asbestos-exposed workers (RR 1.18, 1.03-1.36)
  [@uspstf2022supp] ->
  [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].
- **Meal timing.** A shorter eating window adds nothing to the same calorie deficit
  [@semnaniazad2025]. One trial
  (TREAT) found a lean-mass cost (appendicular, -0.47 kg, -0.82 to -0.12)
  [@lowe2020], and a 12-month trial with calorie
  restriction in both arms did not [@liu2022]
  -> [[Time-Restricted Eating]].
- **Coffee.** No health reason to start or to quit, outside pregnancy and raised fracture risk. Someone with high
  LDL who drinks a lot of unfiltered (boiled) coffee can switch to filtered
  -> [[Coffee Consumption and Health]].
- **Ultra-processed food** is an observational signal with candidate mechanisms, not a tested lever
  -> [[Ultra-Processed Food and Health Outcomes]].
- **Red meat** is a small lever, and processed meat is the better-evidenced half of it ->
  [[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]].
- **Social isolation** predicts earlier death, but no trial shows that reducing it changes the outcome
 -> [[Social Connection and Mortality]].
- **Hormone therapy after menopause.** Hormone therapy treats symptoms and protects bone while it is taken. WHI's investigators concluded that estrogen plus progestin should not be
  started or continued to prevent heart disease
  [@rossouw2002] ->
  [[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]], [[Menopause and the Shifting Levers]].

### What the evidence cannot yet say

- **This stratum's own baseline risk**: SCORE2's under-50 band has no held risk grid, so no held source gives the
  absolute benefit of more movement or weight loss here ->
  [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].
- **Lifestyle weight loss and heart attacks and strokes** in people without diabetes: untested in a trial
 -> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].
- **Trajectory and quality of life** go unmeasured for most levers ->
  [[Ectopic Fat and Depot-Specific Risk]].
- **Harm in the leaner minority**: no study quantifies it; caution there is reasoned, not measured ->
  [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].
- **Cutoffs**: waist and resting heart rate have no outcome-validated cutoffs here ->
  [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]].
- **Protein needs for obese adults in a deficit**, and the survival gain from raising fitness within a person,
  await held reviews.

## The direction matches mainstream guidance; this guide adds an order and outcome labels

On direction, this guide agrees with the guidance bodies it draws on. They say to move more, lose excess
fat, not smoke, keep alcohol low, replace saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat, and cut salt where blood
pressure is high. It adds three things. It ranks levers by effect size and certainty, so the
composition questions sit below movement and weight loss. It labels the effect of lifestyle weight loss on
heart attacks and strokes as untested in a trial in people without diabetes. And it reports that cutting
saturated fat reduced cardiovascular events in trials, while all-cause mortality was not shown to fall. Population guidance also weighs goals beyond one person's health;
this guide does not ->
[[The Weighting Problem - Why Population Guidance Is Ill-Posed and Individual Advice Is Not]].

## Pull the large levers first, then stop

- **Stop smoking**, if you smoke; it comes before everything else here.
- **Drink little.** The intakes where the raised death risk becomes significant are not a permitted ceiling,
  and cancer risk rises from
  low intake.
- **Sleep longer if you now sleep about 5 hours**, toward about 7; standard advice, not trial-tested.
- **Get off the floor of activity**: start with 5-10 extra minutes of walking a day, build toward about 20-40
  minutes of brisk activity, sit less, and add strength work on two or more days a week.
- **Lose weight by eating less if your waist or metabolic profile is raised**, at least 5% of starting weight,
  and plan a year of maintenance from the start; some regain is normal.
- **Measure non-HDL-C and blood pressure, and act on your absolute risk**; with raised pressure, try three to
  six months of non-drug change first.
- **Replace butter with plant oils and cut sugary drinks.**
- **Then stop optimizing.** Supplements, meal timing, coffee and the processed-food label add little on
  current evidence.

### Caveats

- This page grades how well its claims fit their sources, not whether they are true; no step here has been
  checked against what happened to anyone who followed it.
- It appraises evidence and does not prescribe: the drug sections select no drug and no dose.

- It is written for a stratum, and applying it to one person means weighing their own risks, goals and
  constraints.
