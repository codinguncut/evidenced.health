## A treatment's benefit to you depends on your own starting risk

A trial reports how much a treatment cuts risk in relative terms, as a share of events avoided. **What that treatment does for one person depends on that person's own risk of the outcome.** The same relative cut removes a large slice of a large risk and a sliver of a small one.

For statins, and for aspirin across the two risk groups its pooled trials compared, **the relative cut stays about the same from low-risk to high-risk people**, within the ages and risk levels the trials enrolled, so a person's gain is that cut applied to their own baseline risk [@grade] ([[LDL Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]], [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]).

**Blood-pressure drugs hold their relative cut across starting blood pressure** ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]). Across age the pooled trials show a larger cut under 55 and a smaller one in the oldest, but the interaction is borderline and its authors say it may be chance [@rahimi2021bpage] [@rahimi2021bpage]. These are observed patterns, not laws. For statins over 75 without heart disease and blood-pressure drugs at 85 and over, the trials are too thin to confirm the pattern or rule it out ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).

**You can tailor the decision to a person's risk without claiming the treatment works differently in them.** Higher risk means a bigger absolute gain from the same relative effect. The stronger claim, that the relative effect itself differs in some group, needs an interaction test inside a study, and most candidates tested that way came up empty. A few signals stand. For diabetes incidence, one prevention trial found metformin worked far better at high body weight and lifestyle change better at lower post-load glucose, a single-trial result not yet replicated, and a pooled analysis found vitamin D helped only leaner people, as its proposed mechanism predicted ([[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]). For cardiovascular events the signals are weaker: the borderline age gradient for blood-pressure drugs, and one trial's benefit confined to people with high starting systolic pressure ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).

**Harms follow the same arithmetic, on their own baseline.** For aspirin without known heart disease, the pooled trials found the absolute fall in heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths and the absolute rise in major bleeding of similar size at every risk level, so choosing higher-risk people raises both. The outcomes differ in severity, and the weighing is the reader's. A US task force's lifetime model, largely on the same trials, finds a small net gain for higher-risk people in their 40s and 50s not prone to bleeding, and none for starting at 60 or older ([[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]).

**The weakest link is usually the baseline estimate**, not the relative effect. The answer shifts with which risk tool is used, which region it was calibrated for, what horizon it uses and whether the person is already treated. Over ten years older people gain more, because their risk is higher; over a lifetime, younger people gain more years free of heart attack and stroke ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]], [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

**For a low-risk person the proven levers buy small absolute gains.** That is a result, not a failed search: it tells the person where further effort on that outcome stops paying ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]).

## Multiply the relative effect by your own baseline risk

A trial reports a ratio (risk, odds or hazard); a person deciding needs a difference: how many fewer events per 1000 people like them over a stated number of years. **One multiplication gets you there: absolute risk reduction = relative risk reduction x baseline risk.** The relative risk reduction is 1 minus the ratio; the baseline is the person's own untreated risk over the same horizon. GRADE states the same rule [@grade]. A ratio whose confidence interval touches 1 gives an absolute gain whose interval touches zero ([[Reading a Confidence Interval]]).

### One odds ratio, three baselines

Sundström pooled grade-1 hypertension trials without cardiovascular disease (13 comparisons, 15,266 people, about 5 years): odds ratio 0.86 (0.74-1.01) for total cardiovascular events. On three 5-year baselines that one ratio gives [@sundstrom2015mildhtn]:

- **2.4%** (untreated, non-diabetic trial controls; strokes and coronary events only): about 3 fewer events per 1000 (0 to 6).
- **7.4%** (BPLTTC trial controls, 96% with diabetes): about 10 fewer per 1000 (-1 to 19).
- **11.6%** (a primary-care cohort starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB): about 16 fewer per 1000 (-1 to 30).

**The ratio is the same in every row, and the absolute benefit differs about five-fold**, because the baseline does. The lowest row is transported, not measured: the non-diabetic trials' own odds ratio was 0.97 (0.71-1.33) [@sundstrom2015mildhtn].

### Statins hold their ratio across baseline risk, BP drugs across baseline pressure

**Statins.** In CTT's analysis of 26 trials (169,138 people, median about 5 years), each 1.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol cut major vascular events by about a fifth, RR 0.78 (0.76-0.80). The cut held in every subgroup examined, even though control-group event rates differed substantially by medical history [@ctt2010] ([[LDL Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

**Blood-pressure drugs.** In BPLTTC's pool of 48 trials (344,716 people), each 5 mmHg fall in systolic pressure gave a hazard ratio for major cardiovascular events of 0.91 (0.89-0.94) in people without prior cardiovascular disease and 0.89 (0.86-0.92) in those with it. Across seven baseline systolic categories, from below 120 to 170 mmHg and above, the authors found no reliable evidence of heterogeneity, and no subgroup hazard ratio sat above 1.0 [@bplttc2021] ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

One prespecified test inside a single trial points the other way. In HOPE-3, people without cardiovascular disease in the top third of baseline systolic pressure (above 143.5 mmHg) benefited, HR 0.73 (0.56-0.94), while effects were neutral in the lower two thirds (P for trend 0.02, at a nominal threshold) [@lonn2016hope3]. The much larger pool did not find this, so the default stays with the pool [inferred from @lonn2016hope3]. Age is a different matter and is taken up with the horizon.

Stratifying on baseline risk this way (route a) needs prognostic data only, no subgroup claim. Recommendations may differ between risk groups under one relative risk [@grade], with absolute risk from representative populations, because trial event rates are not a reader's baseline [@bplttc2021].

### Departing from the pooled ratio needs evidence from inside a study

Effect modification (route b) means the relative effect itself differs between groups. A plausible mechanism is not enough, because subgroup splits are the commonest source of false positives; **the claim needs an interaction test inside a study.** In the Diabetes Prevention Program, metformin cut diabetes incidence by 3% (-36 to 30) at a BMI of 22 to under 30 and by 53% (36 to 65) at 35 or more, with significant heterogeneity. Its effect was also smaller at lower fasting glucose. Lifestyle change was highly effective in all subgroups, though more so at lower 2-hour glucose after a glucose load. The trial lacked power to assess effects within subgroups, so the stratum figures are imprecise [@knowler2002].

For diabetes prevention, then, **metformin is a drug to use selectively, in the heavier and more hyperglycaemic people it worked in, and lifestyle change is the lever to recommend broadly** ([[Lifestyle vs Metformin for Diabetes Prevention]]) [inferred from @knowler2002].

A pooled analysis of three vitamin D trials in prediabetes adds a stronger design. Cholecalciferol cut diabetes risk below the median BMI of 31.3 (HR 0.74, 0.60-0.90) but not at or above it (1.01, 0.84-1.22; P for interaction 0.023). In the trial that used eldecalcitol, an active form that skips the activation step obesity suppresses, BMI modified nothing (P for interaction 0.82) [@pittas2023vitamindipd]. **The modifier disappears exactly where the proposed mechanism says it should.** The split comes from the two cholecalciferol trials; across all three, the prespecified subgroups did not differ ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]) [inferred from @pittas2023vitamindipd].

**Most modifiers that have been tested came up absent.** Across 262 obesity-drug trials, prespecified subgroups by obesity severity, diabetes status and body-composition method showed no credible subgroup effect [@nong2026obesity]. Within each age band of the blood-pressure trials, baseline systolic pressure did not modify the relative effect [@rahimi2021bpage]. Such an absence means no modification was shown, not that it was excluded [inferred from @nong2026obesity; @rahimi2021bpage].

### Check the form a figure was reported in

- **A guideline's per-1000 figure uses the trial's baseline, not yours.** WHO's fat guideline takes the event rate for its per-1000 figures from the trial control groups or the whole cohort, and notes that real-world absolute effects depend on baseline risk [@who2023saturated].
- **USPSTF puts the absolute figure first.** It generally prioritizes absolute over relative risk reduction and gives less weight to a large relative reduction when the absolute one is small [@uspstfmanual2022] ([[Net Benefit and the USPSTF Recommendation Grid]]).

WHO's own example shows the gap. Its RR of 0.83 (0.70-0.98) for cardiovascular disease with reduced saturated fat, at Moderate certainty [@who2023saturated], becomes 15 fewer per 1000 (25 fewer to 2 fewer) at its trials' 8.5% control rate [@who2023saturated]. **At a 10% SCORE2 baseline it is about 17 fewer per 1000 over 10 years (roughly 2 to 30)**, if the effect holds that long and the endpoints match [inferred from @esc2021; @who2023saturated].

### The same arithmetic outside cardiology

Exercise for falls in community-dwelling older adults has a rate ratio of 0.77 (0.71-0.83) across 59 trials, at high certainty. At the trials' control rate of 850 falls per 1000 people a year, that is 195 (144 to 246) fewer falls per 1000 per year [@sherrington2019] ([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]). In the Diabetes Prevention Program, 6.9 (5.4-9.5) people needed the lifestyle programme for 3 years to prevent one case of diabetes, against 13.9 (8.7-33.9) on metformin [@knowler2002]. This guide has no instrument that gives a person their own baseline for falls or diabetes, so the second factor is missing [inferred from @sherrington2019; @knowler2002].

## Get the baseline from a calibrated instrument, in its own units

**The baseline is a 10-year absolute risk read from a named instrument.** The ESC charts apply only to people whose risk factors are untreated or have been stable for several years [@esc2021], and the 2025 ESC/EAS lipid update says SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP should not be used in people with existing atherosclerotic disease or already on lipid-lowering therapy [@esceas2025]. On those charts a treated person's blood pressure and cholesterol already carry the drug's effect, so the chart would understate the risk the drug is meant to lower. PREVENT is built differently: it takes antihypertensive and statin use as inputs [@khan2024]. **Which instrument produced the number matters as much as the number**.

### Pick the instrument built for your region and population

| Instrument | Endpoint | Ages | Horizon | What sets it apart |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SCORE2 / SCORE2-OP (ESC) | fatal and non-fatal CVD (MI, stroke) | 40-69 / 70+ | 10 y | calibrated to four European risk regions |
| SCORE2-Diabetes (ESC) | same as SCORE2 | over 40 | 10 y | adds HbA1c, age at diagnosis, eGFR |
| PREVENT (AHA) | total CVD (ASCVD plus heart failure); ASCVD and HF sub-models | 30-79 | 10 y and 30 y | race-free, competing-risk adjusted |
| Pooled Cohort Equations (ACC/AHA) | ASCVD | 40-79 | 10 y | overpredicts |

[@esc2021]

**In Europe, SCORE2 is the default, and the region chart does real work**: its four regional calibrations, from low to very high risk [@esc2021], give different numbers for identical inputs ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]). The ESC does not recommend the base model for people with diabetes; **for type 2 diabetes, use SCORE2-Diabetes** [@score2diabetes2023].

**In the US, PREVENT is the successor to the Pooled Cohort Equations.** Its derivation paper puts the older equations' overestimation of ASCVD risk at about 50% (calibration slopes 0.50-0.54) [@khan2024], a figure from PREVENT's own team, not an independent replication [inferred from @khan2024]. USPSTF still scores risk with the Pooled Cohort Equations and notes that external validations show overprediction in broad populations [@uspstf2022].

Inputs only the person can supply mean **the same age and sex can land in different treatment categories on plausible values of them**: a baseline is a case fact, not a property of a stratum ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]).

### A threshold belongs to its instrument and its age band

ESC sets treatment bands on SCORE2 risk, and the bands rise with age because age drives most of the risk [@esc2021]:

| ESC category (SCORE2 / SCORE2-OP) | <50 years | 50-69 years | >=70 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-to-moderate: treatment generally not recommended | <2.5% | <5% | <7.5% |
| High: treatment should be considered | 2.5 to <7.5% | 5 to <10% | 7.5 to <15% |
| Very high: treatment generally recommended | >=7.5% | >=10% | >=15% |

[@esc2021]

An 8% risk is very high at 45 and high at 72. When reading a risk chart, ESC also tells clinicians to adjust the estimate upwards as a person approaches the chart's next age category [@esc2021]. The 2025 lipid update uses a separate set of cut-offs to assign LDL-cholesterol goals ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]).

**USPSTF's statin rule runs on Pooled Cohort Equations risk.** For adults 40-75 with at least one risk factor, it recommends a statin at 10% or more (Grade B) and a selective offer at 7.5% to under 10% (Grade C). At 76 and over it finds the evidence insufficient (I statement) [@uspstf2022]. Below 7.5% the recommendation does not cover the person at all [inferred from @uspstf2022] ([[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]).

### Never carry a number across instruments without converting it

**A Pooled Cohort Equations risk of 7.5% corresponds to a median PREVENT-ASCVD risk of 4.9% in women and 3.7% in men** (ranges 4.4-5.3 and 3.6-4.0, unlabelled in the source). On PREVENT's total-CVD endpoint, which adds heart failure, the same point reads 8.4% in women and 5.9% in men [@khan2024]. So a woman at the USPSTF 7.5% line reads below it on PREVENT-ASCVD and above it on PREVENT total CVD, with nothing about her changed: name the endpoint along with the instrument [inferred from @khan2024]. SCORE2 and the US instruments also count different events, so their percentages cannot be lined up either ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]).

## Let a risk modifier move the estimate only near a threshold

A coronary calcium scan, a high lipoprotein(a) or an ethnic background whose risk differs from the instrument's cohort is information the instrument never sees. ESC calls this a **risk modifier**, and states when it matters:

> «Assessment of potential risk modifiers seems particularly relevant if the individual’s risk is close to a decision threshold. In low-risk or very-high-risk situations, additional information is less likely to alter management decisions.»

[@esc2021]

**A test that cannot carry a person across a treatment threshold cannot change what they do**, however real its link to heart disease -> [[Risk Modifiers - When Extra Information Changes a Risk Estimate]].

ESC sets five criteria for a modifier; very few candidates meet all of them, and ESC gives publication bias in this field's meta-analyses as an example. **A modifier's published relative risk overstates what it does to a calculated absolute risk**: ESC says the shift is generally much smaller than the relative risks reported. How to combine a modifier with the calculator is mostly unknown, and trials testing whether the added information improves health outcomes are generally lacking [@esc2021].

### ESC 2021 grades no modifier above *should be considered*

In ESC's wording, *should be considered* marks class IIa and *may be considered* class IIb [@esc2021]:

- **Ethnicity, IIa/B.** Multiply calculated risk by 1.3 for Indian and Bangladeshi people, 1.7 for Pakistani, 1.1 for other Asian, 0.85 for Black Caribbean, and 0.7 for Black African and Chinese people. The factors come from UK data, and ESC prefers country- and calculator-specific factors where they exist [@esc2021].
- **Coronary artery calcium (CAC), IIb/B.** May be considered for people near decision thresholds; carotid ultrasound for plaque is the alternative.
- **Routine collection of other modifiers, III.** Not recommended; this covers genetic risk scores, circulating biomarkers and other imaging.

**The ethnicity factors can cross a threshold on their own**: a 9% estimate becomes about 15% at 1.7 and about 6% at 0.7. They adjust ESC's own calculators and are not a universal correction; the race-free PREVENT equations have no such term -> [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].

### High Lp(a) raises baseline risk, and with it the payoff from lowering LDL

Lipoprotein(a) is mostly set by genes, and lifestyle barely moves it [@accaha2026]; statins do not lower it either [@esceas2025]. The Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration found the Lp(a)-heart-disease association similar at every level of non-HDL cholesterol, and concluded that cholesterol lowering should bring a larger absolute benefit when Lp(a) is high [@erqou2009lpa]. ACC-AHA puts 125 nmol/L (50 mg/dL) or more at about 1.4-fold ASCVD risk [@accaha2026].

So **a high Lp(a) raises the absolute payoff of LDL lowering, by diet or by drug**, through a higher baseline (route a), with no claim that LDL lowering works differently -> [[Lipoprotein(a) and Cardiovascular Risk]]. The 2025 ESC/EAS update treats Lp(a) above 50 mg/dL (105 nmol/L) as a modifier for people at moderate risk or close to a treatment threshold, says measurement should be considered at least once in every adult's lifetime, and notes that lowering Lp(a) itself has yet to be shown to cut events [@esceas2025].

### A zero calcium score lowers the decision's stakes, not the biology

Nasir and colleagues analysed 4,758 MESA adults aged 45-75 (people over 75 and those on lipid-lowering drugs were excluded) over a median 10.3 years. Of those eligible for a statin under the 2013 US guideline, **44% had CAC = 0**, with an ASCVD event rate of 4.2 per 1,000 person-years against 11.2 for those with any calcium. Applying an assumed 30% relative risk reduction, the ten-year number needed to treat among people recommended a statin was **64 at CAC = 0 versus 28 at CAC above 100**, and 223 versus 46 among those for whom a statin was only to be considered [@nasir2015].

CAC = 0 means low risk, not no risk: in people with diabetes it still carried 4.9 events per 1,000 person-years, an NNT of 69 [@nasir2015]. The assumed 30% matches USPSTF's trial estimate for composite CVD, RR 0.72 (0.64-0.81) [@uspstf2022].

**These numbers are modelled, from one cohort**: observed event rates times a borrowed relative effect, with no trial randomizing statins by calcium score -> [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]. ESC reads a score against what is expected for age and sex: higher-than-expected calcium raises calculated risk, absent or lower-than-expected calcium lowers it, and a zero score misses soft, non-calcified plaque, which can occur in middle-aged patients [@esc2021]. **A zero is informative only where some calcium was expected**; in a young adult it is the expected result and cannot move the estimate down.

At CAC = 0, LDL and apoB remain causal, and a high Lp(a) or a rising apoB can still justify treatment -> [[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]. Familial hypercholesterolaemia, or LDL-C above 190 mg/dL, sits outside USPSTF's risk-based rule altogether, and USPSTF keeps CAC out of its statin rule [@uspstf2022]. ESC grades CAC IIb with no magnitude [@esc2021], and ACC-AHA 2026 maps calcium scores directly onto LDL-C goals [@accaha2026]; this guide records the split rather than choosing a side.

## Fix the horizon before you compare ages

A 10-year horizon is a choice the risk instrument makes for you, and it changes the answer most for the young and the old: **name the horizon every time you compare two ages** ([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

ESC warns that in relatively young, apparently healthy people the 10-year risk is low on average even with high risk-factor levels, while lifetime risk is very high: **women under 50 and men under 40 are almost always at low 10-year risk**, yet may carry modifiable risk factors that sharply raise their longer-term risk [@esc2021]. PREVENT shows the gap for one person. A 50-year-old woman with total cholesterol of 240 mg/dL, treated systolic pressure of 160 mmHg and a BMI of 35, not smoking and not on a statin, has a 10-year CVD risk of 5.4% and a 30-year risk of 31% [@khan2024].

### Over a fixed short horizon, older people gain more

On a fixed horizon, **absolute benefit rises with age, because baseline risk climbs faster than the relative effect shrinks** [inferred from @rahimi2021bpage; @ctt2019]. The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists (CTT) give a primary-prevention example: two people aged 63 and 78 with otherwise identical risk factors might have projected major vascular event rates of 2.5% and 4.0% a year, and a 1.0 mmol/L LDL-C reduction cutting them by a fifth prevents first events each year in 50 of the 63-year-olds and 80 of the 78-year-olds per 10,000 treated [@ctt2019]. The example assumes the relative effect carries over to primary prevention after 75, the cell the trials could not confirm (below) [inferred from @ctt2019] ([[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]).

Across all ages, CTT found a significant reduction in major vascular events in every age group, from RR 0.75 at 55 or younger to 0.87 over 75 per 1.0 mmol/L (figure 1): «Although proportional reductions in major vascular events diminished slightly with age, this trend was not statistically significant (ptrend=0·06).» It stayed non-significant (p 0.3) once the heart-failure and dialysis trials were excluded. For major coronary events the trend to smaller effects with age was significant (p-trend 0.009; 0.01 after that exclusion), though the reduction over 75 remained significant [@ctt2019].

**For blood-pressure drugs, the relative effect may diminish with age.** An individual-participant analysis of 51 trials found hazard ratios per 5 mmHg of 0.82 (0.76-0.88) under 55, 0.91 in each band from 55 to 84, and 0.99 at 85 and over (adjusted p-interaction 0.050), while absolute risk reductions were larger in older bands (adjusted p-interaction 0.024). The authors report «evidence for diminishing relative risk reductions with increasing age» [@rahimi2021bpage]. They add that, with a p-interaction of 0.05 and no meaningful age interaction for ischaemic heart disease or heart failure, a chance finding cannot be ruled out, and relative reductions may be consistent for most participants. Against that, interaction tests are conservative, and shorter treatment in older groups or younger people's fewer risk factors could explain a real gradient [@rahimi2021bpage].

So the under-55 band is evidence for a larger relative cut, about 18% per 5 mmHg (12% to 24%), against 9% in the middle bands, not an established figure. Planning on the middle bands' 9% may understate the benefit under 55 [inferred from @rahimi2021bpage] ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]). Neither collaboration offers its trial-derived absolute figures as a person's own number. CTT says its trial population's absolute risk is not likely to represent any contemporary population [@ctt2019]; the blood-pressure authors say a usable estimate combines the pooled relative effect with absolute risks from contemporary patient registries [@rahimi2021bpage].

### In the oldest groups the relative effect is unconfirmed, not absent

**For statins in people over 75 without known vascular disease, the relative risk per 1.0 mmol/L was 0.92 (99% CI 0.73-1.16)**, on 295 versus 308 events. Among all participants without vascular disease, the relative effect shrank with age: the results call the trend significant (p-trend 0.05, and 0.03 after excluding the heart-failure and dialysis trials), the authors' research-in-context panel weak. They say there were too few such participants for reliable assessment in that group alone. The whole over-75 statin group, with and without vascular disease, did benefit: 0.87 (99% CI 0.77-0.99) [@ctt2019].

**For blood-pressure drugs at 85 and over, the hazard ratio was 0.99 (95% CI 0.87-1.12)**, against 0.91 at 75-84 [@rahimi2021bpage]. The two cells involve different drugs, outcomes and age cut-points: two separate thin results, not one finding about old age [inferred from @ctt2019; @rahimi2021bpage].

**Both cells are insufficient evidence, not evidence of no benefit.** Each interval holds both the younger groups' relative effect and no effect at all. The default stays route (a): carry the nearest younger band's relative effect, not the under-55 one, over to the older person's higher baseline (for blood-pressure drugs at 85 and over, the 75-84 band's 0.91; for statins, the effect seen up to 75), label the result as transported, and widen the uncertainty. At 85 and over the point estimate of 0.99 warns that the true absolute benefit may be smaller than that arithmetic gives ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]], [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]]).

### Frail people were mostly outside the trials

The blood-pressure authors state that frail people, people with heavy multimorbidity and polypharmacy, and people living in institutions were under-represented, and that the findings' generalisability to them remains uncertain [@rahimi2021bpage]. Frailty was never a prespecified subgroup [@rahimi2021bpage]. **For a frail person, route (a) is a transport, not a measured effect** [inferred from @rahimi2021bpage] ([[Frailty]]).

### Over a lifetime, younger people gain more

ESC multiplies a lifetime risk from the LIFE-CVD model by a hazard ratio of 0.78 per 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C. **For a man aged 45-49, 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C buys roughly 0.6 to 1.6 years free of heart attack or stroke**, rising with systolic pressure and non-HDL cholesterol; at 90 and over it buys about 0.0 to 0.3 years. Women gain more than men, and smokers gain less [@esc2021]. These are ranges read from the grid, not cell values.

The unit is median years free of heart attack or stroke, and the endpoint counts death [@esc2021]. **The lifetime figure falls with age for three reasons:** the remaining horizon shrinks, competing causes of death rise, and death sits inside the endpoint ([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).

Cumulative exposure explains why starting earlier pays: the European Atherosclerosis Society consensus holds that any way of lowering LDL particle concentration should cut ASCVD risk in proportion to the absolute LDL-C reduction and to how long it lasts, provided the LDL-C drop matches the drop in particle number and the method carries no off-target harm [@ference2017]. So a modest reduction kept up for decades can outweigh a larger one started late ([[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]).

### ESC uses the lifetime frame in both directions

ESC lets a substantial lifetime benefit justify treatment even when 10-year risk sits in its lowest band, and uses the same construct to set higher 10-year thresholds at 70 and over. **It also bounds the frame: below 40, risk and lifetime-benefit predictions are likely to be imprecise**, lipid- and BP-lowering drugs are not usually considered except in familial hypercholesterolaemia or specific BP disorders, and a healthy lifestyle kept up through life matters more [@esc2021]. ESC asks that lifetime benefit be weighed against how long treatment will last [@esc2021].

The lifetime figures are modelled, not observed. ESC states the model is validated only for low- and moderate-risk countries [@esc2021], and this guide draws on no check of LIFE-CVD's projections against real outcomes.

## Put each harm on its own baseline: aspirin shows why

**A harm runs on the same arithmetic as a benefit: its own relative effect times the person's own baseline risk of that harm**, over a stated horizon. That baseline is not the cardiovascular one, and it need not hold still while cardiovascular risk rises. So benefit and each harm are stated side by side, each with its interval, and **never subtracted into one net number**; how much each counts is the person's call -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

### Aspirin: the benefit and the bleeding rise together

Zheng and Roddick pooled 13 RCTs of daily aspirin in 164,225 people without cardiovascular disease (median 10-year CV risk 10.2%, range 2.6-30.9%; median age 62; median follow-up 5.0 years). Over that horizon aspirin cut the composite of CV death, MI and stroke by an absolute 0.41% (95% CI 0.23-0.59; NNT 241). It raised major bleeding by an absolute 0.47% (0.34-0.62; NNH 210). All-cause mortality did not clearly fall (HR 0.94, 95% CrI 0.88-1.01) [@zheng2019aspirin].

| Stratum (10-year CV risk) | CV composite HR (95% CrI) | ARR (95% CI) | Major bleeding HR (95% CrI) | ARI (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low, <10% (median 6.8%) | 0.87 (0.79-0.95) | 0.34% (0.14-0.52) | 1.45 (1.28-1.63) | 0.40% (0.25-0.57) |
| High, >=10% | 0.91 (0.84-0.98) | 0.63% (0.18-1.04) | 1.41 (1.23-1.61) | 0.64% (0.35-0.97) |
| Diabetes | 0.90 (0.82-1.00) | 0.65% (0.09-1.17) | 1.29 (1.11-1.51) | 0.80% (0.29-1.39) |

[@zheng2019aspirin]

**The relative effects barely move across strata, so this is stratifying on baseline risk (route a).** The diabetes stratum's CV hazard ratio sits close to the overall 0.89, but that is a comparison by eye without an interaction test: no separation, not a tested absence of modification. What moves is the absolute size of both columns: from the low-risk to the high-risk stratum the CV benefit nearly doubles, and so does the bleeding excess.

Zheng's own reading is that the absolute CV reduction and the absolute bleeding increase «were of similar magnitude», and that comparing them turns on how severe each outcome is: aspirin was not associated with a reduction in cardiovascular mortality, and deaths due to bleeding were rare [@zheng2019aspirin]. **Choosing higher-risk people raises the benefit and the bleeding together** -> [[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]].

### USPSTF reads the same trials as a lifetime model

**Adults aged 40-59 with a 10-year CVD risk of 10% or more get a Grade C: an individual decision, with a small net benefit judged at moderate certainty, and only for people not at increased bleeding risk** (no history of gut ulcers, no recent bleeding, no medicines that raise bleeding risk). **Adults 60 or older get a Grade D: moderate certainty of no net benefit, so do not start.** The relative bleeding risk does not differ by age, but the absolute incidence of bleeding rises with age, more steeply from 60 [@uspstf2022aspirin]. A C here means a small benefit known with moderate certainty, not thin evidence -> [[Net Benefit and the USPSTF Recommendation Grid]].

Zheng and USPSTF do not contradict each other, nor are they independent witnesses. **Zheng counts events over about five years in trial populations with a median age of 62; USPSTF models life-years and quality-adjusted life-years over a lifetime, by the decade a person starts**, largely from the same trials. For men at a 10% 10-year risk on lifetime aspirin, its net life-years per 1000 are +36.1 (95% CI 28.1 to 44.1) when starting at 40-49, +4.2 (-2.3 to 10.8) at 50-59, -3.0 (-8.0 to 1.9) at 60-69 and -6.9 (-10.5 to -3.4) at 70-79 [@uspstf2022aspirin]. Women at the same risk come out lower at every starting age. No outcome trial tests this lifetime frame; it is a microsimulation.

### Other harms, each on its own baseline

**Statins and new diabetes.** Low- or moderate-intensity statins raised new diabetes diagnoses by RR 1.10 (95% CI 1.04-1.16), an absolute excess of 0.12% per year (0.04-0.20), about 1.2 per 1000 person-years; high-intensity statins, RR 1.36 (1.25-1.48). The relative effect barely varied across types of participant, so the absolute excess follows baseline glycaemia: **about 62% of new cases arose in people already in the top quarter of baseline glycaemia** [@reith2024diabetes] -> [[Statins and New-Onset Diabetes]].

**Statins and muscle symptoms.** For every 1000 people whose LDL cholesterol falls by 1 mmol/L for five years, statins might cause 11 episodes of muscle pain or weakness, mostly mild, while preventing about 25 major vascular events in people without vascular disease and 50 in people with it [@reith2022muscle]. Both numbers come from the same CTT collaboration -> [[LDL Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**Blood-pressure drugs in untreated mild hypertension.** Withdrawals for adverse effects rose from 23 to 110 per 1000 over five years (RR 4.80, 95% CI 4.14-5.57; low certainty) [@wang2025mildhtn], about 87 more per 1000 (72 to 105) [inferred from @wang2025mildhtn]. The figure comes from a single 1980s trial (MRC) that also enrolled people with prior cardiovascular disease, and current-drug tolerability is not measured. A withdrawal is reversible and not on the scale of a CV event -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**Calcium with or without vitamin D.** Bolland's ledger for 1000 people treated for five years is six more heart attacks or strokes (NNH 178) against three fewer fractures (NNT 302) [@bolland2011]. **It applies to the largely community-dwelling postmenopausal women it was computed in**, not to institutionalised older people, and its cardiovascular harm rests on a disputed subgroup analysis: direction credible, size not -> [[Vitamin D and Calcium Supplementation for Fracture Prevention]].

These harms sit on different scales. **A harm's baseline often rises with the benefit's (aspirin bleeding with CV risk and age) or gathers in one stratum (statin diabetes near the glycaemic threshold)**, so assuming a fixed harm would make high-risk people look better off than the trials show.

## Expect small numbers at low risk, and treat that as the answer

**Run the same arithmetic across every proven cardiometabolic lever for a low-risk person, and none of them buys a large absolute cut in heart attacks and strokes** ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]).

### The proven levers shrink at low risk

**Semaglutide** cut major cardiovascular events in SELECT, HR 0.80 (0.72-0.90), in 17,604 people aged 45 or older with established cardiovascular disease, a BMI of 27 or more and no diabetes: 6.5% vs 8.0% over a mean 39.8 months. Adverse events led to permanent discontinuation in 16.6% on semaglutide and 8.2% on placebo, and people without previous atherosclerotic disease were not studied [@lincoff2023]. That is an absolute reduction of 1.5 percentage points over about 3.3 years (NNT about 67) in secondary prevention, against about 84 more discontinuations per 1000 ([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]).

Two conditions follow. **Without established disease, the drug's effect on events is insufficient evidence, not a null.** And if the relative effect did carry over, a 5% 10-year risk is about 0.5% a year, roughly a fifth of SELECT's placebo rate of about 2.4% a year (8.0% over 3.3 years), so the same HR 0.80 would buy roughly a fifth of the absolute benefit: a route-(a) projection, not a measurement.

**Lifestyle weight loss** did not reduce cardiovascular events in Look AHEAD, HR 0.95 (0.83-1.09), in overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes. The intensive arm lowered HbA1c further and produced greater initial improvements in fitness, while its weight advantage narrowed from 8.6% vs 0.7% at one year to 6.0% vs 3.5% at study end [@look2013].

**Blood-pressure drugs** in the low-risk tail have their own trials: five trials, 9124 people with untreated stage-1 hypertension and no cardiovascular disease, weighted mean age 50, mean follow-up 4.4 years. Total cardiovascular events gave RR 0.93 (0.69-1.24) from four of the trials (25 vs 23 per 1000), at low certainty [@wang2025mildhtn], which is insufficient evidence, not a null. Carrying BPLTTC's per-5-mmHg effect without prior cardiovascular disease, 0.91 (0.89-0.94) [@bplttc2021], over to that 25-per-1000 baseline gives about 2 fewer events per 1000 per 5 mmHg over about 4.4 years. **That figure is transported, not measured** ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]) [inferred from @wang2025mildhtn; @bplttc2021].

### Three reasons the levers land in the same place

**A constant relative effect on a low baseline is a small absolute effect.** Hard-outcome trials enrol higher-risk people, where events can be counted, so the low-risk person's benefit is nearly always an inference. And **a better risk-factor profile does not show that an event was avoided**: in Look AHEAD, weight, HbA1c and early fitness improved more in the intensive arm, and events did not differ ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).

### Two reading errors make the ceiling look lower or higher than it is

**A population ranking of attributable burden is not your expected benefit**: it multiplies effect and baseline by how common the exposure is, so a common exposure can top the list while giving one person little ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]). **A hazard label is not a risk size either**: an IARC group says how firmly a hazard is established, not how much it adds to your risk ([[Hazard Identification Is Not Risk Magnitude]]).

**The ceiling lets a low-risk person stop chasing a heart-attack reduction the trials do not promise.** The levers still earn their place for the outcomes they do move, such as weight, glycaemia and diabetes prevention, and for people at higher risk. Nor does a small or uncertain number forbid acting: how firmly to act depends on cost, reversibility and lever size, not the certainty grade alone ([[Acting on a Direction Without a Magnitude]]).

## Some baselines and strata are still unmeasured

These holes are in the evidence this guide draws on, and each one limits the arithmetic above:

- **No non-cardiovascular baseline instrument was reviewed** for diabetes, falls or fractures ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).
- **How much competing-risk adjustment changes a benefit estimate is not quantified**; PREVENT and SCORE2 build it in ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]).
- **The lifetime model has no outcome validation**; the LIFE-CVD model paper was not reviewed ([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).
- **CAC = 0's negative predictive value by age band is not covered** ([[Risk Modifiers - When Extra Information Changes a Risk Estimate]]).
- **The oldest strata stay insufficient evidence**, and frail and institutionalized people were under-represented ([[Frailty]]).
- **Aspirin's lifetime net rests on a microsimulation**, and **semaglutide has no hard-outcome trial in primary prevention** ([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]).

## Turn every relative effect into your own absolute number

These steps carry the argument above into a decision:

- **Get your 10-year risk from the instrument built for your region and population**, noting instrument and endpoint; SCORE2 reads you untreated, PREVENT takes treatment as an input ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]).
- **Multiply any relative effect by your own baseline and horizon**, and each harm by its own baseline; for blood-pressure drugs, plan on the middle age bands' effect, which may understate the benefit under 55 and overstate it at 85+. Keep benefit and harm side by side, not netted ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).
- **Use a risk modifier such as CAC or Lp(a) only near a treatment threshold** ([[Risk Modifiers - When Extra Information Changes a Risk Estimate]]).
- **If you are young with a low 10-year risk, ask for the lifetime or 30-year frame**; women under 50 and men under 40 almost always read low ([[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).
- **Over 75 without vascular disease weighing a statin, or 85+ weighing blood-pressure drugs, treat the relative effect as uncertain, not absent.**
- **On aspirin without known heart disease, expect the absolute fall in CV events and rise in major bleeding to be of similar size at every risk level**, and weigh them yourself. USPSTF leaves starting at 40-59 (10-year risk 10% or more, no raised bleeding risk) to individual decision and advises against starting at 60+ ([[Aspirin for Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease]]).
- **Treat a small absolute number at low risk as a reason to stop looking for more levers on that outcome.**

## Caveats: this guide checks its reasoning, not your outcome



- The guide grades coherence and fidelity to its sources, not what happened to anyone who followed it; the modelled NNTs and lifetime estimates are the sharpest case.
- It appraises evidence and does not prescribe: choosing, dosing and monitoring a drug need a clinician with your records.
- Every figure here applies to a person only once their own baseline, horizon and priorities are put in.
