## Pull The Two Steps Apart

**Blood pressure is a lever you reach through several exposures, but a lower reading is not the prize — it is a stand-in for one.** Two separate things must be true before "lowering blood pressure" counts as a health decision: an exposure has to move the number, and that movement has to buy fewer strokes, heart attacks and deaths. These are different claims resting on different evidence, and collapsing them is what makes a blood-pressure reading look like a health outcome in its own right. It is not one. It is a surrogate ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]), and its worth depends entirely on whether the second step holds.

**That second step is settled for only one kind of lowering: drugs.** Blood-pressure medication is directly proven to turn a lower number into fewer cardiovascular events, and to do so even in people with no established heart disease. Every lifestyle route — cutting sodium, eating a whole DASH-style pattern, losing excess weight, exercising, and, for heavier drinkers, drinking less — reliably moves the number, but borrows that second link rather than proving it. Whether a lifestyle-induced drop pays off in events is an assumption carried over from the drug trials, not evidence standing on its own.

**So rank the levers by your absolute cardiovascular risk, not by the blood-pressure number.** The proportional benefit of a given drop is much the same whoever you are, which means the same few millimetres are worth a great deal to a high-risk person and very little to a low-risk one. Blood pressure is therefore a big rock for someone with elevated pressure or high cardiovascular risk, and a minor lever for someone merely normotensive. The size of the prize is set by the risk you start from, not by how far the number falls.

**Drug therapy belongs on the same shelf as the lifestyle levers — neither above them nor beneath them.** The drug is the realistic alternative, weighed on the same three axes: how much it moves the number, how certain that is, and how likely you are to keep doing it. It is not a default first move, and not a last resort once "natural" options are exhausted. Structured exercise, compared at like-for-like starting pressures, lowers blood pressure about as much as a first-line drug — though that likeness is on the number, not on proven outcomes, and rests on thin trials with no head-to-head test.

Both facts follow from taking the two steps one at a time. Start with the first: why what moves blood pressure tells you, by itself, almost nothing about what a lower number buys.


## What Moves BP Is Not What A BP Drop Buys

**A blood-pressure reading is a marker, not a health outcome.** It is measured because it is
quick and cheap, standing in for the things people actually care about — strokes, heart attacks,
cardiovascular and all-cause death — which are slow to arrive and rare to count. The US Preventive
Services Task Force files blood pressure alongside serum cholesterol and weight as an *intermediate*
outcome, one of the measures that «are not health outcomes in and of themselves»
[@uspstfmanual2022]. **BP is a surrogate: useful, but a stand-in.**

**That splits every "this lowers your risk" claim into two separate steps, each of which has to be
evidenced on its own.** A lever changes the number (lever -> ΔBP); a changed number changes the
outcomes (ΔBP -> fewer strokes and heart attacks). **Evidence for the first step is not evidence for
the second.** A pill or a diet can be shown, cleanly, to drop systolic pressure by some millimetres —
and that trial says nothing, by itself, about whether the drop buys a single averted stroke.


**A BP target earns legitimacy only when the second link is shown, not assumed.** Correlation is not
enough: GRADE's own worked case found a marker significantly associated with the outcome yet unable to
forecast it — «predicting overall survival using progression-free survival remained uncertain»
[@grade]. USPSTF states the rule plainly — a proven effect on an
intermediate outcome «does not necessarily establish that it can improve outcomes that are perceptible
to patients» [@uspstfmanual2022]. Markers have moved the right way
while patients got worse, so a lever that shifts BP is a candidate for benefit, never a verdict on it.

The second step — does a lower number actually mean fewer strokes — is settled. But, as the next
section shows, only for one kind of lowering.


## Does Lowering Blood Pressure Actually Prevent Events?

For drug-lowered blood pressure the answer is a firm yes, and it is the only directly-evidenced
transfer link here. Pooling «Data for 344 716 participants from 48 randomised clinical
trials», the Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration found that **a 5 mmHg fall
in systolic pressure cut major cardiovascular events by about 10%** — «irrespective of previous
diagnoses of cardiovascular disease, and even at normal or high–normal blood pressure values». The
hazard ratio was **0.91 (0.89-0.94) in people with no prior heart disease** and **0.89 (0.86-0.92)
in those who already had it**; across the baseline-pressure strata there was «no reliable evidence
of heterogeneity», and «in no subgroup was the HR above 1·0». So the benefit needs neither
established disease nor a high starting pressure.
[@bplttc2021]

One landmark trial shows what an aggressive target buys, and what it costs. SPRINT randomised 9,361
high-risk hypertensives to a systolic target of **«less than 120 mm Hg (intensive treatment) or a
target of less than 140 mm Hg (standard treatment)»** and stopped early: the intensive arm cut the
primary composite (heart attack, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, heart failure or cardiovascular
death) with a **«hazard ratio ... 0.75; 95% ... CI ... 0.64 to 0.89»**, and lowered all-cause
mortality to **«0.73; 95% CI, 0.60 to 0.90»** — a **number needed to treat of 61** over 3.26 years.
But intensive control also raised serious adverse events — hypotension, syncope, electrolyte
abnormalities, and acute kidney injury or failure, though **not** injurious falls
[@sprint2015]. A lower target is a
net-effect judgement, not a rule that lower is always better.

The most durable objection to lowering pressure far turns out to be a mirage. Observational
registries show a «J-shaped association between cardiovascular events and blood pressure, with the
lowest risk of events occurring at ... around 130/75 mm Hg», long read as a warning that going
lower could do harm. [@bplttc2021] The randomised evidence dissolves it: benefit continues below 120 mmHg with no
rising hazard in any low-pressure stratum, so the protective-looking lower arm is a study-design
artifact, not a treatment effect. This is a worked case for [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]] —
before believing any protective lower arm, run the diagnostics (reverse causation, frailty, the
sick-quitter who stopped because they were already ill), because a curve equally consistent with
benefit and with artifact carries no decision.
[inferred from @bplttc2021]

One caveat governs everything downstream: **this transfer is proven for pharmacological lowering.**
Blood pressure is a surrogate ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]), and a surrogate earns target status only
where its transmission to a patient-important outcome is itself evidenced — which BPLTTC supplies
for drugs and nothing else here supplies directly. Every lifestyle lever borrows that link as an
assumption. The closest the corpus comes to a direct test is SSaSS, where a potassium-enriched salt
substitute cut **stroke (rate ratio 0.86, 0.77-0.96), major cardiovascular events (0.87, 0.80-0.94)
and death (0.88, 0.82-0.95)** in 20,995 high-risk adults, on an achieved fall of just **−3.34 mmHg**.
[@neal2021] So a diet-route drop can
reach hard endpoints — but it is a joint sodium-and-potassium lever, and its event benefit outruns
what −3.34 mmHg predicts through the drug channel, so the magnitude cannot be read straight off the
pressure change.
[inferred from @neal2021]

The two newly held lifestyle levers sharpen this gap rather than close it. Structured exercise
(Naci) and alcohol reduction (Roerecke) both report a fall in systolic pressure — the surrogate —
with **no cardiovascular events measured at all**. Roerecke goes one modelled step further: its
projected «more than 7000 inpatient hospitalisations and 678 cardiovascular deaths prevented every
year» for the UK rest on «standard methods for comparative risk assessment analyses ... assuming
persistence of lower alcohol consumption within 1 year, and no lag time of eﬀ ects on CVD outcomes».
[@roerecke2017alcohol] Modelled events
are not measured events. Both levers add credible pressure-lowering; neither turns the surrogate
into a demonstrated outcome. [inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol; @naci2019exercise]

Because the relative effect is the same at any starting pressure, a person's absolute cardiovascular
risk decides whether a lever is worth pulling — and, before that, which levers actually
move the number.


## The Levers, One At A Time

Several exposures move the blood-pressure number, but they do not move it in the same currency of
certainty — and, read carelessly, some are the same move counted twice. Here is each on its own terms:
the size of the BP effect, how well that size is known, and — separately — whether the drop is shown to
reach hard events or only assumed to. Read each as a **substitution** (what replaces what), and keep the
two questions apart: a millimetre of mercury is not a prevented stroke until the transfer is evidenced ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).

### Reach For A Drug As The Realistic Alternative, Not The Last Resort

A blood-pressure drug is the one lever whose BP drop is **directly proven to buy fewer events**: in the
largest randomised evidence base, «a 5 mm Hg reduction of systolic blood pressure reduced the risk of
major cardiovascular events by about 10%, irrespective of previous diagnoses of cardiovascular disease,
and even at normal or high-normal blood pressure values»
[@bplttc2021]. Put the
drug on the **same axes** as the lifestyle levers — effect, certainty, achievability — not as a premise
or a last resort. It is the benchmark every lifestyle ΔBP below is implicitly measured against, and the
only one whose event transfer is not borrowed.

*How low to go is a net-effect judgment, not lower-is-always-better:* an intensive drug target buys
further events but carries a real adverse-event cost, so the target is matched to the person's risk
rather than pushed as far as it will go — developed with the SPRINT evidence under **Match How Low You
Go To The Person, Not The Reading** below.

### Cut Sodium — A High-Certainty BP Move You Can Actually Audit

Reducing salt lowers blood pressure with **high certainty**: a modest cut for four or more weeks causes
«important falls in blood pressure in both hypertensive and normotensive individuals»
[@he2013] — pooled **-4.18 mmHg SBP** (-5.18 to
-3.18), and **larger in hypertensives (-5.39, -6.62 to -4.15)** than normotensives (-2.42, -3.56 to
-1.29) [@he2013]; WHO's pooled figure is -3.39 mmHg
[@who2012]. Sodium is also **the rare dietary target a
person can be measured against**: urinary sodium is a recovery biomarker yielding absolute intake, so a
cutpoint is auditable in a way an FFQ-based exposure never is.

*The hard-outcome step is borrowed, then partly redeemed.* The BP drop's transfer to events rides the
same drug-based per-5-mmHg channel above (BPLTTC) — an assumption for a diet-route lowering — plus the
one dietary trial that reached measured endpoints: SSaSS, a potassium-enriched salt substitute, cut
stroke («rate ratio, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.77 to 0.96») on an achieved «-3.34 (-4.51 to -2.18)» mmHg SBP
[@neal2021]. But that trial moves sodium
*and* potassium together, so it evidences the **joint lever**, not sodium alone.

**Potassium (one line, not developed here):** raising potassium lowers BP in hypertensives, does
essentially nothing in normotensives, and is contraindicated in CKD or impaired potassium excretion —
see [[Sodium]] and [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]] for the physiology and the salt-substitute
derivation.

### Eat A Whole DASH Pattern, Not Its Sodium Line

The DASH pattern lowers blood pressure by **-5.2 mmHg SBP** (-7.0 to -3.4) and -2.6 mmHg DBP versus a
control diet, across 20 RCTs [@siervo2015].
The fall is **larger where BP and BMI start higher**: «Reductions in systolic and diastolic BP following
randomisation to the DASH diet were greater in participants with higher BP or BMI at baseline»
[@siervo2015] — so the demonstrated benefit
sits in an elevated-risk population, not a lean, optimal-BP one.

Two honest bounds. First, **every DASH endpoint here is a surrogate** — BP, lipids, glucose over 2-24
weeks; no trial measured an event, and the often-quoted CVD-risk drop is a modelled projection, so the
DASH→events step rides the same borrowed transfer as the rest. Second, DASH's effect is the **whole
pattern (potassium, calcium, magnesium, fibre, low saturated fat), not its sodium content**: the
between-arm sodium difference did not predict the BP change. So do **not** credit DASH to salt, and do
not add its -5.2 mmHg to the sodium lever's -4.18 as if independent — they overlap (flagged again below).

### Lose Weight For Its Proven Benefits, Not A CV-Event Promise

Weight loss is a **real BP lever** and it genuinely lowers pressure — but rank it on the outcomes it
demonstrably moves — glycaemia, type-2-diabetes prevention and remission (in DiRECT, «remission was
achieved in 68 (46%) participants in the intervention group and six (4%) participants in the control
group» [@lean2018]), MASLD, function — **not** on a
cardiovascular-event reduction, because via the lifestyle route that reduction did not materialise. The
largest, longest trial, Look AHEAD, was null: the CV composite showed «hazard ratio 0.95; 95% CI 0.83
to 1.09» and intensive lifestyle intervention «did not reduce cardiovascular events in overweight or
obese adults with type 2 diabetes»
[@look2013]. The 54-RCT meta-analysis
confirms the CV null (RR 0.93, 0.83 to 1.04) while finding an all-cause-mortality benefit routed *not*
through the heart: «weight loss interventions decrease all cause mortality ... risk ratio 0.82 ... with
six fewer deaths per 1000 participants» [@ma2017].

*A thin spot, named not invented:* the corpus holds no reliable per-kilogram ΔBP figure — the
weight-loss sources centre events and mortality, not millimetres of mercury per kilogram, so the BP move
is carried qualitatively here rather than as a dose coefficient.

### Train — Structured Exercise Matches A Drug On The Number, In Hypertensives

Structured exercise (endurance or dynamic-resistance training) is a **credible-magnitude BP lever** in
people who are already hypertensive. Once you compare like-for-like starting pressures, «exercise reduced
SBP by 8.96 mmHg (95% CrI -10.27 to -7.64) among hypertensive populations (>=140 mmHg)» and «We did not
observe a difference between the SBP-lowering effects of medications and exercise (0.18, 95% CrI -1.35
to 1.68)» [@naci2019exercise]. The naive "drugs beat
exercise" gap is an artefact of unmatched baselines: exercise trials started near 132 mmHg, drug trials
above 150, and matching the baseline erases the gap — a worked case of
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]] on a continuous outcome.

**Carry both hedges every time.** First, this is a **surrogate reached indirectly**: Naci measured SBP,
not CV events, and «No RCTs compared directly exercise against medications»
[@naci2019exercise] — the equivalence is inferred
across a network of disjoint trials, not observed head-to-head. Second, the exercise evidence is
**sparse and at high risk of bias** (56 matched-stratum trials, mostly unblinded — structurally
unblindable). So "exercise ≈ drugs **on the BP number**" is not "exercise ≈ drugs **on outcomes**." It
is a defensible adjunct, and an alternative where a drug is declined — for a hypertensive person, not a
normotensive one, where the effect is smaller and the drug comparison was never the point.

### Cut Alcohol — But Only Above About Two Drinks A Day

For a drinker above the threshold, cutting down lowers blood pressure — overall «-3.13 mm Hg (95% CI
-3.93 to -2.32) for systolic blood pressure» across 36 reduction RCTs, dose-dependent at «-0.91 mm Hg»
SBP per drink/day of baseline intake [@roerecke2017alcohol]. The lever carries **a threshold at roughly two drinks/day, and empties
below it**: «People drinking two drinks or fewer per day did not have a significant reduction in blood
pressure when they reduced their alcohol consumption to near abstinence»
[@roerecke2017alcohol]. Read this as a threshold — below
it there is little BP to gain — **not as a permitted intake**; and note the BP endpoint carries **no
protective arm**, so cutting down never *raises* pressure — an interventional refinement of the genetic
(Mendelian-randomization) evidence that «genotype- predicted alcohol intake in all men had similarly
strong positive associations with systolic blood pressure (each p<0·0001)»
[@millwood2019].

*The event payoff is modelled, not measured.* Roerecke observed BP only; its projected «7000 inpatient
hospitalisations and 678 cardiovascular deaths prevented» rest on risk-assessment modelling «assuming
persistence ... and no lag time», not on any hard endpoint the trials counted
[@roerecke2017alcohol]. So this is exactly the
borrowed-transfer case: a real, high-certainty BP effect whose CV benefit is assumed through a model.

### Add MBSR As An Adjunct — The Biggest Number, The Weakest Warrant

A mindfulness-based stress-reduction program posts the **largest BP point estimate of any lever here and
the least trustworthy one**. Pooled, «statistically significant effects of MBIs for reducing SBP and DBP
(MD = -9.12; 95% CI [-12.18, -6.05], p < 0.001, I2 = 92%)»
[@chen2024mbi] — larger than
a salt cut, rivalling a drug. But four features gut it, all pushing the estimate up: the trials are
unblinded (and mostly unblindable), extremely heterogeneous, of low quality, and — decisively — the
effect **vanishes in unmedicated patients**: «studies that included unmedicated patients showed no
lowering effect of MBIs on SBP/DBP (SBP: MD = 0.53; 95% CI [-1.89, 2.95], p = 0.67)»
[@chen2024mbi], which hints
the effect may act through drug adherence rather than a direct physiological pathway.

BP is a surrogate here with transmission unshown for this exposure. Keep it **proportional** — a
low-certainty adjunct for an already-medicated person, never a substitute for a drug or salt reduction;
mindfulness's marketing is an anti-signal, not a reason to promote it.

### Dietary Nitrate (Beetroot) — A Real But Minor Lever

Beetroot juice and inorganic nitrate salts lower office systolic pressure by about 4.4 mmHg (16
crossover RCTs, n=254; 95% CI -5.9 to -2.8), and the drop scales with nitrate dose -- a genuine
effect through the enterosalivary nitrate-nitrite-nitric-oxide pathway.
[@siervo2013nitrate] Diastolic
pressure does not move (-1.1 mmHg, not significant).

Three things keep it a pebble, not a rock. The outcome is a **surrogate measured over hours to 15
days** -- no trial ran long enough to test whether the drop persists or buys fewer events.
**Twenty-four-hour ambulatory monitoring did not confirm
the resting benefit** -- including in older, overweight, diabetic adults, the higher-risk stratum
where a lever would matter most. And the trials recruited mostly young, healthy, normal-weight men
-- often for exercise performance -- so the estimate transfers poorly to the hypertensive adult who
would actually use it. The authors count their 4.4 mmHg as reaching the \~5 mmHg their stroke-and-
mortality projection needs (leaning on the interval to -5.9); the point estimate is nonetheless
below that bar. A candidate worth watching, not a recommendation. -> [[Dietary Nitrate and Blood Pressure]]

Read as a shopping list, this section **over-counts**: several of these levers are the same intervention
seen twice — the DASH pattern already contains the sodium and potassium moves, weight loss carries much
of the diet effect, and the salt substitute bundles sodium with potassium — so their BP effects cannot
simply be summed. The next section shows why, and how to rank what remains once the double-counting is
removed.


## The Levers Overlap — Do Not Add Their BP Effects Up

**Read the lever list as a shopping cart and you will double-count.** Several of these
interventions are the same physiology seen twice, so **their millimetres of mercury do not
add.** The DASH pattern already carries most of the moves listed separately beside it: it
raises «protective nutrients such as K, Ca, Mg, fibre and vegetable proteins and, at the
same time, a lower intake of refined carbohydrates and saturated fat»
[@siervo2015] — so the
potassium lever, the fibre effect and much of the sodium story are inside it, not on top of
it. Losing weight, if it comes from eating that way, embeds much of the diet effect again.
And the potassium-enriched salt substitute moves sodium down and potassium up in one act.
**Summing the levers counts shared channels more than once.**

**Keep two different questions apart: what caused a lever's effect, and whether two levers
combine.** The first is attribution, the second is stacking. Confuse them, and a
BP-lowering to-do list inflates.

**Attribution: DASH's own effect is not its sodium content.** DASH lowers systolic BP by
**about 5.2 mmHg** against a control diet (95% CI -7.0 to -3.4)
[@siervo2015], and cutting
salt alone lowers it by **roughly 4 mmHg** (He: -4.18 mmHg, -5.18 to -3.18)
[@he2013]. It is tempting to credit
DASH's fall to the salt it happens to remove — but Siervo's meta-regression finds
otherwise: «Differences in dietary Na intake between the DASH and control intervention
groups were not associated with changes in systolic and diastolic BP»
[@siervo2015]. **DASH's
vs-control effect is the whole pattern, not its incidental sodium contrast** — so you can
neither attribute the 5.2 mmHg to salt nor add it to the 4 mmHg as if the two were
independent channels.

**Stacking is the separate, real case: deliberately adding salt restriction on top of DASH
buys more.** «Feeding trials have demonstrated the additive effects of salt restriction on
the efficacy of the DASH dietary pattern in reducing BP»
[@siervo2015] — the
DASH-Sodium factorial. **The distinction that governs the ranking:** DASH's own effect is
not driven by sodium (no attribution), yet a purposeful salt cut *layered onto* DASH still
lowers BP further (genuine stacking). Overlap forbids summing the levers as a menu;
deliberate combination is a design choice that can add.

With the double-counting removed, you can rank the levers for a given person.


## Rank By Absolute Risk, Not The BP Number

**Rank the levers by a person's absolute cardiovascular risk, not by their blood-pressure
reading.** The relative payoff of lowering blood pressure is roughly the same at every starting
level: in the pooled trial evidence, «a 5 mm Hg reduction of systolic blood pressure reduced the
risk of a major cardiovascular event by about 10%» with no reliable heterogeneity across baseline
blood-pressure categories down below 120 mmHg
[@bplttc2021]. A
constant relative effect makes the *absolute* gain a product of that effect and the person's
baseline risk — so the same millimetres are worth a lot to a high-risk person and little to a
low-risk one, with no disagreement about the evidence.

**This is route (a): absolute benefit = relative reduction x baseline risk, so a stratum needs a
prognostic number, not a subgroup claim** -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].
SCORE2 supplies that number, converting age, sex, region, smoking, systolic pressure and non-HDL
cholesterol into a 10-year absolute CVD risk, and its treat-thresholds are age-banded — at ages
50-69, risk-factor treatment is generally recommended once 10-year risk reaches \~10%
[@esc2021] (the full ladder, and why it moves,
sits in the guidance section). Read the BP number to decide *how much* a lever moves; read the
absolute risk to decide *whether the lever is worth pulling*.

**For an already-low-risk, normotensive person the honest finding is that the remaining levers are
small — and that is a result, not a failure.** The ceiling is itself a finding: once baseline risk
is low, a proven relative effect on a low absolute base is a small absolute gain, which licenses
someone to stop optimising rather than hunt for a lever the evidence does not promise
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]],
[[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]].

The levers, sorted for the two ends of that risk gradient — the tier is the ranking, the BP effect
and its transfer status are the inputs (magnitudes in the lever section above):

| Lever | ΔBP certainty | Transfer to events | Tier — high CV risk | Tier — low CV risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drug therapy (realistic alternative) | high | **proven** (BPLTTC / SPRINT) | **big rock** | small absolute — decide on risk |
| Sodium reduction | high | assumed (BPLTTC) + SSaSS joint-lever | big rock (dietary: medium) | medium |
| DASH pattern | medium (surrogate, short-term) | assumed | medium-big | medium |
| Weight loss (if overweight) | moves BP; CV-event transfer null via lifestyle | assumed | big rock on its *other* proven benefits | medium |
| Exercise (structured) | held (Naci); surrogate, sparse / high-RoB, indirect | assumed; no direct RCT | medium (≈ drugs when baseline-matched) | small / uncertain |
| Alcohol reduction (in drinkers) | held (Roerecke); dose-response, threshold \~2 drinks/day | assumed; events **modelled**, not measured | medium | medium (drinkers above threshold) |

**One caution about the table: when you *compare* two interventions on a continuous outcome like
systolic pressure, match their baseline pressures before you read the difference.** Absolute BP fall
grows with starting pressure, so a naive head-to-head compares populations, not treatments. Naci's
network meta-analysis is the worked case: exercise trials began at a «mean SBP at baseline» of «132
mmHg» while drug trials sat «consistently over 150 mmHg»
[@naci2019exercise], and the unmatched contrast
made drugs look 3.96 mmHg better. Restricting exercise to hypertensive (>=140 mmHg) trials lifted
its effect to «8.96 mmHg» and erased the gap («0.18, 95% CrI −1.35 to 1.68»)
[@naci2019exercise] — a like-for-like tie on the
number, though still only on the surrogate.

The ranking shifts with the person — most of all between a high-cardiovascular-risk person and a
merely-normotensive one — which is where the question turns next.


## Match How Low You Go To The Person, Not The Reading

**The continuous baseline-SBP gradient is the effect modifier that matters, not the
hypertensive-versus-normotensive label.** The pressure fall a lever delivers grows with starting
pressure: cutting salt lowers systolic by −5.39 mmHg (−6.62 to −4.15) in hypertensives against
−2.42 mmHg (−3.56 to −1.29) in normotensives
[@he2013]. Huang argues the dichotomy is the
wrong cut, «weak because the definition of hypertension is arbitrary and there is a rationale for
expecting a graded interaction between sodium reduction, blood pressure reduction, and starting
blood pressure», with the gradient present even below 120 mmHg
[@huang2020]. This is **route-(b) effect
modification on the size of the mmHg fall** — and route-(b) is the false-positive generator. A
between-study meta-regression is regression-to-mean-prone, so **read it as directional and use it
conservatively.**

**Keep two things baseline pressure does apart.** It modifies the *mmHg fall* (above) — but the
*event benefit per mmHg lowered* barely moves across starting pressures. Pharmacological trials show
«no reliable evidence of heterogeneity of treatment effects on major cardiovascular events by
baseline cardiovascular disease status or systolic blood pressure categories»
[@bplttc2021]. So a
larger fall in a higher-pressure person, times a roughly constant benefit-per-mmHg, compounds with
that person's higher **absolute** cardiovascular risk (route-(a), section 6) — the two reasons to
treat them harder are separate and both real.

**Potassium is the one genuine asymmetry.** Raising potassium lowers pressure in people who already
have high blood pressure but does essentially nothing in normotensives — its normotensive effect is
a flat null, not merely a smaller one, whereas sodium reduction still moves the number in
normotensives. This is a route-(b)
distinction that changes who a potassium lever is worth pulling for: for a normotensive person, it is
not.

**How low to go: the intensive target buys events but carries a cost.** SPRINT randomised high-risk,
non-diabetic adults to a systolic target below 120 mmHg versus below 140, reaching mean 121.4 versus
136.2 mmHg [@sprint2015]. The intensive
arm cut the primary composite (hazard ratio 0.75, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.89, P<0.001) and all-cause death
(hazard ratio 0.73, 0.60 to 0.90, P=0.003) [@sprint2015]. **But the lower target has a harm the standard one does not:** overall serious
adverse events were no higher (38.3% vs 37.1%, hazard ratio 1.04, P=0.25), yet «Rates of serious
adverse events of hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities, and acute kidney injury or
failure, but not of injurious falls, were higher in the intensive-treatment group than in the
standard-treatment group» [@sprint2015].
The verdict is a **net-effect judgment**, not a lower-is-always-better rule.

**Before reading a diastolic floor as a reason to stop, run the artifact diagnostics.** Observational
data show a J-shape — risk appearing to rise again at low diastolic pressure — and it is tempting to
treat that lower arm as a causal harm of going too low. It is **artifact-suspect**: the low-pressure
group is enriched for reverse causation and frailty, exactly the recipe on
[[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]. The interventional record does not reproduce it — SPRINT
achieved a mean systolic of 121 mmHg with net benefit, and the per-mmHg event benefit shows no
threshold across pressure categories (above). So **present the refutation, not the J-curve, as the
finding**; the full interventional rebuttal sits on the transfer step (section 3).

**The salt substitute has a contraindication stratum — this is route-(c).** SSaSS lowered stroke and
death with a potassium-enriched substitute, and found no excess hyperkalaemia (3.35 vs 3.30 per 1000
person-years; RR 1.04, 0.80 to 1.37; P=0.76). But that reassurance is scoped to a **screened**
population: «contraindications included use of a potassium-sparing diuretic, use of a potassium
supplement, or known serious kidney disease. Routine biochemical measurement of kidney function was
not performed» [@neal2021]. **For
someone with chronic kidney disease or otherwise impaired potassium excretion, a potassium-loaded
substitute is a harm-firing stratum, not a free lever** — the safety data do not cover them
-> [[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]], [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]].

**Who counts as high-risk moves with age, and the guidance encodes it in the threshold.** ESC's
very-high-risk cut for 10-year cardiovascular risk climbs with age — >=7.5% under 50, >=10% at 50-69,
>=15% at 70 and over — because age is the dominant risk driver, so a fixed cutpoint would over-treat
the old and under-treat the young [@esc2021]. At 70
and over, ESC also softens the *lipid-lowering* drug recommendation to Class IIb ("may be
considered") even in the very-high band
[@esc2021] -> [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]. Guidance codes much of this stratification into thresholds that move — worth
knowing why they move.


## Read A Moving Threshold As Evolution, Not Error

**The number that flips a person from "watch" to "treat" is not fixed — it moves with age, and it has
moved between guideline editions. Both movements are legitimate, and neither means a body got it
wrong.** ESC sets its treatment thresholds on 10-year cardiovascular risk, and the cut-offs are
**age-banded**: risk-factor treatment is *generally recommended* above \~7.5% under age 50, \~10% at
50-69, and \~15% at 70+ [@esc2021]. So the *same* 8%
risk is very-high in a 45-year-old and merely high in a 72-year-old. The banding is deliberate, not
sloppy: because **«Age is the major driver of CVD risk»** [@esc2021], a single fixed threshold would over-treat the old and under-treat the young.

**The threshold also shifted across editions — and ESC said so about itself.** Revising its 2016
guidance, it recorded that **«Evidence now suggests that the BP targets in the previous iteration of
this guideline were too conservative, especially for older patients»** [@esc2021], then tightened its targets, citing evidence that the lower
targets «are safely achieved in many older patients and are associated with significant reductions in
the risk of major stroke, HF, and CV death». **This is a body disclosing its own error and correcting
on new evidence — lag (divergence class 4), the highest-standing form of that claim because no critic
is needed to allege it.** Reading self-correction as a defect would penalise exactly the behaviour a
sound process produces.

**When two guidance families set different thresholds, name why before crying defect.** ESC stratifies
with SCORE2/SCORE2-OP; the US ACC-AHA guideline uses PREVENT-ASCVD — a different calculator,
endpoint, calibration and age range [@accaha2026]. Their thresholds estimate **different
composites**, so they cannot be lined up as looser-versus-stricter; the divergence is standpoint and
instrument (classes 1-2), not disagreement on any effect. That is the discipline: of the
five reasons guidance can diverge — standpoint, evidence base, genuine disagreement, lag, process
defect — **only the last is a fault, and it is the one to reach for last**, with a documented referent,
never as a reflex against an inconvenient number.

None of this closes the ranking. **Two things the fabric still cannot rank close the picture honestly.**


## What Stays Unsettled — And How To Decide Anyway

**The blood-pressure story is largely settled, but five questions the evidence here cannot yet
rank.** Each is carried as an open question, with no direction assumed — a gap is not a finding.

**The largest is whether exercise that lowers blood pressure actually prevents heart attacks and
strokes, and how it fares head-to-head against a drug.** The size of structured exercise's effect on
the blood-pressure *number* is now in hand — but the network meta-analysis that supplies it reached
that comparison sideways: «we relied on indirect evidence to determine the comparative SBP-lowering
effects of exercise and medications»
[@naci2019exercise], because «In the absence of
head-to-head RCTs that directly compare exercise and drug interventions»
[@naci2019exercise] none had been run.

Those exercise trials were also sparse, often measured blood pressure only as a secondary outcome,
and carried higher risk of bias than the drug trials. So two gaps stay open: **no trial has measured
whether an exercise-induced BP drop cuts hard cardiovascular events, and no direct
exercise-versus-drug trial exists.** The magnitude gap is closed; the outcomes gap is not.

**Cutting alcohol lowers blood pressure in heavier drinkers — but its effect on actual events is
modelled, not measured.** Roerecke projects that in the UK the BP falls «would translate into more
than 7000 inpatient hospitalisations and 678 cardiovascular deaths prevented every year»
[@roerecke2017alcohol] — a translation *from* the
blood-pressure change, not an observed event count. Whether that modelled benefit is realised is
untested.

Three thinner questions round it out:

- **Dietary nitrate (beetroot).** Now held: it lowers office systolic pressure \~4.4 mmHg, but on a
  surrogate over hours-to-days that 24-h monitoring did not confirm, in mostly young healthy men --
  a real but minor, poorly-transferable lever, not a recommendation for the hypertensive adult.
- **The sodium-to-potassium ratio as an optimised target.** It is asserted beneficial, but has never
  been graded against a hard outcome; the underlying physiology sits with the Sodium work, not here.
- **Whether the large mindfulness number transmits to any outcome.** Chen reports the biggest
  blood-pressure fall of any lever here — «reductions in systolic blood pressure (MD = − 9.12, 95% CI
  [− 12.18, − 6.05], p < 0.001)»
  [@chen2024mbi] — but on
  the weakest warrant (unblinded, heterogeneous, a surrogate), and nothing links it to strokes, heart
  attacks or death.

**None of this is closed by the wider activity-and-fitness evidence, which is strong but answers a
different question.** Physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness both track lower all-cause
mortality -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]], [[Measuring and Raising Cardiorespiratory Fitness]]
— but that evidence is observational (bar a single heart-failure trial), and it measures total,
often incidental movement and the fitness it produces, on an all-cause-death endpoint. That is a
different exposure and a different outcome from Naci's structured, aerobic exercise prescribed to
lower blood pressure — keep the two distinct, and note that neither supplies the missing
exercise-versus-drug outcome trial.
[inferred from @naci2019exercise]

**The bottom line, in two steps.** First, several exposures genuinely move blood pressure — cutting
sodium, a whole DASH-style pattern, losing excess weight, structured exercise, and, for heavier
drinkers, less alcohol. Second, a lower number only reliably *buys* fewer strokes and heart attacks
when a drug produces it; every lifestyle route borrows that link on the assumption it holds. So do
not chase the reading itself. **Rank the levers by your absolute cardiovascular risk, not by your
blood-pressure number:** the same few-mmHg fall is worth a great deal to a high-risk person and
little to a low-risk one. And the drug belongs on the same shelf as the lifestyle moves — the
realistic alternative, weighed on effect, certainty and stickability, neither a first nor a last
resort by default.
