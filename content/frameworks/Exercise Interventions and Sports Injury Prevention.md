## Stretching: no benefit shown, not a refuted belief (symmetric standards)

Stretching (before *or* after exercise) is widely prescribed to prevent injury; this MA finds **no
benefit shown**: the stretching subgroup returns RR 0.963 (0.846-1.095), I2=0%, all-ITT (read here until
2026-11-04 as a tight null; superseded, see *Re-judged under one rule*). The
authors state plainly that their data do not support stretching for injury prevention, whether done
before or after exercise; that discussion sentence then pivots to a caveat about the stretching analysis
itself: «this analysis only included two studies on army recruits and one internet-based study on the
general popu- lation.» The caveat narrows the base; it does not withdraw the no-benefit result, which includes a
general-adult RCT. (Corrected 2026-10-22: this paragraph earlier read the caveat as being about the whole
meta-analysis (*mostly athletes*); the sentence is about the three stretching trials.)
[@lauersen2013injury]
(Superseded 2026-11-04.) The page earlier read this as a *no meaningful effect* verdict, not
*insufficient evidence*: the estimate is tight (RR 0.963, CI 0.846-1.095), homogeneous (I2=0%),
ITT-analysed, and «corresponds to earlier reviews».
[@lauersen2013injury]
Under *Re-judged under one rule* (below) the state is *insufficient evidence* in both strata, recruits
and general adults. That is a change of evidence state, not a narrowing: *no meaningful effect* would
close the cell, *insufficient evidence* leaves it open, and it cannot refute the belief that stretching
prevents injury, only show that the trials held do not support it.

## Strength vs the multi-component programmes: a between-subgroup comparison and the dilution hypothesis

Strength training «proved significantly better than multiple exposure studies, even though all
multiple exposure studies included a strength training component.»
[@lauersen2013injury]
Counter-intuitive — bundling more exposures scored *worse* than the single best one. The stated
mechanism is **dilution**: «each component may be reduced quantitatively and/or qualitatively» when
bundled, so «multiple exposure programmes may therefore reduce the proportion of proven beneficial
exposures and consequently reduce the overall preventive effect», and the added burden can erode
compliance. [@lauersen2013injury]
Decision consequence the authors draw: build prevention programmes «on the basis of well-proven
single exposures». [@lauersen2013injury]
(Restated 2026-11-04; the page earlier glossed this as *do the thing that works (strength) at full dose
rather than a diluted mix* and ranked *a concentrated proven lever > a broad thin one*.) The
comparison is between subgroups: Lauersen's four strength trials (elite male soccer, recruits, female
adolescent soccer) against a different set of multi-component trials, so exposure type is confounded
with whatever else differs between the two sets (population, sport, outcome definition). No held trial
tests strength alone head-to-head against a programme. The page holds dilution as a hypothesis, as it
holds Wu's supervised split and Ding's compliance split, and does not advise choosing strength over a
programme on it.

## Acute vs overuse — both reduced, overuse slightly more

Stratified by *injury type* (all exposures pooled): acute injuries RR 0.647 (0.502-0.836; ITT 0.615)
and overuse injuries RR 0.527 (0.373-0.746, I2=19%) — «overuse injuries could be almost halved».
[@lauersen2013injury]
Caveat on attribution: five of six overuse analyses and six of nine acute analyses were
*multiple-exposure* studies, so this split **cannot say which component** did the preventing — a
G-gap the authors name («not possible to derive which parts of these interventions manifested the
preventive effect»). Read the type-stratified table above for "which exercise", this split for
"which injury". [@lauersen2013injury]

## Robustness — the ITT direction is the reassuring one

The compliance worry runs: real-world adherence is worse than trial adherence, so effects should
shrink. The opposite showed: «Contrary to the expected more conservative effect esti-mate, the
intention-to-treat sensitivity analyses revealed even more beneficial effect estimates.»
[@lauersen2013injury]
So the estimates are not compliance-inflated — if anything the better-conducted (ITT) trials showed
larger benefit. A small-study (publication-bias) effect was significant for the *total* estimate and
the *multiple-exposure* subgroup only, and was attributed largely to heterogeneity; strength and
stretching subgroups showed no significant small-study effect — the two cleanest results are also the
least bias-suspect. [@lauersen2013injury]
(2026-11-04) Those two subgroups hold four and three trials, where a small-study test has almost no
power; read *least bias-suspect* as *not shown to be biased* (*Re-judged under one rule*).

## Synthesis — what this adds to the resistance-training picture

[inferred from @lauersen2013injury] — the
[[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]] page is honest that its endpoints
(1RM strength, muscle size) are **surrogates** with no RCT closing the health-outcome link. Lauersen
supplies one **patient-important outcome that resistance-type training moves at causal (RCT-MA)
grade** — injury reduction — which no observational strength/mortality association can claim. The
composite: strength training's case is strongest for injury (interventional, RR \~0.3) in the elite,
recruit and youth trials sampled, moderate for
mortality (observational, via [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]), and weakest for
hypertrophy-as-health (surrogate for a surrogate). **But the exposure is not identical** — Lauersen's
strength arms are eccentric/sport-specific injury-prevention protocols in athletes, not the
hypertrophy-oriented general RT of the prescription page; reading one as the other is the
transportability gap above.
(Restated 2026-11-04; the composite earlier ranked injury first without a population.) One step of the
transfer that caveat names has since been tested, for programmes pooled: adult recreational athletes
(Liddle) and endurance runners (Wu), both *insufficient evidence*, with no strength-only pool in either.
The step to gym-type strength training in non-athletes remains untested (*Transportability boundary*).
So for a general adult weighing resistance training, the held RCTs do not show an injury benefit, and
the *strongest for injury* rank is not shown to transfer to that person.

## The tissue mechanism beneath the strength result

[inferred from @lauersen2013injury] — Lauersen
gives the *outcome* (strength training cuts injury, overuse injury almost halved) but not the tissue
mechanism. [[Tendon Adaptation to Mechanical Loading]] (Bohm 2015 SR-MA) supplies a candidate: high
magnitude loading stiffens and strengthens tendon (stiffness SMD 0.70; high-intensity 0.90 vs
low-intensity 0.04), a plausible route by which strength-type loading builds overuse-injury tolerance.
Two caveats keep this a mechanism, not a second line of evidence: Bohm measures tendon *surrogates*
(stiffness, modulus, CSA), **never injury**; and Bohm also warns that **tendon adapts slower than
muscle**, so loading ramped faster than the tendon can condition is itself an overload pathway — the
rate-mismatch that argues for gradual progression, especially in the deconditioned. So strength
training's injury benefit and its tendon-conditioning mechanism are consistent, but the outcome link
stays Lauersen's and the mechanism stays Bohm's.

## The guideline position: warm-up, cool-down, stretching, progression (ACSM 2011)

The 2011 ACSM position stand on exercise quantity and quality is the held guideline for general adults
(not athletes). It grades with the NHLBI categories: A = RCTs, rich data; B = RCTs, limited data;
C = nonrandomized or observational; D = panel consensus.
[@garber2011acsm]

**Exercise does raise injury risk, and the stand grades it.** Exercise «is associated with an increased
risk of musculoskeletal injury» and of adverse CHD events (B); the benefits outweigh the risks in most
adults (C). «Musculoskeletal injury is the most common ex- ercise-related complication».
[@garber2011acsm]

**The common precautions are graded C, and the stand says why.** Warm-up, cool-down, flexibility exercise
and gradual progression «may reduce the risk of CVD events and musculoskeletal injury during exercise»
(Table 1, grade C). The risks section gives the basis: «Commonly used methods to reduce musculoskeletal
injury and complications, such as the warm-up and cool-down, stretching, and gradual progression of volume
and intensity, seem to be helpful at least under some circumstances, but controlled studies substantiating
the effectiveness of these methods are insufficient (9,123,124,164,248,355).»
[@garber2011acsm]

**No warm-up or cool-down dose for injury.** The stand gives no duration, intensity or content for
either as an injury measure. Its three content statements are not about injury: Table 2 grades at A that
«Flexibility exercise is most effective when the muscle is warmed through light to moderate aerobic
activity or passively through external methods such as moist heat packs or hot baths» (flexibility
gains); a dynamic cardiorespiratory warm-up beats stretching for subsequent performance; and «A pre-event warm-up that includes both car- diorespiratory
and flexibility exercise has benefits for specific recreational sports such as dancing (143).» (unspecified
benefits; ref 143, Gremion, is a review of stretching for sports performance) [searched: `warm-up` / `warm up` / `warming` / `warm` / `cool` across chunks 01-03 with
`bin/srcgrep.py`; every non-reference hit is Table 1, the Table 2 flexibility-pattern row, the risks sentence above, the
conclusions, or the stretching-timing paragraph]
[@garber2011acsm]
The held RT stand is also silent on warm-up -> [[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].
The held warm-up meta-analysis (Ding 2022, next section) does **not** answer the stand's question: it
compares structured injury-prevention programmes against the usual warm-up in under-19s, not warming up
against not warming up in general adults. So the stand's C grade for a generic warm-up stays untested in
every held source. The stand's own warm-up reference (123) is Fradkin 2006, an RCT review of warming up and
sport injury; Fradkin is not held, so whether its trials overlap Ding's cannot be checked here.
[inferred from @garber2011acsm; @ding2022warmup]
(2026-11-04) The nearest held test is one trial in Liddle's adult pool: van Mechelen 1993, male
runners, a «19 minute warm-up & 19 minute cool- down to be done alongside every run» plus daily
stretching, RR 1.20 (0.66-2.18) read off Figure 2. Liddle does not describe what the control runners
did, so it is not a clean warm-up-vs-none contrast, and one imprecise trial does not move the C grade.
[@liddle2023effects]

**Type and intensity over volume.** «The type and intensity of the exercise seem to be more important
factors in the incidence of injury, with the vol- ume of exercise performed apparently of lesser
importance». Walking and moderate activity carry very low musculoskeletal risk; jogging, running and
competitive sports carry more; overweight and obese adults have exceeded the recommended volume «without
adverse sequelae». [@garber2011acsm]

**The initial weeks are the risk window.** «Unaccustomed exercise demands, espe- cially during the
initial weeks of a physical conditioning regimen, often result in muscle soreness, musculoskeletal in-
jury, and attrition (12).» (ref 12 is ACSM's own 1998 stand.) Ramp guidance:
[[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]].
[@garber2011acsm]

**Eccentric-only training and rhabdomyolysis.** «Training that exclusively features eccentric contrac-
tions should be discouraged because severe muscle damage and soreness as well as serious complications
such as rhab- domyolisis can ensue (78).» The risk rises in novice and experienced exercisers alike with
unaccustomed eccentric work, «particularly under hot ambient conditions». The rhabdomyolysis reference
(78, Clarkson) is a short 2007 *Sports Med* article on exertional rhabdomyolysis and acute renal failure
in marathon runners. A route-(c) caution, not a quantified risk. The caution targets unaccustomed,
eccentric-*only* regimens; progressive eccentric hamstring protocols (Lauersen's strength arm, the Nordic
programmes above) sit inside normal mixed training and are a different exposure. (the
distinction is the wiki's).
[@garber2011acsm]

**Balance and agility training for ACL and ankle injury.** «Limited evidence suggests that exercises
involving balance and agility may reduce anterior cruciate injury (165,350) and reduce recurrent ankle
injury in men and women athletes (174).» [@garber2011acsm]
Same direction as this page's proprioception/balance subgroup (RR 0.55). Not independent: ref 165 is a
systematic review of neuromuscular ACL programmes in female athletes, the trial class Lauersen pools, so
read it as attribution.

## Structured warm-up programmes in youth sport (Ding 2022)

***Warm-up* names two different exposures (type-B).** The word covers (i) a *generic* warm-up, light
aerobic work, often with stretching (the ACSM stand's C-graded statement on *warm-up* does not specify
its content), and (ii) a *structured injury-prevention programme* run in the warm-up slot: FIFA 11 /
11+ / 11+ Kids, neuromuscular or balance programmes of about 15-20 minutes, with strength work in all but one trial. Ding's meta-analysis tests (ii)
against (i): «Through meta-analysis, comparisons were made between WIPs and warm-up as usual (e.g., only
running and stretching).» Where a control arm is specified, it is aerobic work plus stretching (static
and dynamic in four of the five specified; dynamic only in Zarei 2020) (Table 3).
[@ding2022warmup]
 Consequence: the benefit below attaches to *adding programme content* (strength, balance,
landing and cutting technique) over an ordinary warm-up, not to warming up as such. No held source tests
warm-up against no warm-up for injury. Two trials (Emery 2007, Emery and Meeuwisse 2010) also added home
wobble-board training outside the session, so there the benefit is not only warm-up content
[@ding2022warmup].

**The estimate.** 15 cluster-RCTs, 21,576 participants aged 7-18, 3910 injuries: «the overall IRR was 0.64
(95% CI = 0.54--0.75), indicating that the implementation of WIP reduced sports injury by 36%.» The
effect measure is an injury rate per exposure hour.
[@ding2022warmup]

- **Spread.** I2 = 82%; «The prediction interval (0.34--1.19) indicates that the real effect of any study
  may be in the range of these values.» So a new programme in a new setting could plausibly show no
  benefit; two included trials did (Steffen 2008, FIFA 11 at 52% compliance, RR 1.0; Slauterbeck 2019,
  FIFA 11+ at 32% compliance, titled «Did Not Reduce Lower Extremity Injuries»).
  [@ding2022warmup]
- **Small-study asymmetry.** Smaller trials showed larger effects (Egger p = 0.009); trim-and-fill moves
  the estimate to IRR 0.70 (0.59-0.82), «or 30% if adjusted for bias».
  [@ding2022warmup]
- **Absolute size.** The source pools no baseline rate. Illustration only: the one control
  rate printed in its text is 3.7 injuries per 1000 h, from Steffen 2008 (female youth football), a trial
  whose own result was null. Applied to that rate, IRR 0.64 gives roughly 1.3 fewer injuries per 1000
  player-hours, and the bias-adjusted 0.70 about 1.1 fewer.
- **Certainty.** Trials were scored on 12 Furlan criteria; all scored at least 6/12, and «The absence of
  blind intervention for intervention group participants and caregivers (who perform the intervention)
  was a common methodological defect.» No GRADE or certainty rating of the pooled estimate is given.
  [@ding2022warmup]
  The reporting is loose in places: the programme-type subgroup is split 5 vs 10 while the text counts
  9 comprehensive, 5 neuromuscular and 1 balance; the trim-and-fill CI is 0.60-0.82 in the abstract and
  0.59-0.82 in the results; the funnel plot «indicated no deviations» one sentence before «asymmetry was
  apparent»; and the conclusions call the IRR a pooled odds ratio.
  [@ding2022warmup]

**Compliance is the one moderator, and it is a between-trial comparison.** Trials with compliance above
70% pooled to IRR 0.56 (0.48-0.67), those below 70% to 0.81 (0.65-1.01); between-subgroup p = 0.01, the
only significant split of eight.
[@ding2022warmup]
 Read it as adherence being part of the effect (an intervention not done has no effect), not
as a measured dose-response: trials were grouped by their own compliance, so compliance travels with
programme, sport and setting. One trial (Rossler 2018) reports compliance as N/A in Table 3 yet sits in
the >70% group of Table 4 by its counts (the 14 trials with a value split 9 above and 5 below; Table 4's
counts are wrong elsewhere, so this is a deduction)
[@ding2022warmup]. The split is consistent with, but not
a test of, Lauersen's suggestion that added burden erodes compliance: Ding has no data on what drove
compliance.

**No other subgroup differs, despite how the source reads them.** Between-subgroup p values: age 0.64,
sex 0.10, setting 0.55, level 0.46, programme type 0.60, injury site 0.70, study quality 0.61. Males pooled
to 0.47 (0.36-0.62, 3 trials) and females to 0.68 (0.44-1.04, 3 trials).
[@ding2022warmup]
The source still explains point-estimate gaps causally (neuromuscular/balance 0.59 vs comprehensive 0.66;
non-school 0.61 vs school 0.69) and says «No significant invalid results were found in all subgroup
analyses», although three subgroup CIs cross 1.
[@ding2022warmup]
 On the five-route rule, none of these is evidence of effect modification (route b): do not
pick a programme type or tailor by sex or age on this table.

**Transport: children and adolescents in organised sport, nothing else.** Inclusion required «sport
participants 18 years of age or younger»; the trials are club, school and PE settings, about half soccer (7 of 15, plus LaBella's soccer and basketball).
No adult sample (recreational or otherwise), no older or clinical sample; no programme harms
reported. [@ding2022warmup]
 For a recreational adult starting exercise, this is indirect at best: the outcome is any
time-loss or medical-attention injury in mostly team ball sports, and the programmes run mostly as a team
routine in club, school or PE settings. The held adult evidence is Lauersen's adult stratum, all exercise-based programmes pooled: «RR 0.683
(0.526–0.885, I2=72%, p<0.001) for adults», a post hoc age split in a sentence that follows its ITT
sensitivity analysis. That stratum pools every exposure type and, by Lauersen's trial populations, very
likely contains the stretching trials (two in army recruits, one internet trial in adults over 18, which
is certainly in it; stratum membership is not reported, and one trial spans both age groups), so it is not an
estimate for structured warm-up programmes in adults. Lauersen's Table 1 does hold four adult-level

**Scope the strength claim to the strata it was tested in** (elite male soccer, military recruits,
female adolescent soccer; for the last, the action is the youth programme, which contains strength), not to *active/athletic populations* at large (restated 2026-11-04; the

programmes pooled, with no strength-only pool in either (Liddle, Wu). The transfer to general or older
gym training stays unproven (an-class gap), so do not oversell *lifting prevents
injury* as a universal.

**Building tolerance vs managing the ramp are distinct injury levers.** This page answers *which
exercise prevents injury* (build tolerance; strength training the standout in the strata tested). A separate decision —
*does ramping load too fast cause injury, and is the "10% rule" sound* — lives on
[[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]] (Damsted 2018 SR: insufficient evidence for the
causal claim, no evidence for the 10% figure). The tolerance lever has the stronger evidence
base in the game-sport, recruit and youth trials (RCT-MA vs Damsted's three cohorts and one unblinded
RCT, corrected 2026-11-04 from *four heterogeneous observational studies*; restated the same day from
*the far stronger evidence base*, unscoped); they are complementary, not
competing. For runners, that page's own population, the ranking does not hold on what is shown: Wu finds programmes pooled
*insufficient evidence* in runners, with strength barely tested, so both levers are *insufficient
evidence* there. The programme evidence is randomised and the ramp evidence mostly observational (its
one RCT, Buist, is the 10%-vs-24% test), a difference in design, not in result (*Endurance runners*).

**The injury *endpoint* also varies by stratum.** This MA's injuries are general sport injuries in
athletes, recruits and youth; the *bone* overuse endpoint (stress fracture) has a distinct risk-factor set and a
distinct population on [[Stress Fracture Risk Factors in Runners]] (Wright 2016) — only prior fracture
(\~5x) and female sex (\~2.3x) are supported, and that lean/often-female host profile does **not**
transport to the obese novice (whose binding constraint is soft-tissue/joint, where this page's
strength lever would apply if it transported; restated 2026-11-04 from *where this page's strength lever
applies*. It is untested in obese novices; in novice runners, Wu's two novice strength trials, Baltich
and Toresdahl, show no clear reduction at low volume, an imprecise result rather than an absence).

method, its injury benefit C/D; the rest ungraded; so labelled practice, not trial-tested): start with

first weeks as the risk window and ramp gradually; and do not train with eccentric-only sessions. Light,
dynamic warm-up is a cheap default (practice-level, C; no dose given, so the earlier *a few minutes* is
withdrawn 2026-11-04; the held warm-up
meta-analysis tests structured programmes against this kind of warm-up in under-19s, so it neither
supports nor undercuts it). The stand is
internally split on it: Table 1 grades warm-up «may» reduce risk (C), the risks section calls the
