**The route-(c) contraindication question for exercise in the osteoporotic/osteopenic stratum.**
The efficacy pages ask whether exercise prevents falls and fractures
([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]); this page asks the *inverted* question a
patient with fragile bone actually voices — *will exercise break me, and which movements are
dangerous?* — a distinct decision (harm, not benefit) that gates whether the efficacy advice is
even attempted. First source held on it: Kunutsor 2018, a gold systematic review pairing 14
observational studies (13 unique) on modality-specific harm with an update of three previously
published interventional reviews (Howe bone-density and Giangregorio, both Cochrane; Sherrington's
BJSM falls meta-analysis).
[@kunutsor2018adverse]

**Evidence-tier note (`confidence: low`).** One stratum-specific gold SR (Kunutsor) plus the
osteoporotic subgroup of an all-population exercise-harm meta-analysis (Niemeijer, section below),
whose trial pools probably overlap; the *underlying* adverse-event data are weak by construction: «none of the trials were powered to look at adverse events including
fractures, and these were poorly reported»
[@kunutsor2018adverse].
The review's own method is light for a gold-tier SR: the authors call the design «While this is an
unorthodox approach, it does allow a wide-ranging summary of the available literature, both randomised
and non-randomised, within a single paper.», and «One author (either MC, LJ or NG) extracted data on
adverse events from the new eligible trials plus all the trials included in the previous reviews using
a self- made data extraction form.» It was commissioned by the National Osteoporosis Society to support
its consensus statement.
[@kunutsor2018adverse]
The safety verdict is a reassurance drawn mostly from the *rarity of recorded serious events* in trials
not built to detect them («Severe or serious adverse events were rarely reported.»; a few serious
events were attributed to exercise, below)
[@kunutsor2018adverse]
— so it is held low on total web support, and the under-reporting caveat below is the load-bearing
limit, not a footnote.

## The bottom line: serious harm rare in the trials that recorded it, minor events raised

The review's headline verdict is reassuring: «Serious adverse events were rare and could not be
attributed to the intervention... Patients with osteoporosis/osteopenia can safely participate in
structured exercise programmes, whether at home or in supervised facilities.»
[@kunutsor2018adverse]
The review reports «For the update of reviews, 57 additional studies were identified.» (the three
updates' own counts, 27 + 3 + 19, sum to 49; the source does not reconcile them). The authors describe
the excess as «these were mainly minor adverse events and did not stop participation», and call the
minor-event increase «what would normally be expected with physical exertion such as muscle aches». The abstract's *could not be attributed* does not hold for every trial in the
body. In the Sherrington update: «Only one trial reported two adverse serious events attributable to
the intervention: Clemson et al.74 reported a groin strain followed by surgery for inguinal hernia in
the structured exercise group, and one pelvic stress fracture in the Lifestyle Integrated Functional
Exercise group (participants with known osteoporosis was not an exclusion criteria for this trial).»
[@kunutsor2018adverse]

- **Sherrington falls-review update (107 trials, 23 288 participants).** Of these, the **8 trials of
  community-dwelling participants** that reported adverse-event counts for both arms gave «The pooled
  effect of adverse events associated with the intervention in this group was 1.08 (95%CI 1.04 to
  1.13).», a small increase in adverse events of all kinds (the source names no effect measure and
  gives no breakdown by severity). The source ranks falls the highest-incidence adverse event
  (156/1000 person-years), musculoskeletal complaints next, and fractures the lowest (21/1000
  person-years); its musculoskeletal figure, 132/10000 person-years, is in fact lower than the fracture
  rate, an inconsistency it does not address. These are falls-prevention trials in older adults, not an
  osteoporotic stratum: of the 19 added trials, «one with a clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis».
  [@kunutsor2018adverse]
- **Howe bone-density update (62 trials, 6607 participants).** More minor adverse events in the
  exercise arms (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.05-2.28, P=0.029, 24 studies). On falls: «In the pooled analysis
  there was no effect of exercise on falls overall (RR 1.04, 95%CI 0.79 to 1.37, P=0.779).» That rests
  on «Nine studies reported on falls occurring as an adverse event.», and the interval runs to a 37%
  increase, so read it as no excess detected rather than a shown null. Serious events were few and
  split across arms: «Two trials reported serious adverse events53,54 with one myocardial infarction and
  one asthma attack in the intervention groups, and one myocardial infarction and one cardiac
  dysrhythmia in the control groups.»
  [@kunutsor2018adverse]
- On severity, for the Howe update's minor events: «In most cases reported, these minor adverse events
  did not stop participation in the study.»
  [@kunutsor2018adverse]

## And a fracture-reduction hint — a trend, not a result

In the same Howe update, «11 trials reported on numbers of fractures occurring as an adverse event.»
(of 62); those trials had 31 fractures in 536 exercise participants (5.8%) vs 43 in 449 controls
(9.6%), pooling to **OR 0.60 (95% CI 0.36-1.01, P=0.053)** — the authors call it «the first evidence
to suggest that exercise interventions for bone health can reduce fractures.»
[@kunutsor2018adverse]
State it as a **trend**: the CI crosses 1 and P=0.053 — this is a suggestive signal on an
underpowered fracture endpoint, not a demonstrated reduction. It matters for the *safety* decision in
one direction only: in the 11 trials that counted fractures, the pooled estimate gives no sign that
exercise raises fracture risk, and a weak hint that it lowers it.

The observational arm (14 studies, 13 unique) mostly reassures too: «The majority of studies (ten out of
fourteen) either reported no adverse events, reduced incidence/improvement in adverse events, or no
significant change in adverse events after physical activity or exercise (Table 2).» Its Table 2 also
holds a harm-direction count, for one small cohort (Kerschan-Schindl 2000, «Exercise (n=19) and
Control group (n=6)», the completers of 33 enrolled, «25 with complete data at end of study», with more vertebral fractures in the exercise group at baseline, «Persons with
vertebral fractures: Exercise group – 9 (48%) Control group – 2 (33%)»): at follow-up «Persons with at
least one vertebral fracture: Exercise group – 6 Control group – 0», beside the same row's «No
differences between groups in terms of fracture rates, falling episodes, and neuromuscular performance
were observed.» The arm does flag a
modality-specific risk: «Activities that involved spinal flexion (certain yoga moves and sit-ups) were
associated with a greater risk of vertebral fractures but these events were rare.» One programme
(Harrison 1993, postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, 4 years of strength and aerobic training, arms
supervised vs independent exercise, no non-exercise control) reported «an increase in vertebral and
non-vertebral fractures at follow- up after the exercise programme», and in the same table cell
«Compared with the group who experienced the least improvement in fitness, the group who experienced
the greatest improvement in fitness had fewer vertebral fractures and less back pain (though not
statistically significant).» The review counts a later paper as the same study, and its 5-year
follow-up points the other way: «One further study of an ‘exercise programme modified to meet
individual requirements’ reported an increased proportion of vertebral fractures in the subgroup who
showed the least improvement in fitness20, but in a 5-year follow-up report of the same study, the
incidence of fractures was significantly reduced over the course of the study period21.» That
follow-up also has no non-exercise control (supervised vs unsupervised arms); its counts are «At study
entry, 11 subjects in each group reported having had at least one fracture in the previous 12
months, while 2 in each group reported having had a fracture over the 5-year period.» The flexion
signal is detailed in the next section.
[@kunutsor2018adverse]

## Which movements to avoid — flexion vs extension

The route-(c) *modality* signals come mostly from the observational arm plus mechanism, with one
randomised trial (brisk walking):

- **Repetitive/loaded spinal forward flexion** is the flagged concern. «However, it is mechanically
  plausible that forward flexion increases the load on the front of the vertebral bodies, and is likely
  to increase the risk of vertebral fractures82.» The observational Sinaki data, a 1984 non-randomised
  cohort with flexion n=9, extension n=25, combined n=19 and no exercise n=6, in which «All the
  treatment programmes included infrared heat and massage.», showed a fracture rate of 89% in the
  flexion group vs 16% in the extension group (53% combined).
  [@kunutsor2018adverse]
- The practical recommendation, as the authors hedge it: «It therefore seems reasonable to recommend
  that people with osteoporosis avoid specific and repetitive spinal forward flexion exercises, and
  instead focus on spinal extension.»
  [@kunutsor2018adverse]
- **But do not over-restrict — the important nuance.** «However, it needs to be emphasized that
  evidence that forward flexion exercise interventions cause vertebral fractures are limited and caution
  should be used in giving advice to patients about appropriate movements based on their low BMD
  measurements alone.»
  [@kunutsor2018adverse],
  and «However, there is no evidence that single forward flexion movements in daily activities or
  ‘roll down’ Pilates movements cause vertebral fractures – a common misconception [personal
  communication from SL based on the National Osteoporosis Society charity’s helpline statistics on
  common concerns expressed by patients].» The *misconception* half rests on helpline statistics, and
  the *no evidence* half is an absence of evidence, the same kind of reassurance this page refuses to
  read as *proven safe*. It is kept as a default because the alternative, banning everyday bending,
  costs activity; the focus becomes **safe lifting technique** for everyday flexion.
  [@kunutsor2018adverse]
- **Brisk walking (a randomised trial, not observational).** «In a study evaluating the effect of
  brisk walking on post-menopausal women who had previously sustained a distal forearm fracture, the
  intervention group had significantly more falls in the first year of follow-up compared to the control
  group (42 and 26 falls per 100 person years respectively)75.» That contrast is brisk walking vs
  control, not vs normal-pace walking. The authors make the pace comparison separately, citing
  Sherrington: «Pragmatically it makes sense for older people who may be at risk of fractures to avoid
  brisk walking as there appears to be an increased risk of falls with brisk walking as opposed to
  walking at a normal speed12, and also repetitive or loaded forward flexion.»
- **Horseback riding and golfing (observational, weak).** «Our findings from the observational data
  suggest that repetitive spinal flexion exercises, horseback riding, and golfing may be associated with
  a greater risk of adverse events such as back pain and vertebral compression fractures, although the
  evidence on horseback riding and golfing is based on one case series each and must be interpreted with
  caution.» The authors do not turn these into bans. On yoga: «In terms of specific yoga poses that
  appear to increase risk, there are modified versions of all yoga poses that experienced teachers can
  recommend, rather than avoiding yoga completely.» On riding: «However, it is difficult to draw a
  strong conclusion to suggest avoiding this activity,». On golf: «Instead, if golf is important to
  someone, the risk/ benefits of continuing should be discussed.» Their conclusion: «Some types of
  activities such as brisk walking, horseback riding and golfing need to be discussed on an individual
  basis, as they may not be appropriate.»
  [@kunutsor2018adverse]

So the modality rule is asymmetric and narrow: **substitute extension for repetitive loaded flexion
in a *structured programme*; do not police ordinary daily bending.** This is a substitution frame
([[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]-style), not a prohibition.

## Who the verdict covers — and the prior-vertebral-fracture group it barely reaches

The evidence behind the reassurance was narrow. Of the observational studies, the authors write «Few
studies recruited women over the age of 60 or with co- morbid conditions.» They limit their own conclusion: «These findings, however, cannot be
generalized to patients with additional medical problems or illnesses, or those who live in care
facilities.» Among the generalizability limits they list, «physical activity or exercises covered were
mainly group-based exercise programmes containing common physical activities used within the general
population and not high impact activities such as tennis, running or aerobics»; for the observational
studies, «However, most included supervised initial instruction to check technique.» The gaps they name
include «(iv) lack of evidence for men with osteoporosis; (v) lack of evidence for the very elderly».
[@kunutsor2018adverse]

For people who have already had a vertebral fracture, the trial evidence is the Giangregorio update;
one observational cohort above also enrolled women with prior fractures (Kerschan-Schindl 2000: «Women
with a history of postmenopausal fractures and an age-adjusted low bone mass»), and in another (Harrison
1993) «44/78 patients had vertebral fractures and 45 had a total of 86 non-vertebral fractures.», with
the timing of those fractures relative to enrolment not stated. Whether
more severe osteoporosis means more adverse events is one of the review's named gaps: «(iii)
uncertainty on the relationship between the severity of osteoporosis and occurrence of adverse
events». The Giangregorio update: 10 trials, 678 participants. Of the three trials it added, «There was
no indication that adverse events were systematically monitored in any of the included studies.» One trial reported two serious adverse events
(breathing difficulties during functional exercises). In another (Evstigneeva), «four participants in
the intervention and two in the control sustained a fracture»; counted as fractures, vertebral were 2
vs 2, and «There were five non-vertebral fractures in the control and two in the intervention group, a
difference which was not significant50.» So two control participants had seven fractures between
them; the source does not comment on it.
[@kunutsor2018adverse]

[inferred from @kunutsor2018adverse] So the
reassurance is strongest for a postmenopausal woman without major comorbidity (and, in the
observational studies, under about 60) doing supervised or instructed low- to moderate-impact exercise, and weakest for men, the very old, care-home
residents, people after a vertebral fracture, and high-impact sport.

## The load-bearing limit: adverse events are systematically under-reported

[inferred from @kunutsor2018adverse] —
this is the **streetlight instance on the harms side**, and it caps every reassurance above. In the
Sherrington update, **55 of 107 trials (51.4%) did not report on adverse events at all, and a further
26 (24.2%) reported zero occurrences** — so three-quarters of trials contribute a blank or a bare
zero. [@kunutsor2018adverse]
The review states the identification problem exactly: «it is unclear whether the many studies
identified in this review that did not report any adverse events were doing so because none occurred,
or because the data was not recorded.»
[@kunutsor2018adverse]

- **This is the *insufficient-evidence* state, not *no meaningful effect*.** A silent trial is not a
  safe trial; a reported zero from a trial not powered to detect fractures is not an observed null.
  The expectancy test *fails* here — if serious harm were happening we could not be confident these
  trials would have recorded it — so the correct reading is *serious harm is poorly measured*. What
  evidence exists is partial: fractures recorded as adverse events in 11 of 62 Howe-update trials (OR
  0.60, upper limit 1.01), which is one harm, not all serious events; and the all-population
  serious-event RR 0.96 (0.90-1.02, Niemeijer, below), which is indirect for this stratum. The
  reassurance reframes that as *harm appears rare*. Both are held; the gap between them is the finding.
- It is the **downstream twin** of [[Which Questions Get Studied]]: that concept is the *upstream*
  filter (which questions get funded and asked); this is the *in-trial* filter (a funded trial runs
  but does not record the harm). Same streetlight mechanism, different stage of the pipeline —
  cross-linked, not the same claim. Also an instance of [[Publication Bias and Selective Reporting]]
  operating within-study (selective *outcome* reporting of harms) rather than across studies.

## The all-population pooling — the osteoporotic subgroup and the reporting rate (Niemeijer)

Niemeijer pooled RCTs of exercise therapy vs a non-exercising control, taken from Cochrane reviews
searched to November 2015, across 16 condition groups. Its *Osteoporotic* group (Table 1: «Osteoporotic
Menopausal Osteopenia Postmenopausal Older adults with metabolic syndrome or low Bone Mineral Density or
fracture risk») gave a non-serious adverse-event RR of **2.14 (95% CI 1.08-4.27), 6 trials, 483
participants** (Figure 3, p6). The authors flag it: «Participants with osteoporotic pain show a
significant increase in RR, but this was based on six studies with a total of 483 participants, so the
results should be interpreted with caution.» Serious adverse events were pooled only across all
populations (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.90-1.02, 378 trials); the paper gives no osteoporotic serious-event
estimate: «As there was no increase in RR of serious adverse events (see below), only characteristics
and results from studies reporting non-serious adverse events are presented.»
[@niemeijer2020]

What the table licenses: the same direction on minor events (more with exercise), and incomplete
reporting in both. It does not compare serious events: Niemeijer gives no osteoporotic serious-event
estimate (its RR 0.96 covers all populations, and is indirect here), and Kunutsor pools none, reporting
serious events as rare, with a few attributed to exercise (above). On reporting: about half of trials in Niemeijer's pool and in the
Sherrington falls update reported any adverse-event data, and Kunutsor's 1.55 rests on the 24 of 62
Howe-update trials (about 39%) that reported minor events. It does not license
an independent confirmation, because the trials probably overlap, and it does not license reading 2.14
as larger than 1.55: populations, comparators and outcome definitions differ, and the 6-trial interval
is wide. Niemeijer's 49% generalizes the under-reporting limit above from falls trials to
exercise-therapy trials at large. Its direction-of-bias evidence may carry over to this stratum, but
it is reported for the whole pool only, not by condition: low-risk-of-bias
trials gave a higher all-population RR (1.49, vs 1.25 unclear and 1.14 high risk of bias), and the authors write «However, as
there was a trend towards a higher risk of non-serious adverse events in studies with a low risk of
bias compared with unclear and high risk of bias, this may underestimate the risk of non-serious adverse
events.» [@niemeijer2020]

## Relationship to the falls-efficacy evidence — F/extension, not independent-E

 provenance note guarding against laundered-E: one of Kunutsor 2018's three updates
re-runs Sherrington's BJSM falls meta-analysis, the **same review lineage** as
[[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]] (Julie Whitney is an author of Kunutsor and of the
Sherrington 2017 meta-analysis the wiki holds).

For the falls part, the agreement that exercise is broadly safe is therefore a **type-F refinement of a
shared evidence base** (same trials, re-examined for harms), **not** independent type-E corroboration.
The Howe and Giangregorio updates and the 14 observational studies sit outside that lineage. Marked so
the confidence is not inflated by counting one review lineage twice.

## Decision relevance

- **Reassure, then prescribe.** For an osteoporotic/osteopenic person afraid to move, the trials
  support starting structured exercise (home or supervised): serious harm was rare where it was
  recorded, though not absent (one falls trial open to people with osteoporosis attributed two serious
  events to exercise, a groin strain followed by hernia surgery and a pelvic stress fracture), within the groups the trials covered (*Who the verdict covers* above).
  [inferred from @kunutsor2018adverse] The source notes that «Patients with osteoporosis also tend to restrict physical
  activity due to the fear of falling or sustaining fractures76», so the fear is a barrier worth
  addressing directly. [@kunutsor2018adverse]
- **Expect more minor events than with no exercise.** «However, as expected with participation in
  physical activity and exercise, minor adverse events such as muscle ache may occur.» [@kunutsor2018adverse]
  Across all exercise-therapy populations Niemeijer's NNTH was 6 (one extra non-serious event per six
  people exercising) and its 6-trial osteoporotic subgroup RR 2.14 (95% CI 1.08-4.27, 483
  participants), which the authors say «should be interpreted with caution»; the Methods say
  differences between condition subgroups were evaluated by meta-regression, but no result is reported
  for condition, so 2.14 is not shown to exceed the pooled 1.19. Because low-risk-of-bias trials ran
  higher, the authors say the all-population figure «may underestimate» the non-serious risk; on the
  half of trials that did not report, they add that the trial numbers «may indicate that the identified
  estimate of increased risk may not change»;
  the risk-of-bias split is reported for the whole pool only (Figure 2), not by condition
  [searched: `risk of bias` x21, chunk 01]. [inferred from @niemeijer2020; @kunutsor2018adverse]
- **Substitute extension for repetitive loaded spinal flexion** in programmed exercise; teach safe
  lifting for everyday bending rather than banning daily flexion (avoid the over-restriction
  misconception).
- **Prefer normal-pace to brisk walking** where fall risk is the concern: the authors' pragmatic
  advice, resting on the Sherrington pace comparison and one brisk-walking trial against control.
- **Hold the safety verdict as provisional** — it rests on trials that did not measure harm well; the
  honest statement to a patient is *serious harm looked rare in the trials that recorded it, though a
  few serious events were attributed to exercise, and minor aches are more common*, not *proven safe*,
  and the gap is a
  named [[Which Questions Get Studied]]-class hole ( for a purpose-designed
  adverse-event trial).
- **The lever is retained; only the modality is constrained.** Physical activity is a
  cross-cutting big-rock lever shared across the age-related musculoskeletal conditions
  ([[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]]); osteoporosis does not remove it, it adds a
  condition-specific *modality* caveat (substitute extension for repetitive loaded spinal flexion). This
  is the shared-lever logic with an outcome-specific qualifier, not an exception to it — the same family
  as the falls benefit weakening once frailty is established.
