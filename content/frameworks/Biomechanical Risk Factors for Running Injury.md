**Peripheral scope** (exercise-programming), but the outcome is **patient-important**: a running-related
injury (RRI) is an overuse injury that stops or modifies training for weeks (route-(c) harm, and the
dropout that ends an exercise attempt). The decision this page answers: *do baseline biomechanical and
musculoskeletal (MSK) measurements — gait kinematics/kinetics, muscle strength, joint range of motion,
lower-limb alignment, foot posture — predict who develops RRI, and should a runner be screened on them to
prevent injury?* The headline is a **strong negative**. [inferred from @peterson2022]

## Headline — the null

A gold SR+MA of **prospective** studies only (the design that can support a risk-factor claim):
«Thirty studies (3404 runners), testing over 100 discrete biomechanical and musculoskeletal risk factors for RRI, were included. Nineteen studies were pooled in twenty-five separate meta-analyses.»
[@peterson2022].

The verdict: «Overall, results of the meta-analyses in this systematic review do not support the role of
biomechanical and musculoskeletal measures as risk factors for the development of RRI in non-elite
runners. Despite significant findings in several individual studies, twenty-three of twenty-five pooled
analyses detected no relationship»
[@peterson2022].

**The decision-change — screening is not justified.** «There is currently insufficient evidence for any
musculoskeletal or biomechanical assessments as risk factors for RRI to recommend their use for
preventative screening, clinically»
[@peterson2022]. Gait analysis, strength/ROM
batteries and alignment screens, widely sold to runners as injury-prevention tools, are not supported by
the prospective evidence as predictors of who gets hurt.

## The two significant pooled effects — both clinically trivial

Only 2 of 25 pooled analyses reached significance, and the authors explicitly down-weight both:

| Measure | Effect (95% CI) | Studies | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knee extension strength (lower in injured) | SMD **-0.19** (-0.36 to -0.02), p=0.03, I2=0% | 4 | «may not be clinically important»; a «trivial effect size» |
| Hip adduction velocity, stance (lower in injured) | MD **-12.80** deg/s (-25.22 to -0.38), p=0.04, I2=0% | 2 | «interpreted very cautiously»; both constituent studies individually null, one small/uncertain quality |

[@peterson2022]
[@peterson2022]

The source's own summary: «the associated trivial to small effects sizes suggest these findings should be
treated with caution» [@peterson2022]. Neither
effect is a bare threshold; both are carried with their CI. The knee-extension CI's upper bound (-0.02)
nearly touches the null, and the hip-adduction pool rests on two studies that were each individually
non-significant — a pooled significance that is fragile by construction.

## Risk factor, not a modifiable lever — the epistemic boundary

These are **predictors measured at baseline in prospective cohorts**, not interventions. A null
predictor result says *baseline knee-extension strength does not identify who will get injured* — it does
**not** say *strengthening the knee will not help an injured or deconditioned runner*. The latter is an
intervention question answered by [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]] (where strength
training *does* reduce sports-injury risk in RCT-MA). Do not launder this screening-null into a claim that
strength or mobility work is useless; the two questions sit on different evidence designs and different
causal objects. [inferred from @peterson2022]

## Why the null — the load-capacity causal shift

Peterson reads the null through a causal framework in which biomechanics is not a standalone cause:
«atypical biomechanical function alone does not cause RRI but may interact with training characteristics
as an effect measure modifier to contribute to the risk of RRI», and RRI «occurs when a runner possesses
multiple risk factors, and then participates in running under particular circumstances to a degree which
exceeds a structure's load capacity»
[@peterson2022]. The null for static biomechanics
«is given some credence» by exactly this: biomechanical parameters were «considered, independent of
participation-related variables»
[@peterson2022]. The modifiable signal, on this
reading, lives in **training load**, not in the runner's static anatomy -> [[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]].

## Relation to Ceyssens 2019 — expands, largely subsumes

Peterson cites Ceyssens et al. 2019 (ref [37]) and positions itself as building on it: «The results of
the present review also expand on those of Ceyssens et al. [37] who performed a systematic review of
biomechanical risk factors for RRI using a narrative synthesis, justifying the omission of meta-analyses
based on between-study heterogeneity»
[@peterson2022]. Peterson covers the same gait
variable families Ceyssens reviewed — running kinematics (9 studies), kinetics (8 studies), spatiotemporal
parameters — but reports them in **narrative synthesis** because between-study heterogeneity precluded
pooling, and adds meta-analysis of the poolable static/strength/alignment measures that Ceyssens did not
pool. Peterson is newer (2022 vs 2019), higher-design (SR+MA vs qualitative SR), and reaches the same
direction. [inferred from @peterson2022]

## Transportability — the non-elite runner is not the obese novice

Peterson's stratum is **non-elite adult runners who are already running**; the review excluded
participants confirmed as competitive/elite where it could. This null does **not** transport to the
sedentary, obese, or untrained novice: it says baseline biomechanical variation among *established*
runners does not predict injury within that group — it is silent on whether an unconditioned joint or
tendon in someone starting from inactivity carries a different risk profile (where the connective-tissue
conditioning and impact-grading concerns of the fabric's deconditioned-novice stratum apply). The
support-factor profile differs. Treat the non-elite-runner null as evidence for the running stratum, not
for the exercise-prescription decision of a beginner. [inferred from @peterson2022]

## Synthesis — intrinsic screening is low-yield; load is the lever

Two independent prospective-evidence reviews converge on the same conclusion from **disjoint author teams
and different injury scopes** `[E-independent]`:

| | Peterson 2022 | Wright 2015 |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | all-cause RRI | stress fracture (an RRI subtype) |
| Factors | biomechanical + MSK *measurements* | all intrinsic risk factors |
| Verdict on biomechanical/anatomical measures | 23/25 pooled analyses null | a near-total absence of supported risk factors (verbatim on [[Stress Fracture Risk Factors in Runners]]) |
| What survives | 2 pooled effects, both clinically trivial | only prior stress fracture (\~5x) + female sex (\~2.3x) |
| Nature of survivors | trivial-effect measures, not actionable | non-modifiable markers (history, sex), not biomechanical |

**Same-quantity check:** both reviews test biomechanical/anatomical *measures* as predictors of running
injury and both return null-to-trivial there — the overlapping quantity agrees. Wright's two survivors
(prior fracture, female sex) lie **outside** Peterson's measurement scope, so they neither confirm nor
contradict it; they are different variables, not a joined tension. The emergent claim is that
**intrinsic/static-trait screening for running injury is low-yield across injury types** — the predictors
that survive are either non-modifiable markers or clinically trivial, and the actionable, modifiable
signal sits in training load, not in the runner's anatomy -> [[Training Load Progression and Running Injury]], and the prevention evidence that *does* work is interventional strength/neuromuscular training,
not screening -> [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]]. [inferred from @peterson2022]

**Confidence: low** — the direction is corroborated across two independent reviews, but Peterson's own
base is heterogeneity-limited (many variables unpoolable; measurement heterogeneity across overground vs
treadmill, isokinetic vs hand-held dynamometry) and the surviving effects are fragile. The null is
believed as a *screening-is-not-justified* decision, not as proof that biomechanics is causally inert.

## The open loop

No operation here grades this against realized injury outcomes; the finding is that current *prospective
association* evidence does not support biomechanical screening, which is a statement about the evidence,
not a validated prevention protocol. [inferred from @peterson2022]
