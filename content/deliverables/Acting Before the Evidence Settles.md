Sometimes a guideline body waits while you, deciding for yourself, should act — and the reason is
not that the body knows something you do not. **It is answering a different question.** A guideline
optimizes a population outcome under constraints you do not share: one message for millions,
safe at scale, communicable. You are deciding for one person, and **your threshold to act moves
with how reversible the choice is, how much it costs, and how large the lever is.** None of that
moves the certainty of the evidence. Keep the two dials apart, and much of the apparent conflict
dissolves.

A direction can be well-founded long before a pooled magnitude exists. **Triangulate** — weigh
trials where they exist, cohorts, genetic natural experiments, dose-response curves and
biological mechanism by how well each fits the question — and back the best-supported direction.
A mechanism earns a *direction*, never a *magnitude*, and it earns even that only with human
corroboration. There is a floor beneath this, not open water: **admit only a claim that is
falsifiable and somewhat quantifiable on a human outcome**, hold the evidence you like and the
evidence you dislike to the same standard, and mark a direction as a direction rather than
dressing it as a settled effect.

Two mistakes bracket the honest path, opposite and equally costly: **acting on nothing**, and
**refusing to act until a proof arrives that this field cannot produce.** Waiting is itself a
choice to keep the current exposure, and its risks keep accruing while you wait. One bound
outlives every section below — the loop is open. This guide can judge only whether a
well-informed advisor *would* act this way, never whether the person who did was better off.


## "How good is the evidence?" and "should I act?" are different questions

The single move that unlocks the rest is to **separate two judgments that ordinary advice welds
together**: how confident you are that an effect is real and about this big, and how strongly you
should act on it. GRADE treats the separation of these two judgments as central to a sound
recommendation. The two do not track each other. High-certainty
evidence routinely pairs with a *weak* recommendation when the right choice depends on the
person's values; low-certainty evidence can warrant strong action when the balance of benefit and
harm is lopsided enough. Certainty is one input to the decision, not a proxy for it.
-> [[Certainty of Evidence vs Strength of Recommendation]]

**A guideline's caution is a fact about its standpoint, not a verdict you must inherit.** A body
optimizes a population outcome under communicability and safety-at-scale; a stringent or a cautious
recommendation can be exactly right *there* and the wrong bar for one person choosing for
themselves. This is the most common reason guidance and a defensible individual decision diverge —
a difference of standpoint, legitimate on both sides, implying no error by either. The body's
threshold can also be moved by considerations you do not share: WHO's own evidence-to-decision
table lists, among the things that shape a nutrition recommendation, the «potential impact on
national economies» [@who2023saturated]. Which
objective moved a piece of guidance is a reading skill, and none of those objectives is *yours*.
-> [[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]]

So what scales your threshold — **reversibility, cost, and the size of the lever** — and what does
not: the certainty grade. A cheap, reversible change on a large lever justifies acting on a
thinner direction than an expensive, irreversible one on a marginal lever. **Never launder the
threshold into the grade**, or the reverse: "I would act on this" is not "the evidence is strong,"
and "the evidence is weak" is not "I should not act."

**Much legitimate individual-versus-population difference needs no subgroup claim at all.** The
relative effect of an intervention is usually similar across a wide range of people; the
*absolute* benefit is that relative reduction applied to a person's own baseline risk, so it
varies across people even when the relative effect does not. GRADE draws the consequence directly —
recommendations «may differ across subgroups of patients at different baseline risk of an outcome,
despite there being a single relative risk that applies to all of them»
[@grade].

A modest, uncertain relative effect can therefore be **decisive for someone at high baseline risk
and immaterial for someone at low risk, with no disagreement about the evidence.** This is the safe
route to acting where a population recommendation is silent: it needs prognostic information only,
not a claim that the treatment works differently in you. Personalizing the *relative* effect is the
expensive route and demands positive interaction evidence, not mechanistic plausibility.
-> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]

## A direction can be sound without a pooled magnitude

**Demanding the meta-analysis before saying anything would leave this field mute.** You cannot
blind a person to a whole diet, randomize how they live for forty years, or measure real intake
cleanly, so the long blinded whole-diet trial mostly does not and cannot exist. Quality is
**fit-to-question, not pedigree**: weigh a randomized trial where one exists, a cohort, a genetic
natural experiment, a dose-response curve and a human-corroborated mechanism *together*, by how
well each answers the question — not ranked by a single design. RCT-or-nothing is the wrong bar
here, and applying it is itself a way to stay stuck.

**What a synthesis-without-pooling can and cannot license is precise.** Counting how many studies
reached statistical significance is «unacceptable» — its power to detect a real effect falls toward
zero as studies accumulate (except with large studies and at least moderate effects)
[@cochranehandbook2024].
Counting the *direction* of effect is legitimate, but it answers a strictly weaker question — *is
there any evidence of an effect?* — and yields no magnitude. So a defensible **direction** is often
available where a **magnitude** is not: treat a non-pooled synthesis as direction-and-existence
evidence, never as effect-size evidence. -> [[Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis]]

**Observational evidence can carry more than its design allows, but the exits are narrow.** GRADE
raises certainty for a large effect, a dose-response gradient, or plausible confounding running
*against* the finding — and the magnitude bar is high. Only an effect larger than about a factor of
two — «>2 or <0.5» [@poole2017] — is big enough
for the size alone to argue causation, and that size is judged on the whole interval, not the point
estimate. Most nutritional associations sit far inside that bar (the typical red- and
processed-meat associations, for instance), where the association on its own is **insufficient —
never false** — and needs triangulation to move.
-> [[Upgrading Observational Evidence]]

**The strongest non-trial routes are a natural experiment and a convergence.** Mendelian
randomization uses inherited genotype as a confounding-resistant proxy for lifelong exposure — a
natural experiment, the strongest rung of the mechanistic gradient. And the feature that separated
durable nutrition findings from the ones that later reversed was not study count or effect size but
**convergence across independent method classes**. Folic acid and neural-tube defects held because
biochemistry, epidemiology, randomized trials and molecular genetics each reached it separately; the
reversals, by contrast, rested on many studies of a single method class. A mechanism admitted this way earns only a
direction, is marked as mechanism, and stays subject to whole-organism compensation reversing the
naive prediction. -> [[Upgrading Observational Evidence]]

## How the methods pin a direction a meta-analysis cannot give

Three techniques reach a direction where a pooled magnitude is impossible or silent, and each buys
a specific thing at a specific price.

**Emulate the target trial.** Behind every observational estimate is a hidden question: *what
randomized experiment are you trying to emulate?* Write it down — the eligibility, the strategies
compared, a single aligned time-zero, the outcome — and either the analysis maps to a coherent
trial or it does not. Where no such trial can be written, the estimate answers no causal question;
where time-zero is misaligned (classifying a person by a treatment they only later received),
immortal-time bias enters by construction. The discipline of specifying the trial surfaces these
errors before they contaminate the estimate. It also reframes the famous observational-versus-trial
discordances: often the naive analysis emulated no trial at all, so the fix is a better-posed
emulation, not blanket deference to the trial. -> [[The Target Trial (Emulation and the Well-Defined Intervention)]]

**Decompose an ill-defined exposure into a well-defined one.** A vague label — "obesity," "fibre,"
"red meat" — hides several *versions of treatment* whose effects need not agree (weight lost by diet
versus by illness; fibre from an isolate versus from the whole food). Until the version is pinned
there is no single effect to estimate. The test is whether the category boundary carries
information: **if the variation within the category exceeds the variation between categories, the
category-level number describes no actual food.** Name the sub-component that carries the mechanism —
cereal fibre rather than "whole grain," long-chain n-3 rather than "fish" — and the direction
sharpens. -> [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]

**Know what each buys.** Mendelian randomization buys a confounding-resistant direction but not the
magnitude a real-world intervention would deliver. Target-trial emulation buys a well-posed question
but still needs the confounders actually measured. Decomposition buys a usable exposure but often
leaves a thinner evidence base for each sub-component than the aggregate carried. None of the three
manufactures a pooled magnitude; each converts a muddled question into an answerable one, which is
what a decision needs first.

## Waiting has a cost too

**"We cannot say" and "we can say it does not help" are different states, and confusing them is how
waiting turns into a mistake.** The evidence menu has four states — benefit, harm, no meaningful
effect, and **insufficient evidence** — and the last two are not the same. USPSTF makes the
distinction procedural: its "I" statement fires when «the current evidence is insufficient to assess
the balance of benefits and harms ... Evidence is lacking, of poor quality, or conflicting»
[@uspstfmanual2022], which is a separate output from a confident
finding of no benefit. A null point estimate is not enough to conclude "no effect" — you must be
confident of the null. -> [[The Insufficient-Evidence Statement]]

**Apply the expectancy test before reading silence as a null.** If the effect were real, could we
realistically expect to have seen the evidence by now? Silence from an *unstudied* question is not a
null result, and an "insufficient" verdict on a decades-old, heavily studied question carries
different information from the same verdict on a novel one. -> [[The Insufficient-Evidence Statement]]

The two failure modes are mirror images, and both are live.

- **Credulity — acting on nothing.** Treating an unstudied question as settled, or a mechanism as
  though it were an outcome. This is the error the anti-rationalization guard below exists to catch.
- **Paralysis — waiting for a proof this field cannot deliver.** Demanding the blinded, lifetime,
  whole-diet randomized trial that measurement error and unblindability forbid, and calling the wait
  rigor. This is the more seductive error precisely because refusing to conclude *reads* as
  discipline.

What discriminates them is two questions asked out loud: **is a defensible direction actually in
hand** — triangulated, human-corroborated, marked as direction — and **is the cost of waiting being
counted?** Waiting is never neutral; it is a decision to keep the current exposure, whose own risks
accrue in the meantime. A high-certainty grade is not the price of admission to act, and an absent
grade is not a licence to do nothing.

## Reading a signal that stops at the null

A common case forces the decision sharply: an interval whose bound just touches the null — a
relative risk running, say, from a clear reduction up to no effect. **Read as a binary
"not significant, so no effect," it licenses nothing; read as what it is, it can license a
decision.** A confidence interval is a range of effect sizes compatible with the data, not a
significance verdict, and the point estimate is the best-supported value inside it. As the
statistics-reform literature puts it, «singling out one particular value (such as the null value) in
the interval as "shown" makes no sense»
[@amrhein2019]. A null-touching interval that
leans to benefit is *cannot say*, not *no effect*.
-> [[Reading a Confidence Interval]]

**The decision this licenses, and its limits.** Where the point estimate favours benefit and most
of the interval lies on the benefit side, **that skew is a legitimate input to a low-cost,
reversible, reasonable-substitution decision** — you may act on the best-supported direction. Three
disciplines bound it, and together they are the anti-rationalization guard applied to a single
interval:

- **The skew is a decision input, not a certainty upgrade.** A wide null-touching interval stays
  low-certainty however favourably it is shaped. Acting on it is a threshold judgment; it does not
  raise the evidence grade, and "the interval leans to benefit" must never be laundered into
  "benefit is established."
- **The weight the skew earns rides on identification, not on interval width.** Width measures random
  error only; a *tight* interval can be centred on the wrong number when confounding or a
  mis-specified comparator biased the estimate. So the credence a favourable skew deserves depends on
  whether the effect is well-identified — the target-trial question above — not on how narrow the
  interval looks.
- **A frequentist interval is not a probability over the truth.** "There is a high chance the effect
  is protective" is a posterior, and it needs an explicit prior; the interval's coverage is a
  property of the method, not a belief about your one estimate. Describe the compatible range and its
  best-supported value; do not silently convert it into a probability of benefit.

The mechanics behind these — the compatibility-interval reading, why precision is *width* rather than
null-inclusion, why coverage attaches to the procedure and not to your single interval — are the
estimate-layer detail, deferred to [[Reading a Confidence Interval]]. What belongs here is only the
decision: a skewed sub-significant signal is actionable under asymmetric costs and reversibility, and
is not a finding.

## The discipline that keeps this from becoming wishful thinking

Acting on directional evidence is one short step from believing whatever is convenient, and the
step is guarded by three rules working together.

**Symmetric standards.** Hold the evidence you like and the evidence you dislike to the same bar.
Demanding more of the evidence you would rather reject is the main way motivated reasoning survives
an "evidence-based" framing, and it runs in both directions: a small *protective* signal earns
exactly the doubt a small *harmful* one does. Symmetric standards will often yield asymmetric
conclusions — that is evidence working, not bias.

**Mark a direction as a direction, and watch the uniformity tell.** Every step that turns an
estimate into a decision is dual-use: decomposing to the right sub-question, reading the skew,
weighting the costly tail, leaning on a mechanism — each is how a careful analyst works *and* the
cover for "the study doesn't apply to my case." The per-step check is whether the move is
*evidenced or merely asserted* — the finer category has its own outcome evidence, the mechanism has
human corroboration, the cost structure is real rather than conjured. The backstop, because a
determined rationalizer will always claim each step is evidenced, is the pattern: **if the
transformations always land you where you already wanted to be, that uniformity is the signature,
however defensible each step looks alone.** The roughly-right answer is usually the less exciting
one. -> [[The Estimate-to-Action Gap]]

**The demarcation line — and it moves with evidence, in both directions.** Admit a claim to act on
only if it makes a falsifiable, somewhat quantifiable claim on a patient-important human outcome. An
effect that is mechanistically reasonable in cells or animals but not yet shown in live humans is a
*candidate*, held under the transportability caveat — neither asserted as a finding nor dismissed —
and it graduates on human evidence. An unfalsifiable or a measured-and-null claim is out. A current
fad meets the same bar and is demoted if the human evidence does not hold; being discussed or popular
is never a pass. This is the line that separates "act on a defensible direction" from "believe what
you like."

One corollary keeps the whole procedure honest against guidance. When a body waits and you would
act, **classify why before you defer**: a different standpoint, a different evidence base, genuine
disagreement on the same evidence, revision lag, or a process defect. Only three of those mean the
guidance is better evidenced than your reasoning. A body's conservatism is frequently its standpoint
talking, not its knowledge — do not mistake caution for a verdict. -> [[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]]

## Caveats and boundaries

- **This is an open loop, and that limit is structural, not temporary.** No operation here grades a
  choice against a realized individual outcome. The guide verifies only the *would*-form — whether a
  well-informed advisor would act this way — never whether the person who acted was better off. No
  amount of further evidence closes this loop; it is a property of the question, not a gap to fill.

- **Adherence is licensed as a direction, not a number.** A change not sustained has no effect, and
  effectiveness data for a given person are sparse, so the direction (a realized effect below the
  idealized one) is carried while the magnitude is not. Shared with [[Better than What]].
- **Deferrals.** *Why* the evidence has ceilings — measurement error, the unblindable whole diet, the
  dominance of observation, the open loop — is not re-derived here -> [[Limits of Evidence]]. Framing
  the chosen action as a substitution, and picking *which tail* of a known interval to act on, live
  on [[Better than What]]. The seam is clean: that page presumes a directional estimate and a
  comparator already exist and asks which quantile to act on; **this page asks the prior question —
  whether to act at all when the pooled evidence is silent, and how a direction is even reached.**

## Where this nets out

If you are waiting for the meta-analysis before you change anything, ask first whether you are
waiting on the *evidence* or on the *standpoint of a body that was never deciding for you*. **Move
the action threshold, not the certainty grade.** Reach a direction by triangulation — trials,
cohorts, genetic experiments, dose-response, human-corroborated mechanism — and act on it when the
change is cheap, reversible, and aimed at a real lever, especially when your own baseline risk makes
the absolute stakes large.

Hold that permission on a short leash: same standards for evidence you like and dislike, a direction
marked as a direction, and only claims that could be shown wrong on a human outcome. Where a
defensible direction genuinely is not in hand, **name the state as insufficient and say so** — that
too is a decision, and refusing to conclude is not the same as being careful. What none of this can
tell you is whether you ended up better off; that loop stays open, and the honest guide says so.
