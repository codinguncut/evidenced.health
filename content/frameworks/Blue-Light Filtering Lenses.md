Blue-light filtering (or 'blue-blocking') spectacle lenses attenuate UV and part of the
short-wavelength visible band (\~400-500 nm) reaching the eye. They are heavily marketed and
increasingly prescribed on three claims: they **reduce digital eye strain**, **improve sleep**, and
**protect the macula / retina**. A gold Cochrane systematic review (17 RCTs, 619 adults, six
countries; follow-up <1 day to 5 weeks) tested all three and found **no demonstrated
patient-important benefit for any of them.** The honest reading is a graded REFUTE: probably no
effect on the visual outcomes, and genuinely insufficient evidence (not a proven null) on the
sleep and macular claims. The authors' bottom line: «Overall, based on relatively limited clinical
trial data, these findings do not support the prescription of blue-light filtering lenses to the
general population for the purpose of reducing visual fatigue or enhancing BCVA. Potential eﬀects
on sleep are indeterminate» [@singh2023bluelight]

**Nucleus of the `blue-light-lenses` cluster** — the intervention-appraisal home; the macular arm
cross-links to [[Age-Related Macular Degeneration and Modifiable Exposures]], the sleep arm to the
sleep cluster.

## Why no meta-analysis — the evidence is thin and heterogeneous

No outcome could be pooled: the trials used different populations (healthy adults through insomnia,
major depression, bipolar disorder), different lens products (varying blue-light attenuation),
different follow-up windows, and mostly failed to report combinable quantitative data. **None of the
17 trials had low risk of bias across all seven Cochrane RoB domains**; \~65% were high-risk for
detection bias (unmasked outcome assessors) and \~59% for performance bias (unmasked
participants/personnel). So every verdict below is descriptive synthesis, not a pooled magnitude.
[@singh2023bluelight]

Masking is the structural weakness here: blue-light lenses have a visible colour tint, so
participants can often tell which arm they are in — the one trial that formally checked masking
integrity found it **unsuccessful** (Singh 2021). This is not incidental sloppiness; it is intrinsic
to blinding a coloured optical device, and it inflates any subjective-symptom or sleep-score benefit.
[@singh2023bluelight]

## The four evidence states, by outcome

**Eye strain / visual fatigue — probably no meaningful effect (low certainty).** All three trials
reporting visual-fatigue scores found no significant difference. The only trial with usable numbers
and robust methodology — Singh 2021, a double-masked RCT and the *only* one recruiting symptomatic
computer users (the marketed population) — reported MD **9.76 units** (95% CI -33.95 to 53.47;
P = 0.66; 120 participants), i.e. no difference (and the point estimate points the wrong way, toward
*worse* symptoms). [@singh2023bluelight]

**Critical flicker-fusion frequency (CFF) — little to no difference (low certainty), on a suspect
surrogate.** Singh 2021: MD **-1.13 Hz** (95% CI -3.00 to 0.74; P = 0.24). The one discordant trial
(Lin 2017) reported a \~2 Hz benefit from a 'high'-block lens — but it was industry-funded,
unregistered, did not mask outcome assessors, and reported no numeric data. Separately, the review
flags that CFF itself may not track the thing it stands in for: «using CFF as a surrogate objective
marker of visual fatigue symptoms, as has been adopted in previous studies, may not be appropriate»
(two 2022 studies found no CFF-symptom association). A benefit on a surrogate that does not transmit
to the patient-important outcome would not be a benefit anyway ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).
[@singh2023bluelight]

**Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) — probably little or no effect (MODERATE certainty — the
strongest verdict on the page).** One cross-over trial in pseudophakic adults (Hammond 2015): MD
**0.00 logMAR** (95% CI -0.02 to 0.02; P = 0.86; 156 participants). [@singh2023bluelight]

**Sleep quality and daytime alertness — insufficient evidence (very low certainty), not a null.** Six
RCTs (148 participants) measured subjective sleep; three reported improvement, three reported no
difference, and the magnitude could not be calculated from the reporting. All three trials with
quantitative data were non-significant (Esaki 2017 MD 19.90, CI -1.86 to 41.66, P = 0.07; Esaki 2020
MD 8.90, CI -6.13 to 23.93, P = 0.25; Janku 2020 PSQI MD 0.03, CI -2.53 to 2.59, P = 0.98). Crucially,
almost every sleep trial studied a **clinical** population (insomnia, major depression, bipolar
disorder) — *not* the healthy computer users the lenses are marketed to — so even the mixed signal
does not transport to the target consumer. Daytime alertness: two RCTs (42 participants), no
difference, very low certainty. [@singh2023bluelight] [@singh2023bluelight]

Singh's sleep arm is **subjective** sleep only; the **objective (actigraphic)** evidence for
*evening-worn* blue-blocking glasses is a distinct, later pooled estimate, covered in its own section
below (Luna-Rangel 2025).

**Macular health / AMD protection — insufficient evidence (no data at all).** «None of the 17 studies
included in this review evaluated the role of blue-light filtering lenses for protecting the macula.
As such, we were unable to identify any RCT evidence to support the application of blue-light
filtering lenses ... for providing macular protection.» This is the *unstudied* state, not *no
effect* — the trial that could answer it (large cohort, multi-year follow-up) has not been done. Same
for **serum melatonin, contrast sensitivity, discomfort glare, colour discrimination, and patient
satisfaction** — no included trial reported any of them. [@singh2023bluelight]

**Adverse events — no evidence of harm (low certainty).** Reported events (headache, increased
depressive symptoms, lowered mood, discomfort) were infrequent, mild, occurred in control arms too,
and were mostly attributed to wearing glasses generally rather than the filtering lens itself. So the
decision is *no-benefit / wasted money*, not *unsafe*: «there is no clear evidence from reports of
adverse events that blue-light filtering spectacle lenses are unsafe to wear.» [@singh2023bluelight] [@singh2023bluelight]

## Objective (actigraphic) sleep — evening blue-blocking glasses (Luna-Rangel 2025)

**Scope — a narrower, cleaner exposure than Singh's.** Luna-Rangel pooled only *evening-worn*
amber/orange-tinted blue-blocking glasses (BBGs), worn 2-3 h before bed (or 6 p.m. to bedtime),
against visually-matched clear-lens controls, with **objective actigraphy** as the outcome. This is a
tighter cell than Singh's broad *blue-light-filtering spectacle lenses* (which spans all-day computer
lenses across eye-strain, sleep, and macular use-cases): same device *family*, but here the
use-case is circadian/sleep only and the measurement is objective, not subjective. The two sleep
evidence bases **overlap rather than corroborate independently**: two of Luna-Rangel's three pooled
trials (Shechter 2018, Knufinke 2019) also sit in Singh's six-trial subjective-sleep arm (Burkhart
2009, Esaki 2017, Esaki 2020, Janku 2020, Knufinke 2019, Shechter 2018)
[@singh2023bluelight]. What differs is the *measurement* —
Singh reads those trials on subjective scores (and counted both shared trials among its positives),
Luna-Rangel on objective actigraphy (null)
[@lunarangel2025bbg]. So the agreement on
no-demonstrated-benefit is largely the same small trials read two ways, not backing from separate
evidence — it is NOT a type-E robustness booster.

**The pooled estimate is null on all four outcomes — but underpowered, not a proven no-effect.**
Three double-blind crossover RCTs, **n = 49 total** (14 insomnia, 20 healthy adults, 15 athletes),
random-effects meta-analysis, all four actigraphic outcomes non-significant with heterogeneity
**I2 = 0%**. Effects are a handful of minutes against control baselines of roughly SOL 16-22 min,
TST 330-450 min, SE 77-86%, WASO 13-84 min — small either way:

| Outcome | Pooled MD (BBG vs clear) | 95% CI | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep-onset latency (SOL) | -4.86 min | -20.23 to 10.52 | 0.54 |
| Total sleep time (TST) | +8.75 min | -35.31 to 52.82 | 0.70 |
| Sleep efficiency (SE) | -0.61 pct-pt | -7.58 to 6.35 | 0.86 |
| Wake after sleep onset (WASO) | -1.47 min | -14.94 to 11.99 | 0.83 |

The confidence intervals are **wide** (SOL spans -20 to +11 min), so this is a **weakly-powered
pooled null** — consistent with no effect *and* with a small benefit; it is not evidence that BBGs do
nothing. The authors' own reading keeps both: «BBGs may provide small improvements in sleep, but
current evidence from RCTs does not support significant effects. Larger, well-powered trials with
standardized protocols are needed.» [@lunarangel2025bbg]

**What this moves.** The *objective* sleep arm goes from **no SR held / insufficient** (Singh pooled
no actigraphic data) to **one small pooled RCT null, underpowered (n=49), I2=0%**. It is the first MA
restricted to actigraphy-from-crossover-trials: «this is the first meta-analysis focused specifically
on actigraphy-derived sleep outcomes from randomized crossover trials» (a design that cuts
between-subject variance). The direction of convergence with Singh's *subjective* sleep arm — both
land on *no significant benefit demonstrated* — adds little *independent* weight, because the two draw
on largely the same trials read by different measurement methods (subjective scores vs objective
actigraphy), not disjoint evidence; and both are non-significant partly *because* they are underpowered,
so this is agreement on absence-of-signal, not a confirmed null.
[@lunarangel2025bbg]

**Actigraphy is objective but still a surrogate.** Wrist-actigraphy estimates rest-activity, not the
patient-important endpoints — perceived sleep quality, next-day function, daytime alertness — and can
misclassify quiet wakefulness as sleep; the *trajectory* / next-day-function outcome most people care
about is unmeasured here ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]). Risk of bias (Cochrane RoB 2) was overall low with
some concerns (washout adequacy, under-described randomization). [@lunarangel2025bbg]

**Where a benefit might still hide (open, route-b).** Most participants were healthy adults with
normal baseline sleep, which caps detectable improvement; the authors suggest BBGs are «most promising
in specific clinical subgroups rather than in the general population» — evening hyperarousal, insomnia,
circadian misalignment. That is an effect-modification *hypothesis* with **no positive interaction
evidence** yet (route (b) unmet), not a stratified finding — a `G`-gap, not a recommendation. No
trial reported the actual melanopic light dose at the eye, so a dose-response cannot be drawn.
[@lunarangel2025bbg]

**Decision framing.** Evening BBGs are cheap, low-risk, and unendorsed: «no clinical guidelines
currently endorse BBGs as a standard intervention for insomnia or circadian rhythm disorders»; the
authors position them as «a pragmatic adjunct, not a stand-alone therapy». For a reasonably-healthy
adult the objective evidence shows no demonstrated benefit at the margin — the realistic substitutes
for evening light hygiene (dimming lighting, limiting evening screens, warm *night* display modes) are
the comparators, and behaviour-plus-BBG synergy (e.g. with CBT-I) is untested. [@lunarangel2025bbg]

## The marketed premise is itself weak

Even the rationale for the intervention is poorly supported, which the review documents directly:

- **Environmental blue light is far below the ocular-damage threshold.** Device emission measured at
  0.034-0.380 W m^-2 sr^-1 vs an ICNIRP damage limit >100 W m^-2 sr^-1 — «at least 100-fold less than
  the intensity considered to have the potential to cause eye damage ... it is unlikely that
  environmental sources of blue light will cause retinal damage.» [@singh2023bluelight]
- **Population evidence does not link light to AMD.** «10 out of the 12 major population-based studies
  that sought to determine whether there was a relationship between light exposure and AMD did not
  report a positive association.» [@singh2023bluelight]
- **The aging eye is already a blue-light filter.** The crystalline lens yellows with age such that
  «by 50 years of age, only 20% of short-wavelength visible light reaches the retina» — so it is
  «unclear how blue-light filtering spectacle lenses might provide any benefit(s) in adults with a
  physiologically-yellowed lens due to age», i.e. the plausible-benefit stratum (if any) is the young,
  not the AMD-risk elderly the retinal-protection claim targets. [@singh2023bluelight]
- **A regulator has already called the claim misleading.** In 2015 the UK Advertising Standards
  Authority found a retailer's promotion of lenses to «filter out harmful blue light» to be
  misleading advertising «in the absence of adequate substantiation» linking blue-light exposure to
  retinal damage. [@singh2023bluelight]

## Why some reviews say the opposite — a design-quality artifact, not a real tension

Two non-Cochrane reviews (Hester 2021; Shechter 2020) concluded blue-light lenses *can* improve sleep
in people with sleep/mood disorders. The Cochrane authors reconcile this by design quality: those
reviews were not prospectively registered, did not assess risk of bias, and admitted non-RCT designs
(single-arm, pre-/post-, case series). Restricting to RCTs collapses the positive signal to
indeterminate. This is *attention-is-an-anti-signal* at the review level — the weaker-design synthesis
manufactures the favourable finding; it is not a joined-issue tension the evidence leaves open.
[@singh2023bluelight]

## Decision relevance (Layer 1)

For a reasonably-healthy adult deciding whether to buy or upgrade to blue-light-filtering lenses for
eye strain, sleep, or eye protection: **on current gold evidence there is no patient-important
benefit to buy** — probably no effect on eye strain or visual acuity, and unknown (unstudied or
mixed) effects on sleep and macular health. The lever is near-zero *by construction*: the marketed
harm it counters (device blue light) is \~100-fold below the damage threshold and unlinked to AMD in
population studies. This is a **don't-buy-for-these-purposes** result, delivered as a finding — it
licenses spending nothing here and reallocating attention. The exposure is not unsafe, so the cost is
money and false reassurance, not health.

**Substitution frame for digital eye strain.** The realistic alternatives that *do* address computer
vision syndrome — regular screen breaks, correcting uncorrected refractive error, managing dry eye,
and ergonomic/lighting fixes — are the comparators; a tinted lens with no demonstrated effect
displaces attention from levers that work.

## Limits and open threads

- **Low/very-low certainty is mostly a design problem, not a settled null.** The visual outcomes lean
  *probably no effect*; the sleep and macular claims are *insufficient evidence* — the trials to
  settle them (RCTs in healthy adults, longer follow-up, a core outcome set, blinding-integrity
  checks, macular endpoints over years) have not been done. Do not overstate this as *proven
  ineffective*; state it as *no evidence of benefit*.
- **Stratum caveat — post-cataract-surgery (pseudophakic) eyes.** A UV-only intraocular lens lets more
  blue light through than an aged natural lens, so this is the one stratum where a protective rationale
  is not already neutralised by lens-yellowing; RCT evidence for benefit here is still lacking. `G`-gap. [@singh2023bluelight]
- **Two authors (SS, LED) co-authored an included trial (Singh 2021)**; the review had independent
  authors perform its risk-of-bias assessment to mitigate this. [@singh2023bluelight]
- **Sleep arm partly upgraded (2026-09).** The *objective* sleep arm is no longer bare-insufficient:
  Luna-Rangel 2025 supplies a small pooled crossover-RCT null on actigraphy (see the objective-sleep
  section). It remains underpowered (n=49), so a **larger well-powered RCT/SR in healthy adults** — and
  a trial *reporting melanopic dose at the eye* — would still move it, as would positive
  effect-modification evidence for the circadian-misalignment subgroup. The visual/macular arms remain
  single-source (Singh) and unconfirmed.
