**Now anchored on the primary harmonised meta-analyses, not the WHO summary `[2026-08-06]`.** The
device-measured dose maxima this page carries were previously held *via* the WHO 2020 evidence-profiles
annex — but that annex is a **recommendation summary that borrowed its evidence** from the underlying
harmonised meta-analysis, **Ekelund 2019** (accelerometry). Per the *cite-the-underlying-SR-for-an-
effect-claim* rule, the effect/certainty claims are re-anchored below on the primary source; the WHO
quotes are retained as the summary that reproduced them. Two further primaries are now held: **Ekelund
2016** (sitting x PA interaction) and **Paluch 2022** (steps/day). All three are gold harmonised MAs of
prospective cohorts — objective/device or harmonised self-report — the objective-measurement corrective
to the older self-report dose-response literature.

**These three are not independent replications of each other.** Ulf Ekelund is a named co-author on all
three, and they re-slice overlapping cohort pools (the shared accelerometry/harmonised consortium —
NHANES, the Women's Health Study, and others recur across them). So their agreement on curve *shape* is
a set of convergent framings of one evidence base, not three separate confirmations — which is why no
`[E-independent]` token is claimed on it (the same-quantity check returns NO; the convergence is
qualitative). The measurement reading in the next paragraph does not close that gap either.


**A fourth consortium paper does not change that `[2026-10-28, Ekelund 2026]`.** Ekelund 2026 (IPD-MA,
seven hip/lower-back accelerometry cohorts, n=40 327, 4895 deaths; UK Biobank wrist data analysed
separately, n=94 719, 3487 deaths) shares eleven authors with Ekelund 2019 and five of its seven cohorts
(ABC, NHANES, NNPAS, REGARDS, WHS) with the 2019 and 2020 pools; only HAI and Tromso are new. Its
agreement with the 2019 curve is therefore a type-F re-analysis of a largely shared data base by largely
the same team, never type E. UK Biobank is the one non-consortium dataset, analysed by the same team.
[inferred from @ekelund2026small]

**The independence gap remains open.** A genuinely independent check was hoped for from
[[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]] (Momma 2022) and is NOT one (same observational
PA-epidemiology lineage, overlapping cohorts). The *measurement-artifact reading* rests mainly on
within-lineage evidence: Ekelund's accelerometry finds the activity-mortality effect is **\~2x larger** than
self-report shows, a comparison against earlier self-report studies made in one of the three base MAs
[@ekelundacc2019].
Mandsager's exercise-treadmill-testing study (estimated METs), from a non-overlapping group, finds objectively-measured
*fitness* with **no upper limit of benefit observed** in one referral cohort (elite vs high HR 0.77,
0.63-0.95, significant overall and in the 70-plus and hypertensive subgroups; no significant elite-vs-high
difference in younger patients; non-significant by sex and in the other with-comorbidity subgroups)
[@mandsager2018] -> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]. Its bands are age- and
sex-relative percentiles: at age 70+, where the step was significant, elite starts at 8.4-11.5 METs
depending on age band and sex [@mandsager2018]. Mandsager calls its result «notably discrepant
from population-based studies» of physical activity and exercise, and its first candidate explanation is «the objective
measurement of physical fitness in the present study, as opposed to self-reported activity levels»
[@mandsager2018]. That explanation is weakened, not refuted, by Ekelund's device-measured curve,
which also plateaus (low light intensity excepted).
Ross's AHA statement places most of the CRF-mortality gradient by \~10-12 METs, with >12 METs having «a
relatively lower impact» [@ross2016], but that sentence has no citation of its own; the only citation in the three sentences ending at ref 290 (on the closing
sentence) is Arem 2015, titled as a pooled dose-response analysis of leisure time physical activity and mortality [@ross2016]; by its title an activity study rather than a fitness study. In chunk 01 Ross writes that many studies find «CRF levels >8 to 10 METs are associated with relative protection», and that «Relatively less is gained by increasing CRF between moderately fit and highly fit individuals.», stated alongside its Figure, modified from Blair 1989, a study in which fitness was estimated by maximal treadmill testing [@ross2016]; the references cited for the >8-10 MET sentence include Kodama 2009 and Myers 2002 [@ross2016], by title mostly fitness/exercise-capacity studies.

1989, treadmill-estimated fitness) adds that less is gained beyond moderate fitness, and its 10-12 MET sentence is itself uncited.
The Mandsager leg is consistent-with the measurement reading, not an independent confirmation: fitness !=
activity, its top-band step is significant overall but by age only in the 70+ subgroup, and objective
measurement is only one of Mandsager's three candidate explanations (genetic/unmeasured-habit contributors
and population differences are the others). Not a banked type-E (corrected 2026-10-22: previously *a
genuine `[E-independent]` corroboration*). An RCT-grade mortality outcome is still owed.

## The decision this page changes

**Most of the mortality association is reached at a dose far below what people assume they need — and the
curve then flattens.** That is a decision-change in the telos's specific sense: it licenses someone
to *stop optimizing*, which is a result, not a failure to find one. Above \~24 min/day of device MVPA
(studied to \~50 min/day), each further 5 min is associated, if causal, with a small reduction not clearly different from none
(see *The plateau region* below): a small, uncertain lever, so stopping there is defensible, and more
is not shown to be worthless. [INFERRED; figure-read]

**But the *outcome* of that activity — cardiorespiratory fitness — is a strong mortality predictor, with no
upper limit observed in one referral cohort**: «Importantly, there was no upper limit of benefit of increased
aerobic fitness.» «The adjusted mortality risk of reduced performance on ETT was
comparable to, if not greater than, traditional clinical risk factors (eg, CAD, smoking).»
[@mandsager2018] -> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]. The two are not one claim:
activity *dose* flattens early here (on the device-measured curve too), while objectively-measured
*fitness* showed a significant step at the top in one referral cohort (elite vs high HR 0.77, 0.63-0.95)
[@mandsager2018] — a discrepancy worth keeping open, not proof that more activity is always
better.

**This page is about total DOSE (how much); a sibling decision governs its DISTRIBUTION across the
week.** Once a weekly volume is fixed, whether it is concentrated into 1-2 sessions (the weekend-warrior
pattern) or spread across the week appears not to change the mortality benefit
-> [[Weekend Warrior Activity Pattern and Mortality]] (with a session-duration floor: sub-30-min
sessions do not bank it, at low observational certainty).

## Scope — this page is LEISURE / TOTAL PA; occupational PA can run the OTHER way `[2026-08-14, Coenen]`

**Every estimate on this page is leisure-time or total physical activity.** The «more is better»
reading does **not** transfer to activity performed *at work*: Coenen 2018 (gold SR+MA, 17 studies,
193,696 participants) finds high *occupational* PA associated with **higher** all-cause mortality in
men (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.05-1.34), while women show a non-significant inverse tendency (0.90, 0.80-1.01)
[@coenen2018paradox]
-> [[The Physical Activity Paradox]]. This is a **domain disambiguation, not a contradiction**: leisure
PA (short intense bouts, recovery) and occupational PA (≥40 h/week, static/repetitive, no recovery) are
different exposures under one word, and a same-quantity check returns NO on exposure, pattern,
measurement, contrast, and direction. The decision-change for a reader: **do not count physically
demanding work as the exercise this page evidences** — meeting a step/MVPA target *via the job* may not
bank the mortality benefit and, in men, may carry cardiovascular risk. The causal reading is
unadjudicated (observational, self-reported exposure, healthy-worker selection), so this is a
stratum-level caveat on transferability, not a claim that work activity should be reduced.


### Active commuting — a third route to the benefit, and the intensity refinement `[2026-08-19, Celis-Morales]`

**A structural PA lever that corroborates the more-PA-lower-mortality reading from a route neither
leisure nor occupational.** Celis-Morales 2017 (UK Biobank, 263,540 workers, maximally adjusted incl.
occupational PA and some leisure PA) finds cycle-commuting associated with **HR 0.59 (0.42-0.83)** for
all-cause mortality, 0.55 (0.44-0.69) cancer incidence, 0.60 (0.40-0.90) cancer mortality, vs
non-active commuting
[@celismorales2017commuting].
This is a **refinement (type-F), not independent corroboration (type-E)**: UK Biobank
overlaps the accelerometry/harmonised cohorts this page already rests on, so it is the *same
observational PA-epidemiology lineage*, not a separate route — it bounds and extends the leisure-PA
reading, it does not raise its confidence as an independent instrument would.

**The refinement: intensity separates the modes, on exactly the CRF axis.** Cycling benefits all five
outcomes with a dose-response by weekly distance; walking commuting is null for all-cause and cancer
mortality but lowers CVD incidence (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54-0.99) and CVD mortality (HR 0.64, 0.45-0.91)
overall; within its weekly-distance gradient the lower CVD incidence is evident only among walkers
covering «more than six miles a week» (corrected 2026-10-06: *lowers CVD risk only above six miles a
week* -> overall CVD benefit, with the six-mile qualifier scoped to the distance gradient,
Celis-Morales 2017 chunk 01)
[@celismorales2017commuting]. The
authors attribute the gap to intensity — «approximately 90% of cycle commuters ... achieved current
physical activity guidelines, only 54% of walk- ing commuters» did
[@celismorales2017commuting]. So the
mode that reaches a CRF-improving stimulus banks the broad benefit; the sub-threshold mode does not —
consistent with the objectively-measured *fitness* signal above -> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]
and with the domain split on [[The Physical Activity Paradox]].

**Decision-change (structural leverage):** commuting active — especially cycling — is a way to bank
the mortality benefit *built into the day*, so adherence is high; but treat walking-commuting as a
lower-intensity dose that may not reach the plateau this page's leisure estimates describe. **Caveat:**
observational, self-reported mode, and cyclists were leaner and fitter than non-active commuters at
the same baseline visit (BMI 25.22 vs 27.51; fitness, measured in a subsample, 11.79 vs 9.32 METs)
but not wealthier: by area deprivation more of them sat in the most-deprived fifth (29.2% vs 17.2%)
and fewer in the least-deprived (11.9% vs 21.2%), while household income was similar (>£100,000:
8.6% vs 7.0%; <£18,000: 10.6% vs 9.8%)
[@celismorales2017commuting].
Leanness and fitness may partly be consequences of cycling rather than markers of who chose it, so
healthy-user selection is possible but not shown — a robust association, not a proven causal offset
-> [[Upgrading Observational Evidence]].
(corrected 2026-10-22: *markedly leaner/fitter/wealthier* -> leaner/fitter, not wealthier, per Table 1)
[inferred from @celismorales2017commuting]

*Self-critique `[run 2026-08-19]`:* the commuting arm is classed **type-F, not `[E-independent]`** —
UK Biobank shares the observational PA-epidemiology lineage this page rests on, so it does not add an
independent instrument and no `[E-independent]` token is claimed. The benefit is stated as an
*association* with the healthy-user caveat explicit (cyclists leaner/fitter at baseline; corrected
2026-10-22: not wealthier: more often in the most-deprived area fifth, similar household income,
Table 1);
walking-commuting is flagged as a sub-threshold dose, not folded into the plateau. No overclaim: the
HRs stay Celis-Morales's, the third-route framing is.

## Where the curve bends — named maxima, device-measured

> «Maximal risk reductions for light intensity PA was \~375 min/day, low light intensity PA at \~325
> min/day, high light intensity PA at \~80 min/day, and MVPA at \~24 min/day.»
> [@who2020]

> «The maximal risk reduction for total PA was observed at about 300 cpm (adjusted HR = 0.34 [95% CI
&gt; 0.27 to 0.43]).»
> [@who2020]

**Certainty: HIGH** (WHO's grade for the association and its non-linear shape; where each maximum sits
depends on the device intensity cut-point, see *The minute is defined by the cut-point* below).
The underlying profile is a harmonized meta-analysis of eight prospective
cohorts in which **all eight used accelerometers** rather than self-report, and *«A non-linear,
dose-response association was found between all exposure variables and mortality (p<0.02 for all
exposure variables)»*. A second profile reports *«A curvilinear relationship was found between total
PA and all-cause mortality (p non-linearity <0.001).»*
[@who2020]

| Exposure | Dose at maximal risk reduction |
|---|---|
| **MVPA** (moderate-to-vigorous) | **\~24 min/day** (ActiGraph >=1952 cpm, Actical >1535 cpm; mostly hip-worn; definition-dependent) |
| High light intensity | \~80 min/day |
| Low light intensity | \~325 min/day |
| Light intensity (total) | \~375 min/day |
| Total PA | \~300 cpm — **HR 0.34 (0.27-0.43)** |

The MVPA row's cut-points (ActiGraph >=1952 cpm, Actical >1535 cpm) and wear sites (hip in all cohorts
but ABC, lower back) are from the underlying meta-analysis, not the WHO annex
[@ekelundacc2019] (Methods + Table 1).

**Read the table as substitution, not as a target menu.** These are *different routes to the same
maximum*, so the operative reading is: roughly 24 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity, **or**
a much larger volume of light activity, reaches most of the available benefit. WHO states the
intensity-independence directly:

> «Any physical activity, regardless of intensity, was associated with lower risk of mortality, with
> a non-linear dose-response.»
> [@who2020]

**Two cautions on the HR 0.34.** It is a *between-quartile* contrast in observational cohorts, so
reverse causation (illness reduces activity) is not excluded by design — the profile's HIGH rating
reflects consistency and precision, not randomization. And the exposure is total accelerometer
counts, which is not a prescription anyone can follow directly.

**And the dose points are regions on a sparse, age-bounded top — not a target menu.** Two caveats the
table drops belong at the point of display. (i) *Studied range:* Ekelund's cohorts «included middle
aged and older adults who were at least 40 years old; it is unclear whether the estimates of the
absolute physical activity levels associated with maximal risk reduction apply to younger people»
[@ekelundacc2019] — the maxima are not
scoped to under-40s. Ekelund 2026 (mean age 63.9 y across all 135 046 participants, UK Biobank included;
under-40s excluded from three cohorts) adds that for device MVPA increments the under-60 stratum is thin too: «there was little evidence
for any meaningful association between increases in MVPA and the risk of mortality in those younger than
60 years; however, statistical power was low because of small numbers (five studies; n=6479; 257
deaths).» [@ekelund2026small] The
next sentence but one covers the wrist cohort: «We repeated the sensitivity analyses in the UK Biobank Study (sex and age stratified; appendix pp 30–31) and without adjustment for BMI, excluding those with mobility limitations and those with chronic diseases (appendix p 32), and the results were materially unchanged.»
[@ekelund2026small] UK Biobank has
about 35 000 under-60s (aged >=60: 60.3% of 53 449 women, 66.2% of 41 270 men, cohort table)
[@ekelund2026small], but the stratified figures are in the appendix (not held) and the cohort
carries healthy-volunteer selection. The Methods define the age strata and include UK Biobank in the
sensitivity analyses: «we stratified our sample by sex, age (<60 years vs ≥60 years), and geographical
location (USA vs Scandinavia) in sensitivity analyses.»
[@ekelund2026small] So the hip-device
consortium alone is the insufficient-evidence state for under-60s on this measure, not a shown null. UK
Biobank's <60 / >=60 stratified results are reported only as «materially unchanged» (one phrase covering
four analyses; appendix not held, no figures in the text): under-60 support from the wrist cohort is thin
and unquantified, not absent. Paluch's under-60 steps curve (below)
does show falling risk to 8000-10 000 steps/day. (ii) *The upper arm is thin:* each dose maximum carries its own wide interval,
the MVPA point most of all («wide confidence intervals were observed at this part of the dose-response
curve» because few participants reach high MVPA), and the \~300 cpm total-PA maximum «mirrors the
median total physical activity for the third quarter»
[@ekelundacc2019] — i.e. the apparent
optimum sits where the data are densest, the *apparent-optimum-equals-sampling-edge* hazard, so read
the dose column as regions on a flat top, not point targets -> [[The Underivable Optimum]].

## The very-high-volume tail in the general population — a smaller benefit, not a reversal `[2026-10-21, Franklin]`

The maxima above say where the benefit flattens. A separate question is whether it *turns back* at
volumes far above guidance. An AHA scientific statement (consensus; no stated search) reviews the
cohorts that reach that far. Arem:
«Maximal mortality risk reduction (HR, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.59−0.62]) occurred at an exercise volume
equaling 3 to 5 times the recommendations. Although the most active indi- viduals (≥10 times the
recommendations) had a lower mortality risk (HR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.59−0.78]) compared with inactive
control subjects, their risk reduction was not as large as the group with optimal exercise volume.»
The statement attributes the wide intervals in the top groups to their size: they «may be explained by
the relatively small sample sizes of the most active sub- groups (typically <5% of the total cohort).»
Its verdict: «In aggregate, there is currently no evidence to reject the curvilinear association
(Figure 8A) between exercise volumes and cardiovascular health outcomes, with AF as a possible
exception.» [@franklin2020acute]

- **AF is the outcome where the shape may differ.** A nonlinear meta-regression (Ricci; 19 studies)
  found a J-shape: 5-20 MET-h/wk lower AF risk, 20-55 MET-h/wk similar to inactive, and a trend to higher
  risk above 55 MET-h/wk (>9.5 h of vigorous exercise per week). In the Physicians' Health Study, men who
  jogged 5 to 7 times per week had a 50% higher risk of AF than men who did not exercise vigorously, after
  adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors.
  [@franklin2020acute] Shape
  is outcome-specific here as elsewhere, and the AF signal starts at volumes a committed recreational
  runner can reach.
- **Coronary calcium at high volume is a surrogate, not an outcome.** In the Cooper Clinic cohort
  (n=21,758 men), the 432 doing ≥3000 MET-min/wk had a higher adjusted risk of CAC ≥100 but no higher
  all-cause or CV mortality than men exercising at more moderate levels: «These findings
  refute the notion that high-volume endurance activity (>1 h/d) increases mortality risk, regardless of
  CAC level.» [@franklin2020acute]
- **Cardiac patients are carved out.** The same paragraph that rejects a U for the general population
  adds: «These find- ings might not be applicable to cardiac patients, because high-intensity, high-volume
  PA may increase all-cause mortality among these patients.» Among CHD patients, Mons found the lowest
  mortality at 2-4 vigorous sessions/week and HR 1.77 (0.90−3.47) at 7 sessions/week; Stewart (n=15,486) found a graded
  reduction instead, and the statement calls the findings conflicting.
  [@franklin2020acute]
- **Reading the Arem pair:** the intervals overlap (0.59−0.62 vs 0.59−0.78), so the smaller
  benefit at ≥10x is a point-estimate difference, not a demonstrated one. Other top-group estimates the
  statement tabulates point the same way (Lear 0.98 for the most active vs 0.89 optimal; Schnohr 1.97 with
  a 0.48−8.14 interval).
- **Decision consequence:** for people without heart disease the relevant tail question is the
  *transient* risk of a bout, not a long-run harmful arm — that lives on
  [[Exercise-Related Acute Cardiac Events]]. The adjudication of the extreme-exercise upper arm as a
  candidate artifact is catalogued on [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]. These are secondary
  citations through one statement, not primaries held here, so they bound the shape claim without
  raising this page's `confidence:`.

## The primary numbers (Ekelund 2019) — the full dose-response, and where the benefit is banked

The two maxima above trace to **Ekelund 2019**, a harmonised MA of 8 accelerometry cohorts (n=36 383,
2149 deaths, model B, least-active referent). The full quartile curve makes the *shape* legible:

| Exposure | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 (most active) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Total PA** (cpm) | 1.00 | **0.48** (0.43-0.54) | 0.34 (0.26-0.45) | 0.27 (0.23-0.32) |
| **Light PA** (min/d) | 1.00 | 0.60 (0.54-0.68) | 0.44 (0.38-0.51) | 0.38 (0.28-0.51) |
| **MVPA** (min/d) | 1.00 | 0.64 (0.55-0.74) | 0.55 (0.40-0.74) | 0.52 (0.43-0.61) |

> «Any physical activity, regardless of intensity, was associated with lower risk of mortality, with a
> non-linear dose-response. Hazards ratios for mortality were 1.00 (referent) in the first quarter (least
> active), 0.48 (95% confidence interval 0.43 to 0.54) in the second quarter, 0.34 (0.26 to 0.45) in the
> third quarter, and 0.27 (0.23 to 0.32) in the fourth quarter (most active).»
> [@ekelundacc2019]

**The single most decision-relevant feature: the steepest drop is the FIRST step off the floor.** Moving
from the least-active to the second quarter of *total* activity goes with roughly *half* the mortality
hazard (HR 0.48). The small increment Ekelund reports belongs to the *intensity* quarters, not to that HR:
the Q1->Q2 gaps were «broadly equal to 60 min/day of light intensity physical activity» and «5 min/day of
moderate-to- vigorous intensity physical activity», and the Q2 hazards in those quarters were 0.60 (light)
and 0.64 (MVPA). [@ekelundacc2019]
So about five more minutes a day of MVPA, or about an hour of light activity, marks a hazard roughly
35-40% lower; the halving is the total-activity step, which bundles both.

**The same step, re-read by starting level `[2026-10-28, Ekelund 2026]`.** The 35-40% above is a
between-quarter contrast (people in Q2 vs people in Q1). Ekelund 2026 re-estimates the consortium's MVPA
curve (a new 4-knot spline on seven cohorts, five shared with 2019) as the hazard change for a hypothetical increment *from each observed level* (fully adjusted, deaths
in the first 2 years excluded):

> «(figure 1A) was associated with an approximately 30% lower mortality risk and a 10-min/day increase from
&gt; 1 min/day to 11 min/day (figure 1B) with an approximately 42% lower mortality risk. We found diminishing
> returns on increasing MVPA at higher levels—eg, increasing MVPA from 9 min/day to 14 min/day was
> associated with an approximately 18% lower mortality risk and from 9 min/day to 19 min/day with an
> approximately 28% lower mortality risk.»
> [@ekelund2026small]

The opening clause is the 5-min increase *from 1 to 6 min/day*. The text prints no CI for these HRs
(Figure 1A shows them graphically).

| Parameter | Ekelund 2019 (Q1->Q2, MVPA) | Ekelund 2026 (increment, MVPA) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contrast | people in Q2 vs people in Q1 | +5 or +10 min from a stated starting level | **NO** — group contrast vs within-spline increment |
| Size of step | Q1->Q2 gap \~5 min/day | +5 min (1->6, 9->14); +10 min (1->11, 9->19) | roughly matched at the bottom only |
| Hazard | HR 0.64 (0.55-0.74) | \~30% (1->6), \~18% (9->14) lower; no CI in text | no |
| Cohorts | 8 consortium cohorts | 7 (5 shared) | largely shared — type F |

So the incumbent line survives as a description of the Q1->Q2 contrast, and the 2026 display sharpens it:
the \~30% figure is the source's example at about 1 min/day, the same 5 minutes from 9 min/day is
associated with about 18% lower hazard, and the gain per added minute keeps shrinking up to \~24 min/day. *Five minutes marks a
35-40% lower hazard* should not be read as a fixed return for any starting point.
[inferred from @ekelund2026small]

**Absolute layer (crude counts, not effects).** Ekelund's Table 2 (model B) counts 1187 deaths among 9096
people in the least-active total-activity quarter and 483 among 9105 in the second, over a median 5.8
years of follow-up (about 13.0% vs 5.3%). [@ekelundacc2019]
Paluch computes risk differences from summed deaths «as com­ parison quartile minus reference quartile»:
-65 deaths per 1000 people (-72 to -58) over a median 7.1 years of follow-up, for the second step quartile (median 5801 steps/day) against the
first (3553). [@paluch2022]
Both figures are unadjusted. The least active tend to be older (in Paluch, adults >=60 took a median 5649
steps/day against 7803 under 60 [@paluch2022]) and plausibly sicker, so the gap mixes the activity effect
with confounding and reverse causation and overstates what adding activity would buy someone in that
stratum; the adjusted HRs are the better estimate of the relative effect, if causal, applied to the
stratum's baseline risk (route a), not an individual effect.

> «the greatest risk reduction for mortality was observed when the second quarter was compared with the
> referent, for all activity intensities.»
> [@ekelundacc2019]
This is the *marginal-minute-worth-most-at-the-bottom* shape, quantified: the near-sedentary person has
the largest lever on the activity curve, and it is a small one to pull, if the bottom-of-curve
association is causal; this is also the region most exposed to reverse causation (see below).

**The plateau region, stated primarily — located, not a *no knee*, but not precisely bounded.** Above
the maxima the curve flattens:
> «No further risk reductions occurred with higher levels of activity except for low light intensity
> physical activity where the risk appeared to decrease further.»
> [@ekelundacc2019]
So this is a located flattening: monotone-decreasing, then no further reduction detected above the
maxima (no U/J, no harmful upper arm detected for all-cause mortality over the studied range). The MVPA
maximum itself is HR 0.39 (0.26-0.59) at \~24 min/day
[@ekelundacc2019], an interval that leaves
the curve's level at its maximum imprecise. It largely survives the *measurement* caveat the wiki attaches to plateaus: measurement error can hide a
knee, and where it grows with activity level it can also manufacture a plateau; here the plateau is on the
device-measured curve, where that error is smaller than in self-report — so it is more credible than a
self-report plateau, though not immune to it (corrected 2026-10-22: previously *error can hide a
knee but not manufacture one*). The one qualifier that
cuts the other way: the 2019 curve's high-dose arm rests on **few participants with wide CIs** (the maxima
caveat above), so it detects no further reduction above the maxima, which is weaker than an
established plateau, and does not pin where the flattening starts. Ekelund 2026
re-estimates the curve on a partly shared pool (five of seven cohorts shared, so type F, not a replication)
and reports no clear risk reduction for a +5 min increment beyond \~24 min/day: «Beyond approximately 24 min/day, no clear risk reduction was evident (figure
1A).» [@ekelund2026small] Its MVPA
minutes are defined by a hip-accelerometer cut-point of \~2000 counts/min (consortium mean 27.7 min/day),
so \~24 min is specific to that cut-point. *No clear reduction* is a per-increment reading, and the
text prints no numbers or CI for it; Figure 1A plots them. Read off figure 1A
(approximate), each further +5 min/day from \~25 to 49 min/day sits at roughly HR 0.97-0.99, with
intervals of about 0.94 to 1.01; Figure 1B's +10 min/day increments there read (also approximate) roughly 0.95-0.98
(intervals about 0.89 to 1.01). So, if causal, each further increment above \~24-25 min/day is associated
with a small reduction, not clearly different from none: a small, uncertain lever, neither a shown zero
nor a shown large gain. The 2019 curve's «wide confidence intervals» caveat
[@ekelundacc2019] describes that curve's
level at high MVPA, not these 2026 per-increment intervals. In 2026, participants are not sparse above
\~24 min/day (the figure's 2-min distribution bars put \~27.5% between 24 and 50 min/day and sum to
only 82.9%, implying \~17% above the 50-min axis end, so \~45% exceed \~24 min/day), but precision is set by deaths, which the main text does not break
down by MVPA level and which plausibly concentrate among the less active, older participants. See the
deaths-averted section below. [INFERRED; figure-read from Ekelund 2026 Figure 1A/1B, PDF p5]
(corrected 2026-10-28: an intermediate wording called the size of any gain above this point *unknown,
not estimated in the held text*; Figure 1A estimates it.)
Cross-check: objectively-measured *fitness*
showed no upper limit of benefit in one referral cohort (Mandsager, in age/sex-relative percentiles)
-> [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]. Fitness and activity dose are not the same construct, and
Ross reports that many studies find relative protection at >8-10 METs and less gain between moderate and
high fitness [@ross2016], while Mandsager saw no upper limit in one referral cohort; a precise
flattening point for measured fitness is not pinned by the held sources
(corrected 2026-10-22: previously *shows NO plateau*).

**Why the effect is this large — the measurement corrective (the type-A payoff of holding the primary).**
> «The observed effect sizes for the associations between physical activity and the risk of death are
> about twice as large compared with those previously reported in studies assessing physical activity by
> self report»
> [@ekelundacc2019]
Self-report attenuates the gradient toward the null; objective accelerometry roughly **doubles** the
observed magnitude and resolves the light-intensity shape self-report could not. This is the
[[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]] binding-constraint lesson firing in the *activity* domain,
and the reason a device-measured HR is not comparable to a self-report HR for the same behaviour.
*Qualified 2026-10-28:* less error is not the only reading of the doubling. A device also records
incidental movement self-report misses (a partly different exposure), and Ekelund 2026's 15-20%
attenuation after excluding mobility limitations (in the cohorts with mobility data; UK Biobank alone
materially unchanged) is consistent with part of the device gradient tracking frailty, i.e. reverse
causation the device captures. The only figures the main text shows for that attenuation are PIFs (the
NHANES worked example); a PIF can fall from dropping high-mortality, low-activity people even if the HRs
barely move, and the main text gives no HR for the mobility-excluded analyses (appendix pp 33-38 not
held). The authors read the same check the other way, as reducing the likelihood of that bias. The held sources do not split the doubling among these three.
[inferred from @ekelund2026small]

**Reverse causation (the frail move less) — handled by the WEAK check only.**
> «We attempted to minimise bias from reverse causation by excluding all deaths within the first two years
> in sensitivity analyses. The hazard ratios were materially unchanged for the associations of total
> physical activity and light intensity or moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity with
> mortality, and slightly attenuated for sedentary time.»
> [@ekelundacc2019]
The PA associations survived early-death exclusion (Ekelund adds «bias from reverse causation might
persist»); there is no MR/genetic instrument, so this is the *weak* adjudication in the
[[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]] sense — enough that the monotone benefit is not purely sick-quitter
artifact, not enough to call it causal. Consistent across the three earlier sources here (see the steps and
sitting sections).

**Ekelund 2026 moves the 2-year exclusion into the main model and adds one more weak check
`[2026-10-28]`.** Deaths in the first 2 years were excluded before analysis (556 consortium, 557 UK
Biobank). Excluding people with self-reported mobility limitations, possible only in NHANES, Tromso and
UK Biobank, attenuated the results (NHANES is the worked example; for UK Biobank alone the Results call
the mobility-excluded results «materially unchanged»):

> «The results were somewhat attenuated (around 15–20% lower) in sensitivity analyses when excluding
> individuals with mobility limitations. Combined with our exclusion of deaths within the first 2 years of
> follow-up, this finding reduces the likelihood of bias from reverse causation. However, as in any
> observational research we cannot fully rule out the possibility that our results reflect some bias
> from residual or unmeasured confounding and reverse causation.»
> [@ekelund2026small]

In NHANES the high-risk +5 min PIF fell by 1.5 points to 7.3% (5.0-9.3) and the population PIF by 2.0
points to 11.5% (5.4-16.7).
[@ekelund2026small] No longer lag and no genetic instrument is reported in the main text [searched: lag / landmark / Mendelian / genetic across chunk 01, 0 hits; appendix not held]. A 15-20% attenuation from removing one marker of
frailty, in the cohorts that recorded it, is consistent with *some* reverse causation in the adjusted
main curve; its only shown figures are PIFs (no HR for these analyses in the main text), so its size is
not a measure of how much the HR gradient moved. The check is still the weak kind.

## Deaths potentially averted by small changes — a model output, not a trial effect `[2026-10-28, Ekelund 2026]`

**What was computed.** Ekelund 2026 turns the consortium's adjusted spline HRs into potential impact
fractions (PIFs): the share of all deaths during follow-up that «could be prevented by changing the
exposure level». The HR is taken for an increment from each observed level, and cumulative PIFs are
then computed for two groups modelled on Rose's high-risk vs population strategies (the method is in
appendix pp 11-12, not held). The **high-risk** group is the \~20% least
active (mean MVPA 2.2 min/day); for sitting it is the \~20% most sedentary (mean 721 min/day). The
**population** group is everyone except the \~20% most active (mean MVPA 17.4 min/day); for sitting,
everyone except the \~20% least sedentary (mean 605 min/day). Cohorts: seven, n=40 327, 4895 deaths, mean
age 63.9 y, mean follow-up 8.2 y across all 135 046 participants including UK Biobank.
[@ekelund2026small]

| Scenario (per day) | Consortium, high-risk | Consortium, population | UK Biobank, high-risk | UK Biobank, population |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MVPA +5 min | 6.0% (4.3-7.4) | 10.0% (6.3-13.4) | 2.2% (1.6-2.7) | 3.6% (2.4-4.8) |
| MVPA +10 min | 8.8% (7.0-10.4) | 14.9% (9.7-19.3) | 3.9% (3.0-4.7) | 6.2% (4.0-8.2) |
| Light PA +60 min | 5.5% (4.0-6.7) | 8.9% (3.1-13.8) | 4.7% (3.9-5.3) | 6.0% (2.7-9.2) |
| Sedentary -30 min | 3.0% (2.0-4.1) | 7.3% (4.8-9.6) | 2.5% (2.1-2.9) | 4.5% (2.8-6.1) |
| Sedentary -60 min | 5.5% (3.9-6.9) | 12.6% (8.4-16.4) | 4.6% (3.8-5.3) | 7.6% (4.4-10.6) |

[@ekelund2026small]
Cells are cumulative PIF, % of all deaths during follow-up (95% CI), checked against the rendered table.

For contrast, the classical PAF: «21·5% (95% CI 18·7–24·0) of all deaths could hypothetically be
prevented if all participants spent at least 22 min/day in MVPA» (the WHO 150 min/week floor); «The
corresponding PAF from the UK Biobank Study was 9·2% (7·4–10·9).»
[@ekelund2026small]

**Every estimate is a substitution.** «Because our models included adjustment for wear time, the
estimated association for higher MVPA reflects both the effect of higher MVPA and the effect of some
combination of lower sedentary time and light activity. Similarly, associations for lower sedentary time
reflect associations for increases in both light physical activity and MVPA.»
[@ekelund2026small] So *+5 min MVPA*
means 5 minutes moved out of sitting or light activity, and *-30 min sitting* means 30 minutes moved
into some light activity or MVPA.

**What the numbers assume.** A PIF is arithmetic on observational HRs, so the *deaths averted* are only
as causal as the HRs. The authors name two of the gaps here (a third, generalisability, under
*Transport* below): «we cannot fully rule out the
possibility that our results reflect some bias from residual or unmeasured confounding and reverse
causation. Exposure variables were only measured at baseline; thus, MVPA might not reflect sustained
activity levels over time, weakening the consistency assumption and potentially affecting the
associations.» [@ekelund2026small]
The paper does not say in words that a PIF assumes causality (the one hit for *causal* is inside
*reverse causality*) [searched: causal across chunk 01]. Read in full, a PIF needs four things: (i) the HR is
causal; (ii) an induced 5 minutes acts like being, habitually, a person who does 5 more (consistency);
(iii) the change is kept up for the whole follow-up; (iv) the cohorts' activity distribution and death
rates match the target population. No held trial tests (i)-(iii), and the 15-20% mobility-limitation
attenuation above is consistent with (i) not being fully met. Read the table as *might prevent*, which is the
authors' wording in the Summary, Discussion and the Conclusion's MVPA sentence (the *if causal* is the
wiki's gloss), though the Results write *would prevent* and the Conclusion's sitting sentence
*prevented*. [inferred from @ekelund2026small]

**The minute is defined by the cut-point.** «Unfortunately, there is no consensus on how to define MVPA
from hip accelerometery. Our definition of MVPA (approximately 2000 counts per minute; CPM) corresponds to
walking equivalent to around 4–5 km/h (appendix pp 6–7), which is probably the most common type of
moderate intensity physical activity in the general population.» An earlier NHANES analysis (not held)
used a definition giving 3.5 times more MVPA, and in this dataset «A direct comparison between the two
definitions of MVPA in our dataset (appendix pp 41–42) yielded even larger differences in the estimated
percentage of averted deaths, suggesting that results can vary depending on the definition used for
MVPA.» [@ekelund2026small]
Devices: ActiGraph 7164 / GT1M / GT3X+ / wGT3X-BT on the right hip (ABC: lower back), Actical in REGARDS
(MVPA analysis only, so the sitting results pool six cohorts); UK Biobank Axivity AX3 on the wrist with
a machine-learning intensity classifier.
[@ekelund2026small]
A wrist-measured minute is not a hip-measured minute, so the UK Biobank column is a transport check, not
a replication.

**Transport.** UK Biobank associations and PIFs «were smaller in comparison», and the authors note that
«The UK Biobank Study is not representative of its source population, and there is evidence of healthy
volunteer selection bias». The analysed cohorts come from «Scandinavia, the UK, and the USA, limiting the
generalisability beyond these high-income populations.»
[@ekelund2026small]
The MVPA PIFs fall to about a third in UK Biobank (6.0% -> 2.2%, 10.0% -> 3.6%) while the light-activity
and sitting PIFs shrink much less, so the MVPA figures are the least portable across devices, processing
and selection.

**What this changes, for whom.**

- **Least-active stratum (bottom \~20%, \~2 min/day MVPA).** The high-risk column is the per-group
  arithmetic for the HR curve's steep bottom: +5 min/day in the least active 12.1% alone is associated
  with 3.6% (2.7-4.4) of deaths averted, against 1.4% (1.0-1.8) for the 6.0% at 2-4 min/day (the
  Discussion restates the first as 3.5%, 2.8-4.4). [@ekelund2026small]
  Per minute, the first few minutes of MVPA (walking at about 4-5 km/h on this cut-point) are the
  largest activity lever on the page. Per option they are not alone: «Finally, the estimated proportions of mortality preventable by 60-min/day increases in LPA and total physical activity were roughly similar to the estimates for 5-min increases in MVPA (table 2).»
  [@ekelund2026small] (high-risk
  column: light +60 min 5.5%, 4.0-6.7, vs MVPA +5 min 6.0%, 4.3-7.4), and the 2019 first-step reading
  put the largest step at the bottom for all intensities. All of this holds *if* the bottom-of-curve
  association is causal: this is the region most exposed to reverse causation, and excluding mobility
  limitations, where recorded (NHANES, Tromso, UK Biobank), lowered the results by about 15-20%, shown as PIFs (UK
  Biobank alone materially unchanged). Ekelund 2026 adds the population size of it, not a new
  direction. **Default, labelled:** if near-inactive, add about 5-10 minutes a day of walking at about
  4-5 km/h, or, where that pace is not feasible, about an hour a day more of light activity (similar
  modelled share, far more time). For older adults at high fall risk, brisk walking is not advised and
  walking is not a stand-alone fall-prevention prescription; balance and functional training is the
  falls lever (see the falls section: Sherrington 2017 on walking, the 2019 Cochrane review on balance
  training). Cheap and reversible; the benefit size is
  observational, if causal.
- **The population column is larger because more people move, not because each gains more.** It sums
  small per-person gains over \~80% of the cohort. A PIF is a share of a cohort's deaths, so it is not a
  person's expected risk reduction. A stratum-level estimate of what someone at a given starting level
  might expect comes from applying the starting-level HR above to that stratum's baseline risk (route
  a). The HR is the same observational input the PIF is computed from, so it carries the same *if
  causal* standing, and it is not an individual effect
  -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].
- **MVPA vs sitting: the authors rank MVPA first, their own table only partly agrees.** «in both the
  high-risk and the population-based approaches, the estimated number of preventable deaths appears
  greater with increasing MVPA by 5–10 min/day compared with 30-min and 60-min reductions in sedentary
  time.» [@ekelund2026small]
  In the consortium that holds pair by pair (+5 MVPA beats -30 sitting, +10 beats -60). In its
  high-risk column the paired intervals do not overlap (+5 MVPA 4.3-7.4 vs -30 sitting 2.0-4.1; +10
  MVPA 7.0-10.4 vs -60 sitting 3.9-6.9); in its population column they do, and -60 min sitting there
  (12.6%) exceeds +5 min MVPA (10.0%). In UK Biobank the point estimates run the other way, with every
  paired interval overlapping (+5 MVPA 2.2% / 3.6% below -30 sitting 2.5% / 4.5%; +10 MVPA 3.9% / 6.2%
  below -60 sitting 4.6% / 7.6%). The consortium's high-risk
  separation is therefore real on its own terms, but it does not carry to the transport cohort, the MVPA
  PIFs pool seven cohorts and the sitting PIFs six (REGARDS is MVPA-only), the high-risk groups are
  different people (least active vs most sedentary), and both arms are substitutions whose minutes differ
  in kind. So the table gives no firm ordering between the two levers that holds across both data sets;
  it says both small changes are associated with fewer deaths.
  [inferred from @ekelund2026small] The sitting side lives on
  [[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]].
- **Outcome and age limits.** All-cause mortality only; no function, QoL or trajectory outcome. Adults
  &gt;=40 in high-income settings (Scandinavia, UK, USA); the under-60 MVPA association was too
  under-powered to read in the consortium, and UK Biobank's age-stratified results are reported
  «materially unchanged» without figures in the main text (above).

## Steps per day — the practical, wearable-native dose (Paluch 2022)

**Steps are the metric people actually have.** Paluch 2022 (harmonised MA, 15 cohorts, n=47 471, 3013
deaths; quartile medians 3553 / 5801 / 7842 / 10 901 steps/day) gives the same shape in the unit a
fitness tracker reports:
> «Compared with the lowest quartile, the adjusted HR for all-cause mortality was 0·60 (95% CI 0·51–0·71)
> for quartile 2, 0·55 (0·49–0·62) for quartile 3, and 0·47 (0·39–0·57) for quartile 4. Restricted cubic
> splines showed progressively decreasing risk of mortality among adults aged 60 years and older with
> increasing number of steps per day until 6000–8000 steps per day and among adults younger than 60 years
> until 8000–10 000 steps per day.»
> [@paluch2022]

Quartiles 2-4 vs lowest = 40-53% lower mortality (highest vs lowest HR 0.47, about 53%); overall nadir \~7000-9000 steps/day; monotone-
decreasing to a plateau (p-nonlinearity <0.0001), same as the MVPA curve.

**The age plateau is genuine effect modification (route-b), not just baseline-risk (route-a).** The
age x steps interaction is significant (p=0.012): **older adults (>=60) reach the plateau LOWER
(\~6000-8000 steps) than younger adults (\~8000-10 000)** — the sex interaction is not (p=0.11). Because
this is *positive interaction evidence*, stratifying the step target by age clears the higher route-(b)
bar (per transportability and effect modification / the five-routes table), not only the route-(a)
absolute-benefit scaling. Decision-change: an older adult can bank the mortality plateau at a lower step
count than the number a younger adult should aim for.

**10 000 steps is a marketing number.**
> «Although the goal of 10 000 steps per day is widely promoted as being optimal for general health, it is
> not based on evidence, but instead originates from a marketing campaign in Japan.»
> [@paluch2022]
The benefit plateaus below 10 000 for most adults and well below it for older adults — so 10 000 is not a
threshold to clear, and treating it as one may discourage the person for whom 6000-8000 already banks
most of the benefit.

**Cadence adds little beyond volume.**
> «We found inconsistent evidence that step intensity had an association with mortality beyond total
> volume of steps.»
> [@paluch2022]
Peak-30/60-min stepping rate stayed significant after adjusting for volume, but time spent at a moderate
cadence did not — total steps carry the signal, so *count the steps, don't chase the pace*. Reverse
causation handled by the weak check (effect stronger at <6 y follow-up, HR 0.32, vs >=6 y, 0.57 — a
sick-quitter tell — but the 2-year exclusion left it significant).

**What an intervention buys on this curve is far smaller than a quartile.** Paluch's quartile medians
sit about 2000-3000 steps/day apart (3553 / 5801 / 7842 / 10 901). Two partly overlapping gold MAs of app
and tracker RCTs (GRADE low to low-to-moderate) put the induced gain over control at roughly +600-750 steps/day after a few months, with durability
unshown. The quartile HRs compare people who habitually differ in steps. They are not the effect of
adding steps to one person. So a device-driven gain cannot be read off the curve as a hazard ratio.
Even if the gradient were causal and applied to induced change (neither is shown, and the paragraph
above notes a sick-quitter tell), \~600-750 steps is roughly a fifth to a third of one inter-quartile
gap, held for a few months.
[inferred from @paluch2022; @brickwood2019wearable; @laranjo2020] -> [[Wearable Activity Trackers and Physical Activity]]

## These three metrics are NOT interchangeable numbers — a same-quantity check

Steps/day, MVPA-min/day and sitting-hours/day are **complementary framings of one construct (total
movement volume), not convertible doses.** The parameter table below is the guard against reading one
source's number as another's:

| Parameter | Ekelund 2019 (accel) | Paluch 2022 (steps) | Ekelund 2016 (sitting) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure unit | MVPA min/d; total-PA cpm | steps/day | sitting h/day; MET-h/week | **NO** — different units |
| Measurement | hip accelerometer (device) | step-counting device | self-report questionnaire | NO — device vs self-report |
| Referent | least-active quartile | lowest-steps quartile | least-sitting + most-active | NO — different referents |
| Where benefit banks | \~24 min/d MVPA (located flattening; ActiGraph >=1952 / Actical >1535 cpm, mostly hip) | \~7-9k steps (age-varying) | 60-75 min/d MVPA offsets sitting | measure a DIFFERENT feature |
| Top-vs-bottom effect | HR 0.27 (total PA) | HR 0.47 (steps) | HR 1.04 vs 1.27 (joint) | NO — non-comparable contrasts |

**The decision-relevant convergence is qualitative, not numeric:** all three show the same *shape* — a
steep early drop, then a plateau, on the objectively/harmonised-measured curve — but the HRs cannot be
equated (a device-measured total-PA Q4 HR of 0.27 is not *the same as* a steps Q4 HR of 0.47; they are
different exposures, referents and measurement instruments). Use steps for a wearable-native target, MVPA
minutes for a guideline-aligned target, and the sitting interaction for the offset question — do not
convert between their hazard ratios.
[inferred from @ekelundacc2019; @paluch2022; @ekelundsitting2016]

## Strength training — an association after adjustment for aerobic activity, never ranked above aerobic

| Finding | Effect | Certainty |
|---|---|---|
| Strength-guideline adherence vs not (Stamatakis 2018, 11 cohorts, N=80,306) | **HR 0.80** (0.70-0.91) | MODERATE |
| *Any* strength-promoting exercise vs none (same) | **HR 0.77** (0.69-0.87) | MODERATE |
| **Meeting both aerobic AND strengthening guidelines** vs neither | **HR 0.71** | MODERATE |

[@who2020]

**The RT dose is modest: lower risk appears at small weekly volumes, and no held source shows a further
all-cause gain at high volume.** Three RT→mortality MAs (pooling many of
these same cohorts — Stamatakis and Siahpush above are constituents) put the all-cause reduction around
−15% for *any* RT vs none, and Shailendra's minutes/week dose-response (4 studies) finds the «maximum risk
reduction of 26% observed at around 60 minutes per week of resistance training (RR=0.74; 95% CI=0.64,
0.86)» with benefit *diminishing* at higher volumes — so a modest weekly volume captures the held signal
and there is no mortality case for high volume. The evidence is observational/self-reported and the upper «U» arm rests on
4 studies (do not act on it). Full appraisal + the U-artifact reading:
[[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]]. [@shailendra2022]
A repeated-measures cohort (Zhang 2026: HPFS, NHS, NHSII; 147,374 people, 35,798 deaths) moves the
lowest-risk point up (Shailendra's nadir \~60 min/week; Zhang's lowest-HR category 90-119 min/week):
aerobic-adjusted all-cause HRs from 30-59 through >=120 min/week of weight-machine/resistance training lie
between 0.87 and 0.92, and the top category (open-ended, mean 204 min/week) is 0.92 (0.86-0.98) on 1093
deaths. [@zhang2026rtmort]
The category CIs from 30-59 up do not separate (no between-category test is reported, and overlap is not
a test; the authors put the lowest risk at 90-119 min/week and read a plateau at >=120: «No additional
benefit was observed above 120 min/week.»), and no rise above the null is detected in the top category;
the open-ended >=120 bin is where the categories stop, not a located knee.
[@zhang2026rtmort]
Momma's pooled spline, on a broader exposure, reaches 1.0 near \~140 min/week, its lower band is above
1.0 from \~165 and it is about 1.18 at \~195, the end of its plotted range (figure-read). Shailendra's
4-study spline crosses 1.0 at roughly \~140-150, its lower 95% band is above 1.0 from \~165-170 and it is about 1.3
at 200 (Figure 3, PDF p 6, figure-read); its authors read «a possible “U”-shaped relationship between the duration of resistance training and the risk of all-cause mortality; however, because this analysis only included 4 studies, it should be viewed with caution».
[@shailendra2022]
Shailendra re-pools a subset of Momma's studies (its 4 dose-response studies are all among Momma's 6), so
it is not independent of Momma. The pooled splines and Zhang therefore disagree at the estimates above
\~140. Beyond each model's last knot a restricted cubic spline is constrained to be linear, so the shape
there (and its band) reflects the model as much as the data; the data beyond the knot still set the
slope. Zhang's and Shailendra's last knots are at 150 min/week (Shailendra: «3 knots placed at 10, 30, and 150 minutes»)
[@shailendra2022]
[@zhang2026rtmort]
Momma's is not reported in min/week; its figure is near-linear from \~85-90 min/week, and Shailendra's use
of the same percentile scheme on a subset of the same studies puts Momma's last knot plausibly somewhere in
\~75-150 min/week (figure-read). Zhang is not independent of the MAs either (its HPFS men, as Grøntved
2012, are already in Momma's and Saeidifard's all-cause pools) -> [[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]].

**Decision default (labelled; a reading across sources, not a test):** \~30 to \~120 min/week. Every held
all-cause estimate that reports this span sits below 1.0, and that stays true out to \~140 (Zhang's
categories 0.87-0.91 across 30-119 and 0.92 at >=120, spline studied to 300; Momma's spline below 1.0 to
\~140, plotted to \~195; Shailendra's 4-study spline below 1.0 to \~140-150, nadir \~60, a re-pooling of
part of Momma's base rather than a further independent source). The lower region carries the same
self-report and partly-shared-data caveats as the upper: in Zhang's Table 2 the 90-119 min/week estimate
moves from 0.66 age-adjusted to 0.76 with covariates and 0.87 with aerobic activity added, and residual
confounding plausibly accounts for part of what remains; how much is not shown.
[@zhang2026rtmort]
The default stops at \~120, not \~140,
because no held source shows further all-cause benefit beyond \~120 (Zhang's plateau reading at >=120,
quoted above; cause-specific shapes differ: Zhang's CVD spline keeps falling, P for
non-linearity 0.98). Above \~140 the held estimates conflict — Momma's and Shailendra's pooled splines
(overlapping studies) above the null, Zhang's top category below it (its spline point estimate stays
below 1.0 to \~270, its upper band reaching \~1.0 near \~190-195, figure-read) — and all are sparse, self-reported and partly shared (HPFS in Zhang
and Momma; Shailendra's studies a subset of Momma's), with the linear-tail caveat above applying to each
curve, so that span is neither a harm nor a safety finding.
[inferred from @zhang2026rtmort]

**Joint RT x aerobic in one pooled analysis — the aerobic contrast is larger, and RT categories go with
lower HRs within most aerobic strata (no RT trend detected at >=45 MET-h/week) `[2026-10-28, Zhang 2026]`.** Against
people with <7.5 MET-h/week of aerobic activity and no RT, RT with <7.5 MET-h/week of aerobic activity
(below the guideline level, not none) went with all-cause HR 0.93 (0.88-0.97) at 1-59 min/week, 0.89
(0.76-1.05; 152 deaths) at 60-119 and 0.97 (0.75-1.26; 58 deaths) at >=120; aerobic activity without
RT went with 0.74 (0.72-0.77) at 7.5-<15 MET-h/week, falling to 0.57 (0.54-0.60) at >=45. The lowest
cells combined both (e.g. 0.55, 0.50-0.60, at 30-<45 MET-h/week and 60-119 min/week RT). The RT trend
within aerobic strata was significant at <7.5, 15-<30 and 30-<45 MET-h/week, P=0.06 at 7.5-<15, and
not detected at >=45 MET-h/week (HRs 0.53-0.58 across RT categories, P=0.55). RERI 0.07 (-0.37 to 0.52): no
interaction on the additive scale was detected. [@zhang2026rtmort] Trend tests inside each stratum are not an
interaction test, and the RERI (any vs no RT, <7.5 vs >=7.5 MET-h) does not test the >=45 stratum, so a
different RT association at very high aerobic volume is not shown. Read with the same labels on both
sides: both contrasts are self-reported, observational and from one pooled analysis of three cohorts
(HPFS, NHS, NHSII) with one adjustment set, though the RT item was validated only in men and records
time only, while aerobic activity is MET-weighted, and the arms differ in units and volume (7.5-<15
MET-h/week is \~150-300 min of moderate activity on the source's guideline equivalence, against RT under an hour). So a larger
aerobic estimate is not a shown larger causal effect, and part of the gap may be measurement and dose.
The >=45 MET-h/week result (about three times the 15 MET-h upper guideline band) is a no-trend-detected
result for **mortality** only, not an interaction and not a located ceiling — the study measured no
strength, function, falls or quality-of-life outcome. The authors' own conclusion is stronger: «The
lowest risk occurs with high levels of both, though resistance training offers no added benefit beyond
≥45 MET-h/week of aerobic activity.» [@zhang2026rtmort]
The wiki reads it more narrowly: a within-stratum trend test (P=0.55) shows no trend detected, which is
not a shown absence of an RT association at that aerobic volume. For an inactive adult this fits the page's all-cause-mortality ordering
(aerobic first, RT added); Zhang did not study people unable to do aerobic activity, so it cannot speak
to RT as a substitute. [inferred from @zhang2026rtmort]

**The operative claim is *both, and together* — not *strength instead of cardio*.** No profile in the
annex ranks resistance training above aerobic activity, and where the two are compared head-to-head
(anxiety, 16 RCTs; depression, 33 RCTs; sleep), WHO reports *«No significant difference was found
between studies examining resistance training vs. aerobic exercise training»* — at **LOW** and **VERY
LOW** certainty. [@who2020]

**A distinction that is NOT a tension.** Siahpush 2019 (N=68,706) reports *«There was no association
between all-cause mortality and meeting strength recommendations (and not aerobic PA
recommendations)»* — which looks opposed to Stamatakis. It is not: **Siahpush's profile is about
smokers**, Stamatakis pools 11 general-population cohorts. Different population, consistent once
matched. Recorded here so the apparent clash is not re-filed later as a *tension*.
[@who2020]

## Sedentary time is a separate exposure with its own thresholds

> «For all-cause and CVD mortality, a threshold of 6-8 h/day of total sitting and 3-4 h/day of TV
> viewing was identified, above which the risk is increased.»
> [@who2020]

The relationship is *«non-linear for all-cause mortality»* and **PA-adjusted** — i.e. it survives
controlling for activity, so sitting less and moving more are not the same lever. (Between people the
two barely correlate; within a fixed waking day, a sitting cut is a reallocation into movement, which is
how the wear-time-adjusted device estimates below must be read.)

**The dedicated anchor for this sub-question is now [[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]]**
(Patterson 2018, gold dose-response MA), which supplies the per-outcome curve shape underlying WHO's
6-8h/3-4h summary range, adds **incident T2D** (TV->T2D the strongest association, 29% PAF) and cancer
mortality, and shows the sitting curve is an *accelerating-harm* knee — the mirror image of the
activity-benefit plateau on this page. `[2026-08-21, Patterson]`

**TV viewing carries a lower threshold than total sitting (3-4 h/day vs 6-8 h/day) and stronger
associations.** Two behaviours inside one category, behaving differently — though what accompanies TV
viewing is not addressed here, so confounding is not excluded.
`-> [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]` for the same structure in a different domain.

**Device and self-report sitting thresholds are not the same quantity and cannot be ordered.** Ekelund
2019 (accelerometry) finds the sitting-mortality association more pronounced above *9.5 h/day*
[@ekelundacc2019]. That is the steep part
of the device curve; it starts rising at \~7.5 h, and with the \~8 h Ekelund 2026 increment boundary below,
the device onset (\~7.5-8 h) and steepening (\~9.5 h) bracket the self-report \~8 h change point
(Patterson, on [[Sedentary Behaviour and Chronic Disease Risk]], the literature behind WHO's 6-8 h).
Device sedentary time (low counts on a hip monitor) is also not self-reported sitting. So no ordering
of device vs self-report thresholds is supported. Read the WHO 6-8 h number as a self-report figure.
(corrected 2026-10-28: was *the objective sitting threshold is HIGHER than the self-report one,
the same self-report/device gap running in the sitting direction*.)

**Re-read `[2026-10-28, Ekelund 2026]`: the device curve starts rising earlier than 9.5 h.** The 9.5 h
above is where the 2019 curve became *more pronounced*; the same sentence says the dose-response relations «increased gradually
from about 7.5 to 9 hours and were more pronounced at greater than 9.5 hours»
[@ekelundacc2019]. Ekelund 2026, on six
hip/lower-back cohorts, finds «no association between reducing sedentary time and the risk of mortality
in those spending less than approximately 480 min/day sedentary», a progressively lower risk from
reducing it above 480 min/day («comprising more than 70% of the participants»), and at >=660 min/day
about 10% (-30 min) and 25% (-60 min) lower risk.
[@ekelund2026small]

| Parameter | Ekelund 2019 | Ekelund 2026 | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number | gradual from \~7.5 h, steeper >9.5 h | no association below \~8 h (480 min) | **PARTLY** — both are features of a device curve, but 2019 describes risk by level and 2026 where *reducing* sitting starts to be associated with lower risk |
| Exposure | device sedentary time (level; model B adjusts sitting and MVPA for each other) | device sedentary time (-30/-60 min from observed level; wear-time adjusted, so a cut is a swap into light/MVPA) | measured alike; contrast and adjustment differ |
| Cohorts | 2019 consortium pool | 6 in the sitting analysis, 4 of them (ABC, NHANES, NNPAS, WHS) also in the 2019 pool and all four in the 2019 sitting curve (the 2019 Methods name the two Actical cohorts, REGARDS and Framingham, as MVPA-only; the Fig 2 caption's «FHS (Women’s Health Study)12» is a labelling slip; [@ekelundacc2019] Methods + Table 1); REGARDS is MVPA-only in both | largely shared — type F |

Read together: below about 8 h/day the 2026 increments show no association, the 2019 level curve starts
rising gently from \~7.5 h, and it steepens past \~9.5 h. *Device threshold 9.5 h* as a single number marks
the steep part, not the onset; for most adults in these cohorts (>70% above 480 min) a sitting reduction
is in the range where it is associated with lower risk. The device onset (\~7.5-8 h) and steepening
(\~9.5 h) bracket the self-report \~8 h change point, and the exposures differ, so no ordering of device vs
self-report thresholds is supported. [inferred from @ekelund2026small]

## Does activity OFFSET sitting? Mostly yes — the Ekelund 2016 interaction

The decision this answers: *if I must sit 8+ hours (desk job, commute), can being active cancel the
risk?* Ekelund 2016 (harmonised MA, >1 million adults, joint sitting x PA analysis) is the source, and
the answer is **high activity eliminates the sitting-mortality association**:

> «Daily sitting time was not associated with higher all-cause mortality rates among those in the most
> active quartile. Compared with the referent (<4 h of sitting per day and highest quartile of physical
> activity [>35.5 MET-hour/week]), there was no increased risk of dying during follow up in those who sat
> for more than 8h/day but who also reported >35.5 MET-hour/week of activity (HR=1.04; 95% CI, 0.99, 1.10).
> In contrast, those who sat the least (<4 h/day) and were in the lowest (<2.5 MET- hour/week) activity
> quartile had a significantly increased risk of dying during follow-up (HR=1.27, 95% CI, 1.22, 1.31).»
> [@ekelundsitting2016]

The striking comparison: **the most-active + most-sitting group had LOWER mortality than the least-active
+ least-sitting group** — activity dominates sitting when both are pushed. Stratified, the sitting
penalty (>8 vs <4 h/day) shrinks stepwise across activity quartiles: 1.27 -> 1.12 -> 1.10 -> 1.04 (ns).
The dose that eliminates it is high — **60-75 min/day of moderate activity**, above the basic guideline:

> «High levels of moderate intensity physical activity (i.e. about 60 to 75 minutes per day) appear to
> eliminate the increased risk of death associated with high sitting time. However, this high activity
> level attenuates, but does not eliminate the increased risk associated with high TV viewing time.»
> [@ekelundsitting2016]

**Two decision-relevant asymmetries.** (i) The offsetting dose (60-75 min/d) is *higher* than the
mortality-plateau dose (\~24 min/d MVPA) above — so *enough activity to bank the mortality benefit* is not
automatically *enough to cancel heavy sitting*; the sitting offset asks more (largely a self-report vs
device gap; see the Ekelund 2020 section below). (ii) **TV viewing is only
attenuated, not eliminated** — even the most active kept excess risk at >5 h/day TV (HR 1.16, 1.05-1.28)
[@ekelundsitting2016], whereas total sitting was
fully offset. TV is not just *sitting*: postprandial-evening timing, snacking,
and fewer sitting-breaks are the offered mechanisms, so the two sedentary exposures are not one lever
(consistent with Willett's near-zero sitting/activity correlation above). Magnitude anchor: the
least-active + >8 h-sitting group's 58% excess risk «is similar to that of smoking and obesity».
[@ekelundsitting2016]

**Caveats (all three sources).** Ekelund 2016 uses *self-reported* sitting and PA at one timepoint
(attenuation toward null), mostly >45 y, western; reverse causation addressed only by excluding
baseline-ill / early deaths — the weak check, same as Ekelund 2019 and Paluch. So the offset is a
robust, biologically-plausible *association* (1 h moderate activity improves postprandial lipid/glucose
after prolonged sitting), not a proven causal cancellation.

### The offset dose, device-measured — the asymmetry narrows on a common instrument `[2026-09-22, Ekelund 2020]` `type-F`

**The self-report offset above (60-75 min/day) is now redone with accelerometry, and the device figure is
about half.** Ekelund 2020 (harmonised MA, **nine accelerometry cohorts, 44 370 middle-aged/older adults**,
3451 deaths) cross-tabulates device-measured MVPA x sedentary time — the JOINT-combinations follow-up to
the SEPARATE-associations Ekelund 2019, so the offset *dose* is a new estimate, not a re-run:
> «Our joint analysis results suggest that about 30–40 min (median of medians=34 min; IQR: 26–48 min) of
> MVPA per day (online supplemental table 2) attenuated the risk of death in our most conservative model.»
> [@ekelund2020joint]

In the highest MVPA third (\~34 min/day) high sedentary time carried no significant excess mortality
(+40%, 95% CI 0.87-2.26, ns); the risk sat entirely in the low-MVPA third (\~2 min/day), where every
sedentary combination was elevated, worst cell HR 2.63, i.e. +163% (95% CI 1.93-3.57; the paper prints it as
263% against an HR-scale interval, a slip its +65% / +40% cells do not make) (corrected 2026-10-06: +263% ->
+163%, Ekelund 2020 chunk 01).
[@ekelund2020joint]

**But the device 34 min and the self-report 60-75 min are NOT the same quantity — the parameter table:**

| Parameter | Ekelund 2016 (self-report) | Ekelund 2020 (accelerometer) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure measurement | self-report questionnaire (leisure/recreational, bouts >=10 min) | hip accelerometer (all movement, multiple domains) | **NO** — device captures incidental movement self-report misses |
| Offsetting dose | \~60-75 min/day moderate-intensity | \~30-40 min/day MVPA (median-of-medians 34) | NO — read off different instruments, not convertible |
| Verb on the association | «eliminate» the sitting-mortality association | «attenuate» the risk (paper's own word) | NO — eliminate != attenuate |
| Sedentary contrast | >8 vs <4 h/day (self-report) | tertiles; low <8.5 h/d, high >10.7 h/d (device) | NO — different thresholds and instrument |
| Grouping | activity quartiles (top >35.5 MET-h/week) | MVPA tertiles (high third median 34 min) | NO — quartile vs tertile |
| Design / n | >1,000,000 adults, harmonised joint MA | 44 370, 9 cohorts, harmonised joint MA | both joint harmonised MAs — **and Ekelund authors both** |

[@ekelundsitting2016]
[@ekelund2020joint]

**So *the offset dose halved* is NOT a clean apples-to-apples claim** — and the paper itself says the drop
«is likely explained in part by differences between self-­reported and accelerometer measured physical
activity and sedentary behaviour», because «Self-­reported physical activity usually only assesses specific
domains ... whereas accelerom­eter measures capture more movement across multiple domains».
[@ekelund2020joint]

**The decision-change is the WITHIN-instrument comparison.** The held asymmetry above — *cancelling heavy
sitting (60-75 min) asks substantially more than banking the mortality benefit (\~24 min)* — was partly an
artifact of pitting a **self-report** offset (60-75) against a **device** plateau (\~24 min, Ekelund 2019).
Device-vs-device, the offset dose (\~34 min) and the mortality-plateau dose (\~24 min) come from the *same*
accelerometry consortium and are **close** — both inside the guideline range (150-300 min/week \~=
21-43 min/day), which is exactly what Ekelund 2020 recommends for heavy sitters. So on a common instrument
the two doses roughly coincide: enough MVPA to bank most of the mortality benefit is, to a first
approximation, also enough to attenuate heavy sitting. The earlier *the offset asks more* reading survives
only against self-report and is largely a measurement gap.

**Independence: type-F, not type-E.** Ekelund 2020 shares first-authorship and overlapping cohorts with
Ekelund 2016 and 2019 (and co-authors Jefferis / Lee with Paluch 2022), so it is a measurement *refinement*
of the held self-report offset, **not** an independent instrument — no `[E-independent]` token, no
`sources:` pad as corroboration. Caveats carry over and add: observational, median follow-up 5.5 y in some
cohorts (short), ≥40 y so not generalisable to younger/more active or LMIC populations; E-values 2.1-4.63
mean «uncontrolled confounding would have to be as large in magnitude as smoking to distort our results».
[inferred from @ekelund2020joint; @ekelundsitting2016; @ekelundacc2019]

## Older adults — falls, which is a patient-important outcome

> «Long-term exercise is associated with a reduction in falls, injurious falls, and probably fractures
> in older adults, including people with cardiometabolic and neurological diseases.»
> [@who2020]

Falls and fractures are outcomes people care about directly, not surrogates — the strongest outcome
class the annex carries for this stratum.

**Quantified and mechanised by a gold Cochrane SR -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]].**
The WHO annex states the *association*; Sherrington's 108-RCT review supplies the *effect and its active ingredient*:
exercise cuts the rate of falls by 23% (RaR 0.77, 0.71-0.83, HIGH certainty), and the active ingredient
is **balance/functional training**, not activity volume — resistance training alone has not been shown
to reduce falls (very low certainty, 5 RCTs; corrected 2026-10-28 from *does not reduce falls*, matching
the nucleus) [@sherrington2019]. *Not volume* means not
general-activity volume; prescribed programme time is a separate question. The Cochrane review notes
most head-to-head trials «were not likely to be adequately powered to detect diﬀerences between
diﬀerent exercise programmes», but within studies found «some indication that higher doses of exercise
were associated with a greater impact on the rate of falls and the number of people falling»
[@sherrington2019]. The team's 2017 between-trial
meta-regression tested prescribed time only as two cut-points: across community trials it found larger
effects where 3 or more hours a week were prescribed (ratio of rate ratios 0.77, 0.65-0.91) but not at 2 or more
hours (0.98, 0.83-1.16); no cut above 3 h was tested, and including walking in a programme was not associated with a different effect
(1.01, 0.86-1.20) [@sherrington2017falls].
That is a cut-point on prescribed time, potentially confounded, not a dose curve -> the nucleus. The same
review's suggested default, taken together with a recent walking trial that did not prevent falls, an
earlier trial in which brisk walking increased falls, and the meta-regression findings, is that «walking should not be pre- scribed as a single fall prevention intervention and high-risk older
people should not be told to walk briskly». The authors' next sentence keeps walking as an add-on:
«However, given the popularity of walking,22 its other health beneﬁts and the inclusion of individually
prescribed walking in some successful interventions (such as the Otago Home Exercise Programme),23 we
suggest that walking programmes may be carefully prescribed to older people in addition to other fall
prevention exercises.»
[@sherrington2017falls]. This is a **refinement, not
independent backing** — the WHO annex's falls conclusion rests on largely the same RCT base the Cochrane
SR pools, so treat the SR as bounding/mechanising the guideline claim, not corroborating it from a
separate route. The fracture arm stays lower-certainty on both.

## Second outcome — dementia (same shape: most benefit at the bottom)

Physical inactivity is one of the 14 modifiable dementia risk factors -> [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]]. A 58-study SR+MA found «physical activity was associated with a decreased risk
of all-cause dementia (RR 0.80, 0.77 to 0.84, n=257,983)» across intensities and follow-ups >=20 years,
with the reduction «greatest when moving from extreme sedentariness to some physical activity».
[@livingston2024] This replicates the *marginal-minute-worth-
most-at-the-bottom* shape on a second outcome. Caveat unchanged: the link is «likely to be bidirectional»
(pre-clinical dementia reduces activity), and the one 5-year RCT of structured exercise found no overall
cognition/MCI difference — so the observational RR 0.80 is a decreased-risk *association*, not a proven
prevention effect. Where a *multidomain* bundle **including** exercise (FINGER RCT) did move a cognitive
composite, the design cannot attribute that to the exercise component
-> [[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]]. Whether single-domain exercise RCTs on
cognition are null may depend on the comparator. In that 5-year RCT the control arm was itself active:
«At 5 years, 96% of the control group adhered to national guidance for physical activity, and 75% and
76% adhered to the interval training intervention»
[@livingston2024].
Livingston reads the overall null as «in line with the small cognitive benefit shown in an umbrella
review of RCTs»
[@livingston2024].
Against health education, Mendes's 109-RCT network finds exercise alone improves global
cognition, SMD 0.14 (0.05-0.22), a small surrogate effect that, per the Results text, is not
significant against an active (stretching / low-intensity exercise) control (the Abstract says the
active-control models were «similarly significant»; see
[[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]])
[@mendes2025multidomain].
That the comparator reconciles the null with the small positive is
the wiki's reading, not a held source's (Livingston reconciles the null with a small benefit, but
not by comparator). (corrected 2026-10-22: bare *single-domain
exercise RCTs on cognition stay null* -> comparator-scoped.)

The direct dementia effect is now contested at the reverse-causation frontier
[@kivimaki2019inactivity]: Kivimaki's individual-
participant meta-analysis (n approx 404,840, 19 cohorts) found inactivity->all-cause dementia present at
<10 y (HR 1.40, 1.24-1.59) but **absent beyond 10 years** (HR 1.01, 0.89-1.14), while its cardiometabolic
positive controls (diabetes 1.42, CHD 1.24, stroke 1.16) held in both periods — so the borrowed RR 0.80
may be a prodromal marker for dementia specifically, not a causal slope. The clash with the 58-study
aggregate MA is filed: [[Does Physical Activity Protect Against Dementia Beyond Ten Years]]. This page's
*dose-shape* claim is unchanged — activity's biggest marginal payoff sits at the sedentary bottom, driven
by the cardiometabolic and mortality outcomes BOTH studies agree on; only the dementia-specific causal
reading narrows.

## Third outcome — cancer, and the dose-shape may differ from the mortality curve

WCRF's Third Expert Report grades **physical activity protective against several cancers** — colorectum
(the evidence «is for colon cancer only», matrix FN56), endometrium and postmenopausal breast (probable),
with a separate probable judgement for vigorous activity and breast cancer.
[@wcrf2018ter] Mechanism: activity «reduces body fatness, in particular
visceral fat» and lowers circulating insulin, oestrogen and inflammation — the same adiposity/hormone/
inflammation routes that make body fatness carcinogenic, run in reverse. [@wcrf2018ter]

**The friction worth naming — the curve's shape is outcome-specific.** This page's headline is that the
*mortality* benefit **flattens** early (\~24 min/day MVPA). WCRF states the opposite shape for cancer:
«For cancer prevention, it is likely that the greater the amount of physical activity, the greater the
benefit» — no plateau asserted. [@wcrf2018ter]
This is not a contradiction: it is the same *outcome-specific dose-shape* pattern the wiki already holds
(fruit/veg plateaus on all-cause mortality but keeps a gradient on CV mortality in one source). So «most
benefit at the bottom» is the mortality reading; for cancer the report leaves the upper arm open, and a
person optimizing specifically for cancer risk cannot bank the mortality plateau as a stopping point.
[inferred from @wcrf2018ter]
Caveat symmetric with the rest of the page: WCRF's cancer grades are **observational cohort** judgements,
so reverse causation (illness lowers activity) is not excluded by design.

## What this page does NOT support

- **No compensation analysis exists anywhere in the annex** — *now held on a sibling page.* Whether a
  given activity dose is offset by reduced activity elsewhere, or by increased intake, is untouched here;
  [[Exercise Energy Compensation]] (Riou 2015) supplies it: compensation is real (\~18%, rising to \~84%
  long-term) but is driven by adiposity/age/duration, **not** by intensity — so *walking is better
  tolerated than intense exercise* remains unsupported (intensity is not a compensation predictor).
- **No ranking of modalities.** See above.
- **No evidence that brief distributed *exercise snacks* change mortality.** For the near-inactive who
  will not do structured sessions, brief vigorous bouts spread across the day raise CRF and lower LDL-C
  ([[Exercise Snacks and Cardiometabolic Health]], Wan 2025) — but that evidence is on **surrogates over
  <=12 weeks**, with no hard-outcome or mortality data, so snacks are a plausible *entry lever* onto this
  curve, not a demonstrated substitute for the doses this page prices against mortality. The authors
  themselves cast snacks as a complement, «rather than a replacement for these recommendations».
  [@wan2025]
- **Frailty as a construct cannot be targeted from this evidence**: Kidd's 2019 systematic review of
  trials in pre-frail and frail adults, reproduced in WHO's annex, found that interventions with physical
  activity components improved physical outcomes and concludes that they «are key to maintaining
  independence in pre-frail and frail older adults», but also that *«A lack of consensus regarding the
  definition of frailty, and an absence of core measures to assess this means any attempt to create an
  optimal intervention will be impeded.»* An ill-defined construct — distinct from a well-defined
  measure that decays under targeting.
  [@who2020]
- **A study-level correlation is not an individual-level one.** *«At the study level, there was a
  positive correlation between the size of the exercise-induced effect on physical function and on
  cognitive function (b = 0.41; p = 0.002).»* That does not license "improving function improves
  cognition" for a person — the ecological-inference trap.
  [@who2020]

## Sedentary behaviour is not the inverse of activity — and devices have type-specific bias `[2026-07-28, Willett ch.10]`

Two findings from Willett's physical-activity assessment chapter bear on how this page's estimates
should be read.

**1. Sitting and moving are close to independent, not two ends of one scale.**

> «Interestingly, there is little correlation between sedentary behaviors and physical activity (Hu et
> al., 2003), suggesting that sedentary behaviors are not simply the opposite of physical activity.»
> [@willett]

**So "sedentary time" and "activity" are two exposures, not one variable read in two directions**, and
a person can be high on both. This is a unit-of-analysis point with a direct consequence: **a
recommendation phrased as *move more* does not automatically address sitting**, and evidence about one
does not transfer to the other (between people; within a fixed waking day, as in the wear-time-adjusted
device estimates above, a cut in sitting is a reallocation into movement). -> [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]] (same structure, different
domain: the label implies a single underlying quantity and there are two).

**2. Device measurement is not error-free, and its error is activity-type-specific.**

> «(2002) found that compared with portable indirect calorimetry, the Tritrac accelerometer
> overestimated the energy expenditure of walking and jogging, and underestimated that of stair
> climbing and stationary cycling in middle-aged women.»
> [@willett]

**This qualifies rather than undermines the device-measured estimate this page carries.** The bias is
**differential by activity mode** — over on ambulatory movement, under on climbing and cycling — so a
device-measured total activity variable is a weighted mixture whose weights depend on the population's
activity mix. **Two populations with identical true expenditure but different activity profiles will
be measured differently**, which is a transportability problem, not an attenuation problem.
[inferred from @willett]

**Note the vintage:** this is 2012 hardware (Tritrac). Modern accelerometry and the processing behind
the estimate on this page may behave differently, and **this wiki holds no source establishing that
either way**. Recorded as a caution about the *class* of instrument, not as a defect in the specific
estimate.

## Is "24 min/day" false precision? Read it as a flat-region central estimate (deliverable-critique, 2026-08-01)

The \~24 min/day MVPA figure is a real source value - the point where the mortality dose-response is
«close to the maximum», i.e. where the curve FLATTENS - not an invented threshold. But the concern is
right: it should not be read as a precise minimum effective dose. Two held rules make it a REGION, not a
number: *a minimum effective dose is a region, not a number* (outcome-specific), and *required precision
scales with local curvature* - near a plateau the exact figure carries little decision weight. So 24 min
is the central estimate of a broad flattening zone (\~20-40 min/day of MVPA); whether it is 20, 24 or 40
barely changes the decision, and the decision-relevant shape is the one this page already gives: the
marginal minute is worth the most at the bottom, and most of the benefit is banked well before the number
