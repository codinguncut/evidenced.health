## 1. Should you lose weight? Four decisions hiding behind one number

*Should I lose weight?* looks like a single question with a yes-or-no answer. It
is really four, and the scale hides all of them: **where** the fat is stored,
whether losing it reaches something you would actually feel or only moves a
blood-test number, **how** the loss is done, and **who** is asking. Each rests
on different evidence, and answering the wrong one is the expensive mistake.

The bottom line: the number on the scale is the least informative of the four
answers, and two distinctions carry most of the decision.

**First, the depot matters more than the total.** The fat that carries the risk
is largely the fat you cannot see — packed around and inside the organs — so
*where* it sits tells you more than your weight or your BMI. Some slim people
carry too much of it; some heavier people, at least for a time, carry less. And
the familiar idea that being a little overweight is protective is best read as a
measurement mirage: once you set aside smokers and people already ill and losing
weight, each step up from a lean-normal weight is associated with more risk, not
less.

**Second, losing fat buys some outcomes and not others.** It can put early
type-2 diabetes into remission, shrink a fatty liver, and — with large, sustained
loss — help you live longer. But those are specific, evidenced wins, not a
blanket promise: the largest weight-loss trial to date did not show that losing
weight prevents heart attacks. Improving a marker is not the same as living
longer or living better, and the two must be kept apart.

How *fast* you lose it is a minor knob. What protects you is holding on to muscle
while the fat comes off, and having a plan for afterward — the body defends the
weight it used to hold, so keeping fat off is a harder and genuinely different
task than taking it off, and failing at it is not a failure of willpower. And the
advice inverts with age: for older adults, losing weight can strip away muscle and
strength they cannot spare, so the goal there is protecting the body, not
shrinking it.

Start with what the scale number hides — where the fat is stored.

## 2. Where the fat is stored, not how much

The operative variable is not total fat mass but **where** the fat sits. Risk
tracks fat that has overflowed into and around the organs — liver and pancreas
above all — more faithfully than it tracks BMI or the number on the scale. Three
storage compartments are not three sizes of one thing; they are three different
objects doing different work.

- **Subcutaneous fat** — the safe, expandable store under the skin. Its capacity
  varies from person to person (and by ethnicity), and it is not itself
  pathogenic.
- **Visceral fat** — the intra-abdominal depot usually cast as *the* villain. It
  is better read as a **marker**: it correlates with the harmful depot without
  being the thing that does the harm.
- **Ectopic / intra-organ fat** — lipid deposited inside the liver and pancreas.
  This is the pathogenic depot, driving hepatic insulin resistance and impaired
  insulin secretion.

Taylor demotes visceral fat explicitly: he treats it as a surrogate marker for
intra-organ fat excess that is not itself pathophysiologically related to the
adverse metabolic consequences
[@taylor2015pft].
So a waist measurement is a **surrogate for a surrogate** — one step removed from
the intra-organ fat, which is itself a marker for the disease process
-> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. Useful because it correlates; never to be dressed up
as a hard outcome avoided.

This reframe explains who is at risk. The **personal fat threshold (PFT)** — the
individual capacity of the safe subcutaneous store, above which fat overflows
ectopically — is proposed to be independent of BMI
[@taylor2015pft].
It is a hypothesis under test, admitted directionally, not a measured quantity.
Its consequence is concrete: some people at an apparently normal weight are
already above their own storage capacity and carrying ectopic fat, while some
heavier people, for a time, are not.

The population data read as the same claim at scale. Metabolically-healthy
obesity (MHO) is not a durable free pass, but neither is it an emergency. Pooled
across cohorts, obesity with currently-normal metabolic markers is associated
with a modestly raised risk of death and cardiovascular events versus
metabolically-healthy normal weight — relative risk about **1.24 (1.02-1.55)**,
and only in studies followed **10 years or longer**; the pooled *absolute* excess
is roughly **0.7% over 10-11 years**
[@kramer2013mho].
The honest framing is a small, slow, non-zero excess that licenses continued —
not urgent — attention.

What dominates, in the same data, is **metabolic status rather than BMI**. A
person of normal weight but with an unhealthy metabolic profile carries a
relative risk of about **3.14 (2.36-3.93)** — far above metabolically-healthy
obesity, and comparable to unhealthy obesity
[@kramer2013mho].
Weight is associated with risk; *how the fat is stored and handled* is what the
risk tracks.

If depot is the operative variable, the BMI number is a crude proxy — and its
famous protective band is a mirage.

## 3. The obesity paradox is an artifact, not a protective band

The claim that being overweight protects against death — the *obesity paradox* — does
not survive bias correction. In the largest individual-participant meta-analysis of the
question (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration: 239 cohorts, 10.6 million people; the
bias-controlled primary analysis restricts to never-smokers, drops anyone with chronic
disease at baseline, and excludes the first 5 years of follow-up), the curve is J-shaped
with its **nadir at BMI 22.5-25 kg/m2** and a **steady monotone rise above it** with no
plateau in the studied range. Each 5 kg/m2 above 25 is associated with **HR 1.31
(1.29-1.33)** for all-cause mortality
[@globalbmi2016]. There is no
protective, or even neutral, band above the nadir once bias is removed.

### Watch the overweight arm walk from *protection* to harm

The paradox is manufactured, and the meta-analysis is built as a stepwise
confounder-stripping cascade that lets you watch it form. The overweight arm (BMI 25-30)
moves from apparent protection toward clear harm as each bias is removed:

| Analysis (cumulative exclusions) | Overweight HR (25-30) |
|---|---|
| All studies, raw | 0.96 (0.95-0.97) — apparent *protection* |
| + adjust smoking / exclude baseline disease | 0.99 (0.98-1.01) |
| + exclude first 5 years of follow-up | 1.03 (1.01-1.04) |
| + restrict to never-smokers (primary) | 1.11 (1.10-1.11) — clear *harm* |

[@globalbmi2016]

Smokers are leaner *and* die more, so they load the lean referent with high-risk people
and make excess weight look protective by contrast; early deaths and prevalent illness do
the same through reverse causation. Strip all three and the apparent protection inverts to
harm. [inferred from @globalbmi2016]

### The cleanest single confirmation — the smoking-stratified sign-flip

Holding the 5-year exclusion and no-baseline-disease constant so that *only smoking
status differs*, the overweight arm flips sign by stratum: **1.07 (1.06-1.07) in
never-smokers versus 0.94 (0.94-0.95) in ex/current smokers** (between-stratum
heterogeneity significant for every BMI group; overweight P=0.0003)
[@globalbmi2016]. The
*protective* overweight signal exists only in the smoking stratum. Smoking is the
confounder doing the work — a spurious *benefit*, the mirror image of a spurious harm.

### The genetic check corroborates causal harm and deflates the low arm

The cascade adjudicates by confounder-*removal* only. Wade's Mendelian randomization in
UK Biobank (77-SNP genetic risk score, 335,308 people, 9,570 deaths) supplies the
orthogonal genetic natural experiment, immune to the reverse causation and smoking
confounding the cascade removes by exclusion — and it corroborates the same reading. It
finds a causal association of higher BMI with all-cause mortality (HR 1.03 per 1 kg/m2,
0.99-1.07; directional but imprecise) that reaches significance for cardiovascular-cause
mortality (HR 1.10, 1.01-1.19)
[@wade2018]. Genetically, the
**nadir drops from \~26 (observational) to \~23 kg/m2**: the observational curve
«overestimate[s] the harmful effects of having underweight while underestimating the
harmful effects of having overweight or obesity»
[@wade2018]. The
correction inflates the high arm and deflates the low arm.

### The rule this instances — never file a protective arm as real

The underweight arm is a caution against over-reading in the other direction: it falls
from 1.81 (raw) to 1.47 (primary) but stays elevated, so low BMI carries residual real
excess mortality, not pure artifact
[@globalbmi2016]. The general discipline
is to **adjudicate the arm, not the curve**, and to run the artifact diagnostics —
referent-correction and a genetic/quasi-experimental check — before believing any
protective or plateau arm. A shape equally consistent with the causal and the artifact
explanation has no diagnostic value; the overweight *protection* here failed every check
-> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].

So carrying excess fat is associated with higher risk, and the association is
genetically corroborated as causal harm — but does *losing* it lower a hard outcome, or
only the markers?

## 4. Does losing fat reach hard outcomes, or only surrogates?

This is the decisive question, and the largest, longest randomized trial answered it
against intuition. **Look AHEAD** randomized 5,145 overweight/obese adults with type 2
diabetes to intensive lifestyle intervention versus diabetes support and education. Nearly
every **surrogate** moved the right way — greater weight loss, HbA1c reduction, blood
pressure, fitness, and (in a substudy) a large drop in C-reactive protein, «−44.9 and
−42.3 %» on statins plus lifestyle
[@belalcazar2013]. Yet the trial **did not
reduce hard cardiovascular events** (the composite of CV death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal
stroke, hospitalized angina): **HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.09**, stopped early for futility
[@look2013].

### Bound the null — it does not mean lifestyle weight loss is useless for the heart

The null is narrower than it sounds. The arms converged (the weight gap shrank from \~7.9
points at 1 year to \~2.5 at study end), so the contrast was a *modest sustained
difference*, not big-loss-versus-none. LDL — the strongest lipid CV lever — was the one
risk factor lifestyle did not improve
[@look2013], because statins were
adjusted more aggressively in the control arm
[@belalcazar2013]. Both
arms received good diabetes care, and the trial was powered for a *large* (18%) effect
with the endpoint changed mid-trial
[@look2013]. So the honest reading
is that a hard-CV-event benefit of lifestyle weight loss is *unproven at a moderate,
converged dose* — not disproven.

### The meta-analysis generalizes the CV-null, but finds mortality falls by a non-CV route

Ma's 54-RCT meta-analysis (30,206 obese adults, mostly non-diabetic) reads Look AHEAD
inside the broader picture and splits cleanly:

| Outcome | Ma 2017 (54-RCT MA) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| CV events | RR 0.93 (0.83-1.04), high quality | null — confirms Look AHEAD |
| CV mortality | RR 0.93 (0.67-1.31) | null |
| All-cause mortality | RR 0.82 (0.71-0.95), high quality | falls — \~6 fewer per 1000 |

[@ma2017]

On the heart, the meta-analysis **confirms and generalizes** the null: dietary/lifestyle
weight loss does not measurably cut cardiovascular events. But all-cause mortality **does
fall** — and since CV events are null, that benefit is **not routed through the heart**. It
is not a Look AHEAD artefact: removing that trial, the all-cause effect is if anything
larger (RR 0.78, n=33 trials, I^2=0%)
[@ma2017]. One confound: Ma's trials were
«usually low in fat and saturated fat», so part of the benefit could travel the
SFA-reduction channel rather than weight loss per se.

### The extreme-dose anchor agrees on mortality — SOS

At the opposite end of the dose-response, **SOS** (bariatric surgery in severe obesity,
\~14-25% sustained loss) finds the mortality benefit the moderate-dose trials could not
show: adjusted **HR 0.71 (P=0.01)** for all-cause mortality
[@sjostrom2007]. Two firewalls hold.
SOS is **not an RCT** — it is a matched, prospective, non-randomized cohort with
self-selection («desiring surgery») untouched by adjustment, so it warrants the arm
*directionally*, not causally-clean. And it cannot isolate weight loss per se: surgery also
alters gut hormones and satiety, and within SOS mortality did not track the degree of
weight loss achieved
[@sjostrom2007].

### SOS-positive versus Look AHEAD-null is not a tension

The apparent clash dissolves on the quantities compared: Look AHEAD's *null* is on **CV
events at a moderate dose**; SOS's *positive* is on **all-cause mortality at an extreme
dose**. On the one quantity they share — all-cause mortality — SOS **agrees** with Ma
(both show a reduction). So there is nothing joined to file. Two value moves instead: a
**directional dose-graded pattern** across interventions (moderate Ma RR 0.82, extreme SOS
HR 0.71 — suggestive, not an evidenced weight-*loss* dose-response, since neither isolates
grams lost); and, on the **all-cause strand only**, independent backing — lifestyle RCTs
and a surgical matched cohort, non-overlapping authors and design classes, reaching the
same mortality claim `[E-independent]`. The CV-event strand stays null.
[inferred from @ma2017; @sjostrom2007]

### The pattern channel is distinct from the weight channel — PREDIMED

The sharpest complement comes from the diet-pattern side. In **PREDIMED**, an
energy-unrestricted Mediterranean diet — no calorie target, little weight change — cut the
CV-event composite by \~30% (**HR 0.70, 0.55-0.89**) in high-risk primary prevention
[@estruch2018]. Set beside the weight-loss trials
that moved events little, this separates two levers that advice usually bundles: *what you
eat* (fat quality, whole pattern) appears to carry the CV-event signal that *how much you
weigh* did not. The caveat: populations and comparators differ (a reasoned cross-trial
contrast, not a head-to-head), and PREDIMED's own all-cause mortality was null — its win is
on events, not death -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]], [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

The composite lesson: a bundle of moved surrogates (weight, HbA1c, BP, CRP) is not a
patient-important outcome, and absolute benefit scales with baseline risk, so any true
small CV effect is smallest exactly where risk is lowest. The hard outcome weight loss most
clearly *does* move is not the heart attack — it is diabetes.

## 5. The hard outcome fat loss most clearly moves: diabetes (and the fatty liver)

Type 2 diabetes is the flagship reversibility outcome for fat loss, and the evidence is
unusually clean about it. DiRECT — an open-label, cluster-randomised trial run in routine UK
primary care — set **remission of type 2 diabetes as a primary outcome** and used a **total
diet replacement (TDR)** intervention: a low-energy formula diet of **825-853 kcal/day** for
about three months, then stepped food reintroduction and structured maintenance. Note the
formula is **59% carbohydrate** — a low-*energy*, not a low-*carbohydrate*, diet
[@lean2018].

At 12 months the intervention arm put **46% into remission** (drug-free HbA1c below the
diabetic range, off all antidiabetic medication for at least two months) versus 4% of usual
care — **odds ratio 19.7 (95% CI 7.8-49.8)**
[@lean2018].
This is a **patient-important outcome — drug-free normoglycaemia — not a surrogate**, and it
carries a measured quality-of-life gain, not just a marker change.

### Remission scales with weight lost — a clean dose-response

Pooling both randomised arms, remission rises monotonically with the weight sustained at 12
months: **0% among those who gained weight, 7% at 0-5 kg lost, 34% at 5-10 kg, 57% at
10-15 kg, and 86% at >=15 kg lost**
[@lean2018].
The operative variable is the *amount of weight lost*, not the diet's composition — the
gradient is monotone across the studied range with no visible plateau below 15 kg (whether it
plateaus above is untested). This is a within-population association, so responder confounding
is live; read it as direction, well-corroborated, rather than a randomised per-kilogram effect.

### The mechanism is depot-specific — back to the ectopic-fat story

DiRECT rests on Taylor's *twin-cycle* account: short-duration T2D is driven by excess fat in
the **liver and pancreas**, and draining those depots by negative energy balance restores
normal glucose control and beta-cell function
[@lean2018].
This ties section 5 back to section 2's ectopic-fat argument: remission is depot-drawdown
made clinical.

### A second dietary lever reaches the same outcome — refinement, not a diet war

Carbohydrate restriction also achieves remission (Goldenberg's meta-analysis reports a large
six-month remission advantage, an NNT of about 3, under the medication-permitted definition)
[@goldenberg2021].
This is *not a tension with DiRECT*. Both routes drive weight loss and both produce remission;
the shared engine is **energy deficit and depot drainage**. DiRECT hits 46% remission on a
59%-carbohydrate formula — so carbohydrate restriction is **one lever for the weight loss that
drives remission, not a necessary condition**. Goldenberg's own remission advantage attenuates
toward null by 12 months as the weight advantage is regained, consistent with a weight-mediated
effect delivered by a low-carb route. Two dietary levers, one outcome, one engine — an
**F-refinement**.
[inferred from @lean2018; @goldenberg2021]

### Fatty liver regresses on a weight-loss ladder

MASLD (metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease, formerly NAFLD) responds to the same lever
on a **dose-laddered** schedule, each rung tied to a distinct outcome: sustained loss of
**>=5% reduces liver fat (steatosis); 7-10% improves liver inflammation (steatohepatitis/MASH);
>=10% improves fibrosis**
[@easl2024].
The guideline states the association is dose-dependent, but attaches caveats: the ladder traces
to a single histology-endpoint trial, few sustain the loss, and lifestyle weight loss is **not**
shown to reverse advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis — it works on the reversible stages
[@easl2024].

Diabetes and liver fat respond to *losing* the fat. The cancer link runs the other way — through
*carrying* it.

## 6. Body fatness and cancer

Excess body fat is a graded *cause* of cancer at many sites. The World Cancer Research Fund
(WCRF), pooling cohort epidemiology, finds that **12 of the 17 cancers it reviewed are linked to
greater body fatness** — graded convincing or probable — and presents weight management first
because the evidence «has grown stronger over the last decade»
[@wcrf2018ter].
IARC independently adds three further sites (thyroid, multiple myeloma, meningioma), taking the
footprint to roughly a dozen-plus sites of the 17 assessed
[@wcrf2018ter].

The plausible mechanisms — informing direction, not proving causation, which rests on the cohort
data — are **hyperinsulinaemia** (insulin signalling via mTOR/PI3K/AKT driving proliferation),
**sex hormones** (elevated oestradiol in oestrogen-receptor-positive tissues such as breast and
endometrium), and **chronic inflammation** (obesity as a chronic inflammatory state)
[@wcrf2018ter].
*Note: WCRF assigns the IGF-I pathway to dairy and height, not to body fatness, so it is not
claimed here for adiposity.*

The essential point is the **direction of the evidenced claim**: this is a **harm of *carrying*
excess fat** — adiposity is associated with higher cancer *incidence*, observational-grade. The
claim that *losing* fat lowers incidence is a **distinct, thinner question and a [G]-gap**: WCRF
notes the survivor evidence on whether changing body fatness after diagnosis alters clinical
course is explicitly limited, and the one reversibility signal it offers (C-reactive protein
falling with weight loss) is a **surrogate**, not a cancer-incidence outcome
[inferred from @wcrf2018ter].
So the direction of benefit from loss is named as unestablished, not inferred from the harm of
carrying.

Carrying excess fat clearly harms; how you take it off decides how much of the benefit you keep.

## 7. How you take it off: rate, composition, and the realistic alternative

The popular question is *how fast is too fast?*, as if speed were the exposure. It is not.
The rate of loss is a **minor knob**. What protects a person while fat comes off is preserving
**lean mass** (fat-free mass — the muscle and organ tissue a deficit erodes) and choosing a
method they can actually sustain, because *adherence is part of the effect*: a smaller deficit
held for a year beats a larger one abandoned in a month. Every method below is judged as a
**substitution** against the realistic alternative, not against an ideal -> [[Better than What]].
The operative variables are muscle protection and sustainability; rate rides along with the size
of the deficit rather than causing harm on its own.

### Supervised rapid loss is not intrinsically harmful

The *slow-is-safer* folk rule is **weakly grounded for supervised protocols**. The most decisive
held evidence runs the other way: DiRECT deliberately used a genuinely rapid loss — a
«low energy formula diet (825–853 kcal/day; 59% carbohydrate, 13% fat, 26% protein, 2% fibre)
for 3 months»
[@lean2018]
— and delivered the biggest remission signal in the corpus (section 5), rising monotonically
with kilograms lost. Rapid, when the method is right (protein-adequate, micronutrient-complete,
supervised, drugs managed), is safe and effective. The harm folk wisdom pins on *speed* mostly
belongs to the *method* — protein-poor, unbalanced, unsupervised regimens — not to the number on
the scale.

### Lean-mass preservation is the real work under a deficit

Two levers defend fat-free mass while fat comes off, and neither depends on going slowly.

- **Adequate protein.** The energy-balance target of \~1.6 g/kgBM (Morton's non-significant break
  point «1.62 (1.03, 2.20) g/kg/day»
  [@morton2018])
  **rises under a deficit**: Refalo's exploratory analysis finds «increasing protein intake up to
  1.9 g/kgBM/day or 2.5 g/kgFFM/day, on average, is associated with less FFM loss»
  [@refalo2025].
  A deficit raises the requirement; read \~1.9 g/kgBM as a floor-and-direction, not a fine target.
- **Resistance training.** The training stimulus, not the protein dial, is the larger driver of
  FFM retention under restriction; protein is the adjunct. Get the training right first.

These are the muscle-protective moves that make *rate* second-order: with protein and resistance
training, a faster loss is largely the same loss, sooner.

### Exercise: weak as a sole weight lever, strong for lean mass and visceral fat

Both halves are true and must be held together. As a **sole** weight-loss lever exercise
disappoints, because the body claws back part of the burn — *energy compensation*. Across 61
studies «The overall mean energy compensation was 18% ± 93%»
[@riou2015];
a doubly-labelled-water estimate is larger — «energy compensation averages 28%, i.e., only 72% of
the extra calories we spend on additional activity translates into extra calories burned that day»
[@careau2021].
So exercise is a poor way to open the *deficit*.

But it is a strong **lean-preservation and visceral-fat** lever. Recchia's discussion: during
caloric restriction «both fat and muscle mass are reduced», whereas «exercise might stimulate fat
loss while maintaining muscle mass»
[@recchia2023].
And per unit of prescribed deficit, «exercise had a superior dose–response effect on reducing
visceral fat compared with caloric restriction (ES −0.18 (−0.33 to −0.04); p=0.012)»
[@recchia2023].
The defensible posture: **create the deficit with diet, protect the body with exercise
(especially resistance training).** The compensation caveat is not *exercise doesn't matter* — it
is *exercise is the wrong tool for the scale and the right tool for composition*.

### What is not held — name the magnitudes as gaps, do not invent them

Two rate-specific magnitudes are load-bearing and **not held**, so they are stated as gaps rather
than papered over with numbers:

- **[G] Rate and regain.** No held randomized rate-of-loss trial (the gradual-vs-rapid, method-
  and-total-loss-matched design) isolates whether *speed itself* changes regain. The regain driver
  the corpus does hold is the maintenance phase, not the descent (section 8).
- **[G] Speed vs muscle.** How much fat-free mass is lost per kilogram at 0.5 vs 1.0 vs 1.5 kg/week,
  with and without protein plus resistance training, is not held. What is held is that lean-mass
  loss scales with **deficit size** (which correlates with rate) and is defendable.

Taking it off is one task; the body then fights to put it back.

## 8. The body defends its fat: set-point and maintenance

After weight loss the body does not simply hold the new, lighter state. It pushes back — lowering
what it spends and raising what it seeks to eat — so that **maintenance is a distinct, harder task
than loss**, and regain is a *physiological headwind, not a willpower failure*. This is admitted
**directionally**: the mechanism is real and human-corroborated, but the persistence limb is
contested even in the sources that carry it, so it is held as a defended set-point, not a hard law.

### Two limbs of the defence — and the appetite limb is the larger one

- **Expenditure falls below what size predicts.** After loss, energy expenditure drops more than
  the smaller body accounts for (*adaptive thermogenesis*), and Hall reads the data as «most
  commensurate» with a set-point model. But he flags the persistence as unsettled — adaptation
  «may continue for years after energy balance is reestablished at a lower weight», «although
  controversy remains regarding its persistence»
  [@hallguo2017].
  Hold this limb directionally, not as established fact.
- **Appetite rises — and this is the bigger driver.** Hall ranks the limbs: after loss, intake
  climbed «about 100 kcal/d per kilogram of lost weight – an effect more than 3-fold larger than
  the corresponding energy expenditure adaptations»
  [@hallguo2017].
  The \~100 kcal/d/kg is the **hunger** signal, not the expenditure drop — the pull to eat, not only
  the frugal metabolism, is what the body defends with.

### The hormones shift toward regain, and the shift persists

Sumithran measured the appetite-hormone side directly after a very-low-calorie loss. A year out,
satiety signals stayed suppressed and hunger stayed elevated: leptin (a satiety/adiposity signal)
«remained 35.5±4.7% below baseline levels»
[@sumithran2011],
and ghrelin (the hunger signal) «remained significantly higher at 62 weeks than at baseline»
[@sumithran2011].
The decision-relevant reframe is Sumithran's own: these «alterations persist for 12 months after
weight loss, even after the onset of weight regain, suggesting that the high rate of relapse ...
has a strong physiological basis and is not simply the result of the voluntary resumption of old
habits»
[@sumithran2011].

Two caveats keep this honest, and neither undoes the reframe. The study is single-arm and
uncontrolled, so the causal step from *hormones persist* to *hormones cause regain* is inferred,
not proven; and *not simply* is doing work — the claim is that a purely volitional account is
**incomplete**, not that behaviour is irrelevant.

### Why the reframe changes the intervention, not just the mood

If regain is a defended physiology rather than a moral lapse, then *success* is not a fixed
endpoint reached and held by resolve. The body defends the lower weight **asymmetrically** —
harder against loss than against gain — so a maintenance plan that assumes willpower will close
the gap is mis-specified. What this licenses is a shift from a one-time target to **structural or
continued support**: a standing environmental or pharmacological prop that does not depend on
daily resolve, because it is working against a biological headwind that does not tire. Reading
regain as personal failure both mis-locates the problem and points at the wrong fix.

The defence is real for everyone — but for one group the whole sign of the advice flips.

## 9. For whom: the stratum ladder (and where the sign flips)

Who is asking changes the answer more than any single number does. Route (a) — baseline risk —
does most of the stratifying: absolute benefit scales with how much risk a person already carries,
so the same intervention is a big rock for one stratum and a refinement for another. Personalize
the *relative* effect only on positive evidence of effect modification, never on mechanism alone.
The ladder below is ordered by who is asking, not by intervention.

### Rung 1 — the metabolically-at-risk, central-obese adult

The big rock. Largest absolute benefit, because baseline risk is highest and route (a) does most
of the work. The target is not the scale number but type 2 diabetes remission and liver-fat
regression — drawing the pathogenic intra-organ depot back down with sustained negative energy
balance
[@taylor2015pft].
Depot, not weight, is what is being steered.

### Rung 2 — the metabolically-healthy, higher-BMI adult

A small, slow, time-latent excess. Metabolically-healthy obesity is not durably benign, but its
excess mortality risk emerges only after roughly a decade and is modest — RR 1.24 (1.02-1.55) at
>=10-year follow-up (section 2)
[@kramer2013mho].
So the framing is continued but non-urgent attention; depot and trajectory matter more than the
current number.

### Rung 3 — the elderly: the sign flip

Here the sign of the recommendation inverts, and this is a first-class stratum finding, not a
caveat. Intentional weight loss can strip muscle and bone the person cannot spare, and low muscle
mass predicts higher all-cause mortality — pooled ASMI standardized mean difference -0.18 (-0.23
to -0.12), observational, so it tracks rather than proves
[@santana2021].
The goal therefore becomes *protecting* the body — resistance training plus adequate protein to
preserve lean mass — not shrinking it. See [[Low Muscle Mass and Mortality]], [[Big Rocks (Elderly)]].

### Methods, named for what each moves

The methods are not interchangeable; each moves a different thing, and the route (a) add-on
(preserve lean mass) is universal across strata.

| Method | What it moves |
|---|---|
| Diet / total diet replacement | T2D remission + rapid depot drainage (the twin-cycle drawdown) |
| Resistance training + protein | lean-mass preservation — the universal add-on, every stratum |
| Exercise | visceral fat + weight maintenance; weak *sole* loss lever (compensation) |
| GLP-1 / bariatric | largest sustained loss + a CV-event signal at a lean-mass cost -> [[GLP-1 Drugs]] |

The GLP-1 / bariatric route earns its own page for the benefit-risk appraisal and is not absorbed
here. Named for the ladder: it delivers the largest sustained loss, and in secondary prevention
SELECT cut MACE (hazard ratio \~0.80)
[@lincoff2023]
— an RCT result, so *cut*, not merely *tracked*. The cost is lean mass: semaglutide sheds about
-5.44 kg of it, though lean *as a proportion* of total weight actually rises (\~+1.81%) because fat
leaves faster
[@laverde2026].
Which face governs is the stratum question above; the full appraisal is [[GLP-1 Drugs]].

## 10. Gaps and the bottom line

### The gaps [G] — absence of evidence, no direction inferred

Each item below is a hole in what the wiki holds. Absence is stated; no sign is inferred into any
of them.

- **Rate-versus-regain, muscle-loss-by-speed, and diet-deficit vs exercise-deficit body
  composition** — no gold meta-analysis is held. The wiki cannot say how loss speed trades against
  muscle retained, nor whether a diet deficit and an exercise deficit reshape the body differently.
- **Clean causal direction between fatty liver (NAFLD) and type 2 diabetes** — no
  Mendelian-randomization umbrella is held. The depot spine is internally coherent, but the arrow
  between the two is not causally pinned.
- **Whether *losing* fat (rather than carrying less of it) lowers cancer incidence** — the evidence
  for the loss step is thinner than the adiposity->incidence association it would have to ride on;
  the wiki cannot assert the loss inherits the risk reduction.
- **Weight cycling -> hard outcomes** — a confounding-prone exposure with no universally accepted
  definition and no gold outcome meta-analysis
  [@montani2015cycling].
  The absence of that meta-analysis is itself the finding: the wiki cannot quantify whether cycling
  per se moves a patient-important outcome.

### The bottom line

Depot beats the scale: where fat sits — overflowing into liver, pancreas and viscera past a
personal threshold — tracks cardiometabolic risk better than total mass or BMI. Losing fat buys
diabetes remission, liver-fat regression, and — where the loss is large and sustained — longer
life. But lifestyle weight loss is *not* proven to prevent heart attacks. So preserve muscle and
plan for maintenance from the start, because the benefit decays on the timescale of stopping. And
for the elderly the instruction inverts: protect the body rather than shrink it.
