**Processed food is not one exposure, and treating it as one is the mistake to avoid.** The
word covers rice, oats, wholegrain bread, plain yoghurt and cheese in the same breath as soda,
crisps and cured meat. The ultra-processed (UPF/NOVA) class attaches a single risk number to that
whole grab-bag — and inside it, some items track worse health while others are neutral or even
protective.

**On disease and death the honest verdict is: broad, consistent associations at low certainty.**
Every hard-outcome study of UPF is observational, so it cannot separate processing from the sugar,
salt, fat, low fibre, poverty and inactivity that travel with it. A randomized trial nails one thing
down: at matched nutrient content, a heavily processed diet drove people to eat \~500
more calories a day and gain weight. That is a real effect on a surrogate (intake and weight), not
proof that processing causes heart disease or cancer.

**So act on what you can see, not on the label.** The measurable culprits — energy density, soft
fast-to-eat texture, low protein, added sugar and sugary drinks, sodium, cured/charred meat — are
already ranked exposures a shopper can target. *Avoid ultra-processed food* is a fuzzy shorthand for
those, and a modest lever besides: it sits near the bottom of the dietary-risk rankings, well below
the big rocks of smoking, obesity, heavy drinking and inactivity.

## Split *processed* into aspects and categories before asking whether it is bad

The lay question hides several separate decisions. Break *processing* into the aspects that could
carry harm — **matrix disruption, additives, refining/substrate change, energy density and
hyper-palatability, and processing contaminants** — and break the exposure into the specific
**categories** that differ in evidence. Every defensible claim attaches to one aspect or one
category, never to *processing* bare.

The NOVA *ultra-processed* class is the paradigm case of a category boundary that may not carry its
own information. It is defined by *«industrial formulations primarily composed of chemically modified
substances extracted from foods, along with additives»* [@lane2024upf] — that is, by *manufacturing*, not composition. UPF is a large share of
intake (42-58% of energy in Australia and the US) [@lane2024upf], and it pools foods that behave oppositely.

Lane's own data show it. Within a type-2-diabetes meta-analysis, some UPF subcategories carried
higher risk while others were **inversely associated** — *«ultra-processed cereals, dark/wholegrain
bread, packaged sweet and savoury snacks, fruit based products and yoghurt, and dairy based
desserts»* [@lane2024upf]. A category that pools
things that help and things that harm produces an average describing no actual food -> [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]].

NNR (a gold-tier body) names the same defect at the point of use: many staple foods — infant
formula, bakery wholegrain breads, yoghurt, fish and vegetable products — land inside or outside the
category *«depending on their formulation and processing»* [@nnr2023]. Even a perfectly informative boundary is useless if a shopper cannot
tell what is inside it.

## One inpatient trial shows processing itself drives overeating — on a surrogate

The single piece of randomized causal evidence is Hall 2019 — keep it sharply separate
from the confounded cohorts. Twenty weight-stable adults (BMI 27) spent 28 days on a metabolic ward,
two weeks each on an ultra-processed and an unprocessed diet in random order. The meals were *«designed
to be well matched across diets for total calories, energy density, macronutrients, ﬁber, sugars, and
sodium, but widely differing in the percentage of calories derived from ultra-processed versus
unprocessed foods»* [@hall2019], and eaten
ad libitum.

Hall's result is the causal foothold: on the ultra-processed diet people ate **508 ± 106 kcal/day more**
(p = 0.0001) and **gained 0.9 ± 0.3 kg**, versus losing 0.9 kg on the unprocessed diet [@hall2019]. So processing does independent work on
energy intake even when total composition is matched — moderate certainty, but on a **surrogate**
(intake and weight over two weeks), not a hard outcome.

Two things keep this honest. First, simple palatability is **not** the explanation: hunger, fullness and
pleasantness ratings did not differ between the diets [@hall2019]. Second, the effect runs through **identifiable properties**, not NOVA
magic — the ultra-processed meals were eaten faster (+17 kcal/min), had 85% higher non-beverage energy
density, and diluted protein (modelled to explain *«at most 50%»* of the gap) [@hall2019]. n=20, one trial, two-week arms: it establishes
*that* processing moves intake and points at *which* properties, without isolating a single lever.

That one trial is not a gap in this page's reading but a gap in the **field**. Aramburu 2024, the first
systematic review restricted to *randomized* UPF trials, searched to 2024 and found only **four** — Hall's
feeding trial plus three *educational* interventions that changed dietary advice (and much else) rather
than processing at matched composition, and every one at high risk of bias
[@aramburu2024]. Aramburu **includes Hall** as
one of the four, so it is not a second witness confirming him — it is the systematic accounting that shows
Hall essentially *is* the direct-feeding RCT base
[inferred from @aramburu2024; @hall2019].
Aramburu itself declines to attribute the overeating to processing *per se*. The upshot is an evidence
**state**, not a verdict: for processing-as-cause on hard outcomes the randomized base is one small
surrogate trial amid three confounded ones — **insufficient, not refuted**, exactly the low certainty
this page carries.

## On disease and death, the evidence is broad association at low certainty

For the hard outcomes, the corpus rests on Lane's 2024 umbrella review — 14 meta-analyses, 45 pooled
analyses, nearly 9.9 million people. It is **observational by necessity**: the authors found no
pooled trials, and note that long-term RCTs of a suspected-harmful whole diet on cancer or CVD *«will
not be possible, for obvious ethical reasons»* [@lane2024upf]. The breadth is real; the causal certainty is not.

Lane grades every association twice, and the two axes come apart. The strongest signal by
credibility — CVD-related mortality, RR 1.50 (1.37-1.63), rated *convincing* — is GRADE **very low**.
Only 4 of 45 analyses reach GRADE *moderate*; 22 are low and 19 very low [@lane2024upf]. A convincing-and-consistent association is not strong
evidence of causation. The honest one-line summary is: *broad, consistent associations at
low-to-very-low certainty.*

The magnitudes bound the decision. Class I/II relative effects run RR/OR **1.12 to 1.66** — for
type-2 diabetes RR 1.12 per 10% UPF, for all-cause mortality RR 1.21 (1.15-1.27) [@lane2024upf]. Against modest baseline risks these are small-to-
moderate, not big-rock, effects.

And the bias screens fired on the *headline* outcomes, not just the weak ones: excess-significance
bias in 9 of 28 analyses (including all-cause mortality, obesity, T2D) and small-study effects in 5 of
28 [@lane2024upf]. A nominally-convincing
association can still carry the fingerprints of publication bias.

## Two gold bodies read the same numbers and split on what to do

The evidence does not determine the recommendation, and that disagreement is itself the finding. Two
gold-tier bodies read the same associations and reach opposite conclusions.

- **Lane 2024** reads the umbrella as a *«rationale to develop and evaluate»* population and public-
  health measures against UPF — a call to build and test them, not a flat prescription — because the
  association is broad, consistent, and *«adjusting for diet quality or patterns does not change the
  consistent evidence»* [@lane2024upf].
- **NNR 2023** declines to recommend against UPF as a class, judging that the NOVA category *«does not
  add to the already existing food classifications and recommendations»* [@nnr2023].

The gap between them is a real appraisal move, not hand-waving. Adjusting for a diet-quality
*pattern* is not the same as matching the *nutrient profile* — the very sugar/salt/fat/fibre content
that makes a food both ultra-processed and nutrient-poor. NNR names the collinearity
directly: most UPF is *«energy dense... high in added or free sugars, salt and total fat/saturated
fat, and low in fibre»* — every one a variable it already regulates [@nnr2023]. If the category's predictive power runs through variables you already
target, targeting the category too is double-counting.

The Hall RCT partly breaks the tie — **for intake only**. At matched total composition, processing
still moved intake, so on that surrogate the collinearity argument fails. But it fails *through the
sub-components NNR named* (energy density, eating rate, protein), so the lesson holds: name the
property, not the label. The hard-outcome boundary stays composition-confounded.

## The harm attaches to properties you can see, not to a NOVA label

Route each finding to the aspect or category doing the work, and judge every change against its
realistic replacement.

- **Energy density and hyper-palatability / texture.** The proximate drivers of overeating in Hall —
  high non-beverage energy density and fast-to-eat soft texture [@hall2019]. These are measurable and directly targetable.
- **Added sugar, in liquid form especially.** Swapping free sugars for other carbohydrate at equal
  energy moves weight by **0.04 kg (-0.04 to 0.13) — null**: the harm is the added energy, not a
  sugar-specific toxin [@who2015]. Sugary
  drinks add poorly-compensated liquid calories — per 250 mL/day, type-2-diabetes RR 1.19 (1.13-1.25),
  obesity 1.12, all monotone over the studied range [@qin2020] (observational; residual confounding not excluded).
- **Refining / matrix disruption.** The cleanest direct test on hard outcomes is null: SACN found
  refined vs whole grain gave **RR 1.00 (0.98-1.01)** for both cardiovascular events and type-2
  diabetes [@sacn2015]. Any benefit tracks *fibre
  content*, not the fact of refinement — so *refined therefore harmful* is not supported at the
  hard-outcome level.
- **Curing and high-heat cooking.** This is where a processing step has a graded carcinogen mechanism:
  processed meat RR 1.16 (1.08-1.26) per 50 g/day for colorectal cancer, and the mechanism attaches to
  the processing itself — «Processed meats are a source of nitrate and nitrite, both associated with
  N-nitroso compounds, which in animal models have been shown to induce cancer development», with
  high-temperature cooking adding heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons
  [@wcrf2018] — though even here
  NutriRECS grades the same evidence low-to-very-low with small absolute effects (\~1 to 8 fewer cancers
  per 1000 over a lifetime) [@nutrirecs2019].

## The additive question is mostly unanswered — and unanswered is not safe

Additives are the aspect the public worries about most and the evidence covers least. *Thin patient-important-outcome evidence* is the **insufficient-evidence** state, not a clean bill of
health — so calibrate per compound, and neither assert harm from mechanism nor dismiss a named one
.

- **Non-sugar sweeteners.** WHO suggests against using them for weight or NCD control (conditional,
  low certainty). The trial weight benefit only appears versus sugar and fades to null in true
  sugar-replacement trials (-0.61 kg, ns); versus water there is no benefit [@who2023nonsugar]. A causal glycaemic *mechanism* now exists — Suez 2022 showed
  saccharin and sucralose *«significantly impaired glycemic responses»*, confirmed by microbiome
  transplant into germ-free mice [@suez2022] — but on a two-week surrogate, personalized, not on diabetes or CVD.
- **Aspartame specifically.** The hazard/risk split resolves the scary headline. IARC calls it
  *«possibly carcinogenic to humans»* (Group 2B, the weakest positive tier, on limited evidence) [@iarc2023]; JECFA reaffirmed the ADI of 0-40 mg/kg and found
  that realistic dietary exposure estimates «do not exceed the ADI» [@jecfa2023]. A
  weak *can-it-ever* hazard flag is consistent with *safe at the doses people consume*
  -> [[Non-Sugar Sweeteners]].
- **Emulsifiers, preservatives, contaminants.** Emulsifiers (CMC, polysorbate-80) sit at
  mechanism/animal data with no hard-outcome evidence — genuinely *not yet*. Cooking
  contaminants (advanced glycation end products) move some surrogate lipids but leave weight, glucose,
  HbA1c and blood pressure unchanged, with no hard-outcome trial [@baye2017].

Whether to avoid a named-mechanism additive precautionarily is a personal (layer-3) weighting — a
legitimate one, and distinct from the wiki asserting harm.

## Against the big levers, cutting ultra-processed food is a small, over-discussed one

Attention runs inversely to effect size here. The most-litigated dietary exposures rank near the
*bottom* of the attributable-burden league table: GBD reports that *«high consumption of red meat,
processed meat, trans fat, and sugar-sweetened beverages were towards the bottom in ranking of dietary
risks for deaths and DALYs for most high-population countries»* [@afshin2019]. The top dietary levers are boring under-eaten staples — whole
grains, fruit, nuts.

UPF is among the most-discussed exposures and its effect sizes are modest and confounded — the
anti-signal at full strength. It also correlates strongly with the true big rocks (obesity, poor diet
quality, low activity), so much of its apparent ranking weight is likely already captured by exposures
higher in the hierarchy. For anyone with a dominant exposure unaddressed — smoking,
obesity, heavy alcohol, near-total inactivity — no precision about processing changes what to do next
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

## *Processed therefore bad* earns the same scrutiny as *natural therefore good*

The naturalistic fallacy runs both ways, and both directions are weak. *Recently-bred / processed / unnatural, therefore bad* carries no more health information than *natural / traditional, therefore good*; a food's origin is not evidence about its outcomes. Judge the current product on its measured
properties.

Identity also drifts under a constant name. Today's bread, yoghurt or breakfast cereal is not the
version behind decades-old evidence, and the NOVA label reassigns the *same* food across the boundary
on manufacturing details a consumer cannot read off the plate [@nnr2023]. Specify the exposure — the fortified cereal, the sweetened yoghurt,
the cured meat — not the word *processed*.

## What the evidence still cannot tell you

Three gaps are structural, not oversights, and naming them is part of the answer.

- **Additive outcome evidence.** For most non-caloric additives the human patient-important-outcome
  evidence is mechanism/animal or short-term only. This is a gap, not a null.
- **Aspect attribution in humans.** Beyond the intake channel Hall demonstrated, how much of the UPF
  signal is matrix vs additives vs delivered nutrients is not cleanly partitioned in people
.
- **Long-latency and trajectory outcomes.** The UPF RCT is short (intake, weight). Hard-endpoint
  causal data do not exist, because a whole-food-matrix intervention cannot be blinded and the
  blindable isolate is a different exposure [@lane2024upf]. The shape-of-decline outcome many people care about most is unmeasured.

## The bottom line

- **Do target the properties, not the label.** Cut energy-dense, fast-to-eat, low-protein foods;
  sugary drinks; excess sodium; cured and charred meat. These are the measurable culprits a shopper
  can act on.
- **Do treat the intake effect as real and the disease effect as unproven.** Reducing heavily
  processed food genuinely lowers ad-libitum calories and weight (trial-grade, surrogate); its link
  to mortality and disease is observational and low-certainty.
- **Do keep the big rocks first.** If smoking, obesity, heavy drinking or inactivity is in play, UPF
  precision is not the next move.
- **Do not read *processed* as one thing.** Wholegrain bread, plain yoghurt and lentils are not soda
  and cured meat; do not let a category average decide a food-specific choice.
- **Do calibrate additives per compound.** Thin evidence means uncertain, not safe — and a
  precautionary personal choice is legitimate without the evidence asserting harm.

**Caveats.** Every hard-outcome figure here is observational and low-certainty; the one randomized
result is a two-week surrogate in 20 people. The corpus grades sources for coherence and
source-fidelity, never for whether the recommendation actually improves a realized outcome — that
loop is open. Guidance itself disagrees (Lane vs NNR), which is a statement about the state of the
appraisal, not a settled verdict.
