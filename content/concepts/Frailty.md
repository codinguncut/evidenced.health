Nucleus of the `frailty` cluster. Frailty is the older-adult syndrome of **reduced physiological
reserve** — the state in which «a number of physiological systems are close to, or pass, the threshold
of symptomatic clinical failure ... the frail person is at increased risk of disability and death from
minor external stresses» [@vermeiren2016frailty].
Two gold SR-MAs, read together, answer the decision-relevant question no single one poses: frailty is
**both a potent prognostic marker AND a modifiable target** — so it is a *lever*, not merely a
risk-stratifier.

<div class="recent-update" data-last-updated="2026-10-28">

## The emergent claim — prognosis + modifiability = a lever, not a fate

[inferred from @vermeiren2016frailty; @racey2021frailtypa]
Vermeiren establishes frailty *predicts* a wide set of patient-important outcomes; Racey establishes
physical-activity interventions *improve* frailty status and its functional consequences. Vermeiren
itself flags the missing leg — «frailty is a dynamic process ... More research on the dynamic process
and reversibility of frailty are essential» — and Racey is (part of) the answer. The joined implication:
identifying frailty is decision-changing because the identified stratum has both high baseline risk
(large absolute benefit available) *and* a lever that moves some of those outcomes: in Racey, mobility,
ADL, quality of life, cognition and frailty status moved, falls were not significantly reduced (RR 0.80,
0.51-1.26, 7 studies, very low certainty — unestablished, not shown absent), and mortality was not measured
(detail below). This is what a RAG over either
paper alone misses — Vermeiren reads as "frailty is bad prognosis," Racey as "exercise helps the frail";
the *act-on-it* conclusion is the composite.

## What frailty predicts (Vermeiren — prognostic leg, observational)

[@vermeiren2016frailty]
31 prospective studies, 158,764 community-dwelling adults >=65. (Pre)frail vs robust (pooled OR; HR/RR) —
the source's overall estimates pool frail-vs-robust and prefrail-vs-robust contrasts (the abstract reports them as
(pre)frailty estimates); where split out — e.g. mortality — frail RR 2.01 [1.82-2.22] vs prefrail RR 1.47
[1.32-1.62]. The cognitive-decline row pools 2 studies mixing both contrasts (corrected 2026-10-06:
*Frail vs robust* -> *(pre)frail vs robust*, Vermeiren chunk 01):

| Outcome (patient-important) | OR [95% CI] | HR/RR [95% CI] |
|---|---|---|
| Premature mortality | 2.34 [1.77-3.09] | 1.83 [1.68-1.98] |
| BADL disability | 2.05 [1.73-2.44] | 1.62 [1.50-1.76] |
| IADL disability | 2.52 [2.08-3.06] | — |
| Physical limitation | 2.58 [1.85-3.62] | 1.46 [1.37-1.56] |
| Hospitalization | 1.82 [1.53-2.15] | 1.18 [1.10-1.28] |
| Institutionalization | 1.69 [1.02-2.81] | 1.65 [1.48-1.84] |
| Falls | 1.70 [1.18-2.44] | 1.24 [1.12-1.37] |
| Fractures | 2.78 [1.30-5.99] | 1.37 [1.21-1.54] |
| Cognitive decline | — | 1.47 [1.23-1.76] |

- **Prefrail is already predictive** — for mortality, prefrail OR 1.76 and frail OR 2.55, which the
  authors read as «a similar elevated risk of mortality» (the risk ratios, 1.47 vs 2.01, do separate)
  [@vermeiren2016frailty], so frailty is a spectrum, and the *prefrail* state is the earlier,
  more-remediable handle.
- **Prognosis, not cause.** These are observational associations, maximally adjusted (age/sex/baseline
  health) but confounded, with high heterogeneity (I2 95-98% for mortality). Frailty here is a *risk
  marker*; the causal test is the intervention leg.

## Frailty is modifiable (Racey — intervention leg, RCT/GRADE)

[@racey2021frailtypa]
26 studies (24 RCTs), 8022 identified prefrail/frail adults >=65, GRADE-rated. Physical-activity
interventions vs true control: «Significant effects were found for mobility (standardized mean
difference [SMD] 0.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.37 to 0.83), activities of daily living (SMD 0.50,
95% CI 0.15 to 0.84), cognitive function (SMD 0.35, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.61), quality of life (SMD 0.60,
95% CI 0.13 to 1.07) and frailty (SMD –1.29, 95% CI –2.22 to –0.36; risk ratio 0.58, 95% CI 0.36 to
0.93), with moderate certainty of evidence.» Effects large for frailty, medium for mobility/ADL/QoL,
small for cognition.

- **What works:** muscle-strengthening and *mixed* (aerobic + strength) are the studied, effective
  categories — but read the frailty-outcome subgroup bases with care: muscle-strengthening frailty
  RR 0.21 [0.10-0.43] rests on a **single trial (n=66)** and mixed RR 0.72 [0.63-0.83] on 3 trials, both
  moderate GRADE. The tight CI on the 0.21 is one small study, not a robust category effect. Aerobic-only
  and balance/flexibility-only are under-studied here — a gap, not a null.
- **Dose the intervention DOWN for the frail.** «recommended physical activity may be too advanced or
  too intense for a frailer population, put-ting them at risk for falls and injuries» — the general PA
  guideline does not transport to this stratum; the reviewed programmes were typically supervised
  and adjusted (\~29 weeks, 30-60 min, 1-4x/week) — Racey notes «as was evident from the high level of
  supervision in the studies in this review» that programmes should engage professionals able to adjust
  protocols [@racey2021frailtypa]. Racey's harms read is narrative: interventions «do not appear to introduce undue harm» [@racey2021frailtypa]; adherence is
  poor in the real world (fear of falling, low self-belief) — adherence is part of the effect.

**How much resistance-training volume the frail need is not estimated (gap).** The one held
network meta-analysis of RT volume in adults >=60 (Radaelli, 151 trials) found a low weekly volume not
outperformed by a high one on most function and size outcomes (higher volume did better on fast walking speed, and
had larger strength point estimates), but in a pool 78.8% physically healthy; frail
trials numbered 5, and the physically-impaired subgroup (sarcopenia, frailty, mobility limitation) produced significant
estimates for few cells: low and moderate volume on knee-extension and leg-press 1RM, low and high volume
on isometric strength, high volume on timed up and go, and moderate volume on lean mass. The authors: «While resistance train- ing can prevent muscle wasting in older
adults with sar- copenia or frailty [222–224], the most effective resistance training dosage for this
group remains to be determined.» [@radaelli2024rtvol]
The authors translate the low band's median (\~12 weekly lower-limb sets) as «equivalent to two sessions
per week and two sets» «per exercise for three lower-limb resistance training exer- cises», which they say
«has proven highly effective in enhancing physical function measured by timed up and go and 6-min walk
test but not fast walking speed in healthy older adults.» On
strength they add: «Interestingly, both physically healthy and physically impaired participants derived
significantly large effects from moderate-to-high resistance training vol- ume, which is important to
preserve muscle strength for those targeting healthy ageing and achieve a greater safety margin before the
threshold for disabilities.» [@radaelli2024rtvol]
So the not-outperformed finding is not carried to this stratum as evidence; the supervised, adjusted
programmes above remain the held frame. In the impaired subgroup even low volume gave significant 1RM and
isometric strength gains, so a low-volume start (the authors' Discussion translation of \~12 weekly lower-limb sets: two sets per exercise, three
lower-limb exercises, twice a week, inside a supervised programme; their Conclusions give eight exercises
for the same volume, probably a full-body count — see the RT page — and the held general default covers
all major muscle groups) is a reasonable cheap default to
begin with. It rests on these subgroup cells and on a translation the authors give for healthy older
adults; it is not a trial-tested optimum, and the dose for the frail stays unestimated (whole-pool strength
certainty is already very low, and the subgroup is ungraded; 5 frail trials). Because moderate and high volume also gave large strength effects in this
subgroup, the low start is a floor to progress from, not a ceiling.

## Reconciliation — target the measurable COMPONENTS, read "frailty" as the composite that moves

[inferred from @racey2021frailtypa]
The wiki's Big-Rocks reading is that frailty *itself cannot be targeted — it is too ill-defined*, and
one should aim at measurable components (strength, balance, aerobic capacity). Racey *refines rather
than overturns* this: the effective interventions ARE component training (resistance / mixed), and
Racey's own binding limitation — frailty «was measured infrequently ... only 4 of the 23 studies ...
reported frailty outcomes after the intervention», with heterogeneous, sometimes unvalidated frailty
definitions — directly corroborates the "too ill-defined" concern. So: **train the measurable
components; frailty-status improvement is the down-stream composite that follows, but is measured too
rarely/inconsistently to serve as the primary endpoint.** The RR 0.58 frailty-reversal figure rests on
a thin 4-study base and should be read with that caveat, not as a robust treat-to-target number.

## Stratification and decision relevance

- **Route (a) — baseline risk (the safe workhorse).** Frailty identification places a person in a
  high-baseline-risk stratum, so the *absolute* benefit of any intervention scales up even without a
  subgroup claim -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. This is the legitimate use of the
  prognostic magnitudes: not "frailty causes death," but "the frail have more to gain per unit of a
  working lever."
- **Frailty vs sarcopenia (B-disambiguation).** They overlap but are distinct objects: **sarcopenia**
  is the muscle-specific disorder (low strength/mass/performance ->
  [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]]); **frailty** is the *multisystem* reserve-capacity syndrome of which sarcopenia (weakness,
  slow gait, weight loss) is a major physical component but not the whole. Physical-focus frailty
  instruments (Fried phenotype) overlap sarcopenia heavily; multidomain / deficit-accumulation
  instruments add cognitive/psychosocial/medical deficits sarcopenia excludes. Vermeiren's mechanism
  list names «sarcopenia» as one driver, linking the two clusters without merging them.
- **Falls in the frail — a distinction, not a tension.** Vermeiren: frailty predicts falls (OR 1.70).
  But Racey finds PA does NOT significantly reduce falls in the *already-frail* (RR 0.80 [0.51-1.26],
  very low certainty), whereas [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]] (the Sherrington 2019 Cochrane review) finds
  HIGH-certainty fall reduction in *general* community-dwelling older adults. Not-joined check (ii):
  different population (identified-frail vs general older adult) and different certainty (very-low vs
  high) — consistent once matched, so a distinction, not a filed tension. Read: fall-prevention exercise
  is best-evidenced *before* deep frailty; in the frail the falls signal is uncertain on thin data.
  - **The residential-care stratum gives a parallel, but it is a setting, not the syndrome
    `[2026-10-28, Sherrington 2017]`.** Pooling 15 comparisons of exercise alone in care homes (nursing
    homes and hostels), the 2017 falls meta-analysis found a rate ratio of 0.90 (0.72-1.12, I2 68%). None of
    its sensitivity analyses firms this up, and every interval spans 1: fixed-effect 0.98 (0.88-1.08, 15
    comparisons), the three n>200 comparisons 1.01 (0.77-1.33), and the four lower-risk-of-bias (PEDro >=7)
    comparisons 0.82 (0.57-1.19) [@sherrington2017falls].
    The authors: «The pooled estimate of the effect of exercise on falls in residential care settings was more variable in the sensitivity analysis, reﬂect- ing greater uncertainty about the effectiveness of exercise on falls in this population (table 2).»
    [@sherrington2017falls]. They concluded that
    «exercise as a single intervention cannot be recommended as a fall prevention strategy at present»,
    noting that several care-home trials which did reduce falls included other strategies alongside
    exercise. The same passage keeps exercise for function there: «There are other beneﬁts
    of maintaining function in this population such as enhanced quality of life, decreased care needs
    and fewer complications such as chest infections and pressure injuries and appropriately designed
    exercise programmes may be able to achieve this»
    [@sherrington2017falls].
    The wiki's reading of the default for a care home: exercise for function, and for falls exercise as
    one component of a multi-component programme (several care-home trials that did reduce falls also
    included other strategies, «including education of staff and attention to the environment»
    [@sherrington2017falls]),
    whose optimal form the authors say is not yet established. The held multifactorial and
    multicomponent programme evidence on [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]] (a network
    meta-analysis of community trials) comes from community-dwelling populations and is not transported
    to care homes. Care-home residents are not a frailty-instrument sample, so this does not
    test Racey's population (not-joined check (ii): setting, not syndrome). Racey's own pool includes
    care-home residents, and its trial overlap with the 2017 residential pool is unchecked, so the two
    agree without giving independent backing: the general
    community result is not the anchor for the most dependent older adults, since in-setting trials
    exist; there the falls effect of exercise alone is unestablished on heterogeneous data whose
    fixed-effect and large-trial analyses sit near null — insufficient evidence of an effect, not a shown
    absence.
    [inferred from @sherrington2017falls; @racey2021frailtypa]
- **Route (a) assumes the relative effect transports to the frail; for BP drugs the trials
  under-represented them (G-gap) `[2026-10-27, Rahimi]`.** In what its authors call «the most detailed
  analysis of blood-pressure-lowering treatment effect by age and blood pressure to date» [@rahimi2021bpage]
  (BPLTTC, 51 RCTs, 358,707 people [@rahimi2021bpage]), they state the limit themselves: «Relatedly, because of the typically restricted
  eligibility criteria of RCTs, other groups such as those with a high multimorbidity and polypharmacy
  burden, frail individuals, and individuals living in institutions have been under-represented.» and «The
  generalisability of the findings to these highly relevant and growing patient groups remains
  uncertain.» [@rahimi2021bpage] So the *more to
  gain per unit of a working lever* reading above is established only where the lever's relative effect
  has been estimated in people like this; in this pool frail people were under-represented and not analysed
  separately, so the estimate is transported to them. The analysis pools no adverse-event outcome at all [searched: adverse / orthostatic / syncope /
  hypotension across chunks 01-02], so harms whose baseline risk is higher in the frail (falls: Vermeiren OR 1.70 for (pre)frail vs robust, above;
  orthostatic symptoms, plausibly but not sourced here) are unquantified there; whether the drug-attributable harm is larger in the frail is as untested
  as whether the benefit transports. The authors cite, but do not pool, external randomized harm data: «For
  instance, in a subgroup analysis of the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT), the total
  serious adverse event rates were similar in the two study groups.», plus cited analyses showing no
  worsening in function or quality of life in older people, and read their own pooled deaths as «the lack
  of excess mortality risk in older groups suggests that harmful fatal effects of the treatment are
  unlikely in any age group.» [@rahimi2021bpage]
  That is about fatal harm only, and the held text does not say whether any of the cited harm data
  addresses frail people specifically. The authors' own stance on people already treated: «However, in the
  absence of any strong evidence for excess harms from randomised studies, we believe that it is
  appropriate for patients who are on blood-pressure-lowering pharmacotherapy to continue receiving such
  treatment if well-tolerated and when prevention of fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular events remains of
  importance.» [@rahimi2021bpage] By age, the
  relative-absolute split held in the same data (relative effect no larger, absolute effect larger
  in older bands); frailty is a separate stratum the age bands do not capture. The trial
  the authors name to address it is ATEMPT (multimorbid, polymedicated)
  [@rahimi2021bpage]; no result is held. Detail:
  [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]] (*By age*).

## Limits — the open loop

[inferred from @vermeiren2016frailty; @racey2021frailtypa]

- **No closed causal chain to mortality.** The prognosis is observational; the intervention evidence
  moves frailty status / function / QoL (moderate certainty), NOT (in these two sources) mortality. No
  RCT here shows that *reducing frailty* cuts death — the loop stays open, as with the sarcopenia
  cluster's mass->mortality gap.
- **Definitional heterogeneity is the binding constraint** on both legs (many instruments, no single
  operationalization) — it inflates prognostic heterogeneity and makes "frailty" a noisy trial endpoint.
- **Durability unknown** — Racey's outcomes were measured immediately post-intervention; the frailty
  levers must be sustained (adherence), and long-term effect is unstudied.
