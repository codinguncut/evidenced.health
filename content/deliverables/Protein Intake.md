## 1. The four questions inside *how much protein?*

*How much protein should I eat?* reads like one question with one answer. It is
four, and each rides a different curve: how much to avoid deficiency, how much to
build or keep muscle, what food to get it from, and who is doing the asking. The
familiar target numbers each answer just one of these. Reading a number lifted
from one curve as the answer to another is the mistake that drives most of the
confusion here.

The bottom line, and the so-what:

- For most adults already eating a normal amount, more protein is a *small*
  lever. Resistance training is what actually builds and holds muscle; protein
  is its adjunct, not a substitute for the work.
- The amount matters far more for three groups: people lifting to gain muscle,
  people dieting in an energy deficit, and older adults. For them, muscle,
  strength, and staying on their own feet are what is at stake, so undershooting
  has a real cost.
- There is no single best dose the evidence can point to. There is a sensible
  *range* — a floor below which you lose ground, and a ceiling above which more
  buys nothing. Because undershooting costs more than overshooting for these
  groups, when in doubt round up.
- On living longer, the amount barely moves the needle; the *source* moves it
  modestly. Shifting protein-bearing foods toward plants tracks lower mortality,
  though that evidence is observational and the signal may belong to the plants,
  not the protein.
- The old worry that more protein damages the kidneys does not hold for healthy
  kidneys. It flips only where kidney disease is already established.

So the useful move is to fix *which* question you are asking before reaching for
a number. Start with the numbers people actually quote, because they answer
different questions.

## 2. Which number answers which question

Three protein figures circulate as if they were rival answers to one question.
They are not: they sit on different curves, built for different purposes, and
only make sense once each is put back where it came from.

- **0.8 g/kg body weight per day — the RDA.** This is a *deficiency floor*, not a
  target. It is the population-coverage number: the daily intake that keeps
  roughly 97% of healthy adults out of protein deficiency, set against
  nitrogen-balance data. PROT-AGE states the standard guidelines «specify the
  same recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for all adults: 0.8 g/kg BW/ d» and
  judges this «too low for older people» for maintaining muscle
  [@bauer2013]. It answers
  *how little can I get away with*, not *how much is good for me*.
- **\~1.6 g/kg body weight per day — the muscle number.** This is the region where
  the muscle-building return from added protein, on top of resistance training,
  flattens out: «Protein supplementation beyond total protein intakes of 1.62
  g/kg/day resulted in no further RET-induced gains in FFM»
  [@morton2018].
  Held loosely — the break point is «1.62 g protein/kg/day, p=0.079» with a wide
  95% CI of «(1.03 to 2.20)»
  [@morton2018],
  so it is a central estimate, not a sharp threshold. This is a *muscle-adaptation*
  curve, a different object from the deficiency floor.
- **The *1 g per pound* rule (\~2.2 g/kg body weight per day) — the gym rule of
  thumb.** A round conversion (one gram per pound is about 2.2 g/kg), not a
  finding. It sits at the top of the studied intakes, near the upper edge of the
  muscle number's own confidence interval (2.20) rather than out past it — so it
  is not reckless, but it is a ceiling-of-diminishing-returns figure being passed
  around as the everyday target.

The recurring error is treating the RDA as the answer to the muscle question:
a *floor* set against deficiency gets deployed as if it were a target for
building muscle. Those are different constructs on different curves — a minimum
to clear versus an amount to aim for — and the denominator matters too: name the
kilogram (Morton's 1.62 is grams per kg of *total* body mass), because the number
is undefined until you do. This is the distinction between repleting a deficiency
and enhancing a function -> [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]], and the
category slip of reading a *what-prevents-lack* number as a *what-you-ought-to-hit*
number -> [[The Descriptive-Normative Category Error]].

Take the muscle number first — it is the best-measured of them.

## 3. Amount -> muscle and strength: the dose-response

**Resistance training is the driver; protein is a modest adjunct.** Protein does not
build muscle — it lets the training build slightly more, and only up to a point. Pooling
49 RCTs and 1863 participants who trained for at least six weeks, protein
supplementation added, on top of the training and versus placebo, «2.49 kg (0.64, 4.33)»
to 1RM strength and «0.30 kg (0.09, 0.52)» of fat-free (lean) mass
[@morton2018]. It moved
neither total body mass («0.11 kg (−0.23, 0.46)» — no effect) nor maximal voluntary
contraction («SMD: 0.04 (-0.09, 0.16)» — no effect)
[@morton2018].

Read the magnitudes before the significance. A few kilograms on a 1RM and a third of a
kilogram of lean mass, accumulated over weeks of training, are *small* increments on top
of what the training itself delivers. This is RCT-grade evidence, but on **surrogates**
(muscle mass and strength), not on a patient-important endpoint.

### How much — a break near 1.6 g/kg, held loosely

The decision-relevant number is a dose-response knee: added protein beyond a total intake
of about 1.62 g/kg/day produced no further training-induced gain in fat-free mass. The
underlying mechanism is real — muscle-protein synthesis saturates, so past the point where
synthesis is maxed out extra protein has nothing to add. But the *chronic* break-point that
operationalises that mechanism as *1.62* is **not statistically established**: the biphasic
fit was non-significant («p=0.079»
[@morton2018]) and
explained little variance (R2 about 0.19). On the *baseline*-intake axis the data went the
other way — a straight, monotone line «explained significantly more variance than biphasic
regressions»
[@morton2018].

Two independent analyses point the same way. Tagawa's larger pool (a different lab, no
shared author) put a slope-drop near 1.3 g/kg but found the curve stayed positively
correlated with lean mass across the whole studied range, with no plateau
[@tagawa2020]; in a
resistance-training population its effect kept *rising* past 1.3. Refalo's meta-regression
in energy-restricted trainees likewise found a linear model beat quadratic and cubic — «a
97% probability that FFM change becomes more positive» as protein rose
[@refalo2025] — no knee.
By the appraisal rule that the burden falls on whoever asserts a knee to *locate* one, the
plateau is held as **weak**: mechanism-supported, not a demonstrated threshold. Do not read
*1.62* as a fact about the curve.

Two caveats bound the number further.

- **The tidy interval is a fingerprint, not a measurement.** The break-point's reported
  95% CI, 1.03-2.20, is almost exactly symmetric around 1.62 (the midpoint of 1.03 and
  2.20 is 1.615). That neatness is the signature of a Wald / normal-approximation interval
  — estimate +/- 1.96 x standard error, symmetry assumed in rather than found. A break-point
  is a **non-regular (boundary) parameter** — it indexes which observations fall on which
  side of the knee — and precisely there the normal approximation mis-covers, so its
  standard-error interval need not carry its nominal 95%. The upshot: *1.6* is *false
  precision*, not something the data pin down; the honest object is a region with a wide,
  possibly mis-shaped interval, whose symmetry is no proof of tight identification.
 -> [[Limits of Evidence]], [[The Underivable Optimum]]

- **The denominator is undefined until the kilogram is named.** Morton's intake is grams
  per kg of *total* body mass, so the target is g/kg-**total**, not g/kg of lean
  mass. In a lean person the two barely diverge; in someone carrying much more fat they
  differ roughly twofold, so *1.62 g/kg* is not even well-defined for them until the
  reference weight is stated. The muscle curve was sampled only over a narrow band (roughly
  1.0 to 2.2 g/kg-total) in healthy, non-energy-restricted, non-obese adults — the shape is
  described within that band, not beyond it.

A saturating supply curve like this cannot pin a best dose — only a region.

## 4. Why there is no point-optimum, and which way to round

The honest object here is not a peak. This evidence yields a **floor** (enough protein to
support the training adaptation), a **direction** (more still helps across the studied
range), and a **harm-ceiling** (a dose past which the exposure would harm) — but not a
single best intake -> [[The Underivable Optimum]]. Both the non-significant break and the
monotone fits that beat it say the same thing: there is no located optimum to aim at, so
naming one would launder a study-edge or a noise band into a target.

Given a region rather than a point, the decision turns on the *shape of the costs* around
it. For a hypertrophy objective the loss is one-sided. Under-dosing forfeits lean mass —
the very thing the training is for — while over-dosing, within the studied range up to
about 2.2 g/kg, is close to costless for healthy kidneys (higher protein does not change
kidney function in adults without established renal disease). With the plateau's true
location uncertain and the penalties asymmetric, the decision-relevant target therefore
biases **up** within the region — toward roughly 1.8-2.2 g/kg/day for hypertrophy — as a
rational response to that one-sided loss -> [[The Estimate-to-Action Gap]],
[[Better than What]].

This is a move about *where to round under uncertainty*, not a claim that the true optimum
sits higher. The optimum stays underivable; biasing up simply hedges against the expensive
side of a wide interval. Two boundaries hold it in check: the up-bias has a practical
ceiling (beyond about 2.2 g/kg the evidence thins and the costs of displacing other foods,
satiety, and expense rise), and it flips entirely for anyone with pre-existing kidney
disease, where high protein is a contraindication rather than a cheap overshoot.

That reasoning treats muscle mass as the goal — but mass is a surrogate; what does it buy?

## 5. Getting the amount right buys function — but mind the surrogate-to-outcome chain

Why get protein amount right at all? For older adults the payoff runs through muscle, and
here the outcome discipline matters. **Muscle mass and strength are surrogates; physical
function, sarcopenia, and mortality are the patient-important outcomes**
-> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. Lean or fat-free mass (FFM) is a *quantity* marker — and FFM is not skeletal
muscle, since it includes fluid. Strength is a *force* measure that predicts adverse
outcomes better than mass does. Function — falls avoided, gait speed, independence — is
what a person actually experiences. The chain from *raise protein* to *better function* is
worth taking seriously, but each link is a separate evidenced claim, and the last one is
not closed.

### Strength before mass — the EWGSOP2 reversal

The 2019 EWGSOP2 consensus rebuilt the sarcopenia case definition around exactly this
split -> [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]]. For three decades sarcopenia was defined
by low muscle *mass*; EWGSOP2 promotes **strength** to the primary criterion and demotes
mass to a *confirmatory* role, because «muscle strength comes to the forefront, as it is
recognised that strength is better than mass in predicting adverse outcomes»
[@cruzjentoft2019]. The
diagnosis is thus **strength-defined**: *probable* on low strength alone (enough to start
treatment without waiting for imaging), *confirmed* when low muscle quantity is added.
Mass leads nothing here — it is the noisier, harder-to-measure, worse predictor.

Protein enters as a named lever. EWGSOP2 lists **inadequate energy/protein intake** among
the causes of *secondary* sarcopenia
[@cruzjentoft2019], alongside
inactivity — so adequate protein plus resistance training is the modifiable handle, most
of all in the older adult whose per-meal threshold has risen.

### The surrogate-to-outcome chain — what predicts, and what is proven

Two markers on this chain do predict the hard outcome, in large observational bodies:

- **Low muscle mass predicts mortality.** de Santana's gold-tier SR-MA (9 cohorts,
  n=10,028, community-dwelling non-frail >=65) finds those who died carried \~0.18 SD less
  appendicular muscle mass — ASMI SMD -0.18, 95% CI -0.23 to -0.12
  [@santana2021]
  -> [[Low Muscle Mass and Mortality]]. A small effect, tight interval, and a between-group difference — not a
  per-unit hazard ratio.
- **Low grip strength predicts mortality.** Celis-Morales (UK Biobank, n=502,293) reports
  all-cause mortality hazard ratios per 5 kg *lower* grip of 1.20 (95% CI 1.17-1.23) in
  women and 1.16 (1.15-1.17) in men
  [@celismorales2018]
  -> [[Grip Strength and Mortality]]. Strength, the more outcome-relevant surrogate, is a broad predictor at
  scale and adds discrimination to a cheap office-based risk score.

**But a predictor is not a target.** Both associations are observational: low mass and low
grip may sit *downstream* of occult decline as much as upstream of death. No RCT shows that
*raising* mass or strength — by protein, training, or anything else — cuts mortality. The
loop is open: *raise protein -> better function -> lower mortality* is asserted through a
plausible chain, not proven end to end. Steer toward function and sarcopenia status, which
are patient-important in their own right; do not read the mortality numbers as a promise
that a stronger grip buys years.

So the amount is worth getting right for function. The next lever is not amount at all but
**source** — which is where the protein-quality and food-matrix questions come in.

## 6. Source: animal vs plant — a substitution, not a dose

On mortality, the signal is protein *source*, not amount. In Naghshi's meta-analysis of
32 prospective cohorts (all observational — associations, not causal effects), total
protein is only weakly inverse with all-cause mortality (HR 0.94, 0.89-0.99) and null on
both CVD (0.98) and cancer (0.98). Plant protein carries the benefit: inverse for
all-cause (HR 0.92, 0.87-0.97) and CVD (0.88, 0.80-0.96), null on cancer. Animal protein
is flatly null on all three (all-cause 1.00, 0.94-1.05)
[@naghshi2020]. The
total-protein inverse arm is the plant component showing through — it becomes
non-significant once studies adjust for fat and carbohydrate, while the plant signal
survives that adjustment.

**The effect is modest, and the shape is inverse-monotone with no U.** The one
significant dose-response: an additional 3% of energy from plant protein per day was
associated with roughly 5% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.95, 0.93-0.98)
[@naghshi2020].
Total and animal protein dose-responses were both flat. No
protein type shows an upper-arm harm, so there is no protective lower arm to adjudicate
— the [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]] hazard does not arise here. The open
question is only whether the inverse arm is causal, and being observational it is
unchecked by a genetic or trial design.

**Confidence is low, and stays low.** The cohorts pointing the same way are not
independent routes. Naghshi, Seidelmann, and Budhathoki share data and school lineage:
Willett co-authors across the protein and carbohydrate meta-analyses, they draw on
overlapping NHS/HPFS cohorts, and Budhathoki's Japanese cohort turns out to be one of
the 32 studies pooled *inside* Naghshi — so it is refinement, not replication. No
independent-corroboration credit is earned. A genuinely independent cohort, one not
among Naghshi's constituents, is still owed.

**The animal-protein null is a null on the *nutrient*, not a green light for the
*foods*.** Animal protein is not animal meat. The animal bucket pools fish, dairy, eggs,
and red meat, so a red-meat-specific effect diluted to null inside the mix cannot be
ruled out, and red/processed-meat food-level harm (established elsewhere) is untouched by
this null
[@naghshi2020].
Descending from the food to the nutrient *removes* the signal —
which is exactly [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]: name the food and its
mechanism, never the nutrient. The red-meat and plant-food appraisals are not re-derived
here — see [[Red Meat]], [[Plant Foods]], and [[Soy Products]].

**So the lever is a substitution, not a target.** Raising or lowering total protein does
little on its own; shifting protein-bearing foods plant-ward (legumes, nuts, whole
grains, soy) is what tracks lower mortality — judged, as always, against the realistic
alternative it replaces -> [[Better than What]]. One caveat bounds even that:
plant-protein foods *are* largely the legume/whole-grain/nut foods already credited with
lower mortality through fibre, so whether the signal belongs to the *protein* or to the
*pulses and fibre* carrying it cannot be separated here — a standing gap, and a caution
against counting plant protein as a second, separate lever.

Source and amount are two decisions you satisfy together — and quality is the bridge
between them.

## 7. Timing, per-meal distribution, and quality (DIAAS)

**Protein quality is composition times digestibility, capped by the limiting amino
acid.** DIAAS (Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score) multiplies how well a food's
indispensable amino acids match human requirement — where the single *limiting* amino
acid sets the score — by true-ileal digestibility (how much is actually absorbed).
Unlike its predecessor PDCAAS, DIAAS is uncapped, so it rewards complementation: a
high-scoring protein can lift a lysine-poor cereal, and the old truncation hid exactly
that. FAO's worked example ranks plant below animal: wheat \~40, peas \~64, whole milk
powder \~122 [@fao2013].

**The consequence is a caveat on the number, not a change to it.** Hitting a given
protein target from low-DIAAS plant sources delivers fewer usable amino acids per gram,
so it takes more total grams or deliberate complementation (cereal + legume, whose
limiting amino acids mirror each other) to match what animal protein delivers. Whey and
casein out-rank single plant powders on this score — but only on the surrogate. DIAAS
predicts how well a protein *could* meet amino-acid demand; the wiki holds no evidence
that a higher score improves a patient-important outcome for someone already at adequacy.
The edge is largest acutely and per gram, and it is a small lever that bites mainly
*below* adequacy: for someone already meeting their target from a mixed diet, the
limiting-amino-acid problem is solved at the diet level and the source barely matters.

**Per-meal distribution is a real lever, but mainly in one stratum.** Spreading protein
across meals rather than loading it into one matters most for *older adults*, whose
muscle needs a larger per-meal stimulus to trigger the same building response. The
mechanism — anabolic resistance and the raised per-meal leucine/dose threshold —
belongs to the stratum section and is not derived here -> [[Anabolic Resistance]]. For
younger adults at adequate intake, timing is a minor refinement.

Distribution and quality both bite hardest in one stratum — older adults — so turn to
who the number changes for.

## 8. Stratum variation — the same unit, a ladder by goal

The strata below are **not rival estimates of one protein *knee*.** They are the same daily
unit (g protein per kg body weight per day) read against **different objectives and energy
states**, so a higher number in one row does not contradict a lower number in another — it
answers a different question. Two denominators sit off this daily ladder and must never be
folded into it: the **per-meal** dose (g protein or g leucine per meal) is a *distribution*
constraint, and the energy-deficit target scaled **per kg fat-free mass** is a different kg.
Keep the denominator named; a number without its kg and its per-day/per-meal frame carries no
decision.

### Younger / general adult already above the RDA — a small lever

For a lean, active adult already eating above the 0.8 g/kg/d RDA
[@bauer2013], protein is a *small*
remaining lever: the big rocks (training, adequacy) are already pulled, and adding grams buys
little. This is the ceiling-is-a-finding case — the honest report is that the marginal protein
question is minor here, not that a hidden optimum is waiting to be tuned.

### Older (>\~65) — the floor RISES, it does not fall

The most-misread stratum. Aging does **not** lower the protein requirement; it raises it,
because of **anabolic resistance** — the age-related blunting of the muscle-protein-synthesis
response to a protein meal (a route-(b) effect modification, not a preference)
[@bauer2013]. The mechanism raises the
**per-meal leucine threshold** to \~2.5-2.8 g leucine, delivered by \~25-30 g protein per meal
[@bauer2013]; Moore's metabolic breakpoint
sits \~0.40 g/kg body mass per meal in older versus \~0.24 in younger men — a rightward shift of
roughly two-thirds [@moore2014]. Katsanos
showed the threshold is real and leucine-triggered: a low-leucine essential-amino-acid bolus
failed to raise synthesis in the elderly, while the same dose enriched with leucine restored it
[@katsanos2006].

The **daily maintenance target** that follows is 1.0-1.2 g/kg/d for a healthy older adult, 1.2
for the active, and 1.2-1.5 during acute or chronic illness
[@bauer2013] — above the 0.8 RDA, which is
a deficiency floor deployed as a target and undershoots this stratum. **Distribution matters as
much as total:** because each meal must clear the raised threshold, spread protein across
breakfast, lunch and dinner rather than back-loading it at one meal.

**The floor-not-ceiling rule.** The young resistance-training figure (\~1.6 g/kg/d; the
break-point estimate was 1.62 g/kg/d and statistically non-significant, p=0.079)
[@morton2018] must **not**
be transported *down* as a cap for an older trainee. Anabolic resistance runs the transport the
other way: more protein is needed per anabolic stimulus with age, so \~1.6 is a **floor, not a
ceiling**, for an older adult training for hypertrophy. The maintenance floor (1.0-1.2) and the
training floor (\~1.6) answer *maintain muscle in the old* versus *build muscle*; the honest
object for the older stratum is a direction and a floor, not a point optimum.

### Resistance-trained — the \~1.6 g/kg region, biased up for hypertrophy

For a resistance-trained adult in energy balance, protein is an adjunct to the training, not a
driver. This is the dose-response of sections 3-4: added protein modestly raised strength and lean
mass up to a non-significant break near 1.62 g/kg/d (CI 1.03-2.20, p=0.079)
[@morton2018] — a region,
not a knee, so those prioritizing hypertrophy reasonably read toward its upper end (\~2.2). Get the
training right first; the protein dial is the smaller lever.

### Energy deficit — the requirement RISES (exploratory)

In a caloric deficit the target moves **up** again, to defend fat-free mass. Refalo's exploratory
meta-regression (nonobese, resistance-trained adults) places the intake at which average FFM
change crosses from loss to hold at \~1.9 g/kg **body mass** per day, or \~2.5 g/kg **fat-free
mass** per day [@refalo2025] —
above the \~1.6 balance figure, matching the premise that a deficit raises the requirement. Read
it as a floor-and-direction on a monotone line with no observed peak, and hold it loosely: the
authors call the finding **exploratory**, and the protein effect is **small** next to the
resistance-training stimulus and the deficit size
[@refalo2025].

Two boundaries bound this figure. **Leaner people benefit more per gram** (protein defends body
protein more when fat stores are low), so the target scales inversely with body-fat; and the
**obese are off-support** — the whole sample was nonobese, so the g/kg-total figure does not
transport to them
[@refalo2025].

### Renal — the healthy-kidney safety null, and the one hard flip down

The standing objection to every raised target is that protein harms the kidneys. For **healthy
kidneys this is a null**: pooling 28 RCTs in adults *without* CKD (higher protein \~1.81 vs lower
\~0.93 g/kg/d), the change-from-baseline in GFR did not differ between arms (SMD 0.11; 95% CI
-0.05 to 0.27; P=0.16)
[@devries2018].
The small GFR rise higher protein does produce is read as benign **kidney functional
reserve** — a whole-kidney adaptation to a larger solute load, distinct from the single-nephron
hyperfiltration that precedes damage
[@devries2018]. So kidney
worry is **not** a reason to cap protein in a healthy adult, and a modest overshoot is low-harm
on this axis.

The **one hard flip down** is established **chronic kidney disease** (roughly eGFR < 30, not on
dialysis), where protein is *restricted*, not liberalized
[@bauer2013]. This is a route-(c)
contraindication and a genuine stratum boundary — narrow, not a general caution — and its
management is a prescriber act (diagnosis, titration, screening) outside this cut's scope.

Two things the evidence still cannot do close the picture.

## 9. Named gaps and the bottom line

The strata above are built on surrogates and observational signals. State the open loops plainly,
because a clean number would misrepresent what is known:

- **(a) Protein -> HARD kidney outcomes is unmeasured.** Devries measures GFR — a **surrogate**,
  graded low-to-very-low — over weeks to months, not kidney failure, dialysis, or doubling of
  creatinine over years
  [@devries2018].
  No protein-to-hard-renal-outcome trial is held. The direction of the healthy-kidney
  null is robust; its certainty is low, and it defuses the objection without proving safety at any
  dose indefinitely.
- **(b) Raising muscle mass or strength -> lower mortality is unproven.** The muscle targets move
  a *predictor* (lean mass, strength, MPS), and the causal link from raising it to a
  patient-important outcome — function, fracture, mortality — is asserted, not demonstrated. The
  loop is open: nothing here grades a chosen protein intake against a realized health outcome.

- **(c) The plant-protein mortality signal may be the fibre/pulse lever under another name.**
  Plant-protein foods largely *are* the legumes, whole grains and nuts already credited with lower
  mortality via fibre, so whether plant protein is an independent lever or a proxy for those foods
  cannot be separated in the observational data — a caution against double-counting it as a
  separate big rock.
- **(d) The obese are off-support for the g/kg-TOTAL number.** The deficit-stratum evidence was
  derived entirely in nonobese adults, so its total-body-mass target does not transport to obese
  individuals — they are unstudied here, not shown to need less
  [@refalo2025].
- **(e) Protein -> mortality independence is still owed.** The mortality signal rests on cohorts
  sharing a data or school lineage (the pooled meta-analysis, the overlapping cohorts, a
  constituent study double-counted); a genuinely **independent** cohort — one not pooled inside the
  primary meta-analysis — has not confirmed it.

### Bottom line

Treat every target as a **region, not a point** — the underlying curves are monotone or
non-significant knees, so no single number is a true optimum. **Bias the target up** within a
stratum where the cost of undershoot is high (older age, illness, an energy deficit, training for
muscle): a modest overshoot merely fails to help on healthy kidneys, rather than harming.
**Shift protein-bearing foods plant-ward** for the mortality outcome — a modest, observational
lever, not a dose change. And if older, **distribute protein across meals** so each clears the
raised per-meal threshold, rather than loading it at dinner. The one hard reversal in the whole
picture is established CKD, where the direction flips to restriction and the decision leaves this
cut's scope.
