**Peripheral scope** (exercise programming). The outcome the evidence actually measures here is **joint
range of motion (ROM)**, an intermediate outcome, not a patient-important one in its own right. It matters
to a person only through function (reaching, dressing, gait) or through an injury, pain or soreness claim.
The held evidence does not test ROM's transmission to function; it measures near-nulls for injury and for
stretching around a single session on soreness (with one small field effect for a 12-week routine); and
low back pain is unproven. So this page ranks low in Layer 1: a dose is held for the ROM goal, and the
commonly believed benefits are mostly unsupported.
 (ROM-as-intermediate is the wiki's classification, per [[Surrogate Outcomes]]; the source
does not grade ROM's transmission to function.)

**Evidence-tier note (`confidence: medium`, split by claim: drop-levers medium, dose/timing/ROM low).**
The two drop-lever claims (injury, soreness) rest on RCT meta-analyses, the soreness one on a Cochrane
review whose pooled before-or-after estimates are near zero with quite precise CIs (GRADE low; moderate
only for the single 12-week field trial; the smaller pre-exercise-only pools are wide); see their sections below. The dose and ROM claims are weaker: they
rest on one source, the 2011 ACSM position stand, a `high`-tier guideline that grades each recommendation with the NHLBI A-D categories (A = RCTs, rich data; B = RCTs,
limited data; C = nonrandomized or observational; D = panel consensus). It gives grades but no pooled
effect sizes, no confidence intervals and no studied ranges for its dose numbers.
[@garber2011acsm]
The prescription below is therefore a graded guideline position, not a meta-analytic estimate.

## What stretching does: range of motion, and little else that has been shown

- **ROM improves, transiently and then chronically.** «Joint range of motion is improved transiently after
  flexibility exercise, chronically after approximately 3–4 wk of regular stretching at a frequency of at
  least two to three times a week», and «it may improve in as few as 10 sessions with an intensive
  program». Table 1 grades the statement that flexibility exercise improves and maintains joint range of
  movement at **A** (the printed row is garbled: «improve and maintain and joint range of movement»).
  [@garber2011acsm]
- **The gain reverses when stretching stops.** «Improvements in joint range of motion reverse within 4–8 wk
  on cessation of stretching exercise, although there are variable responses among muscle–tendon groups
  (115,145,146,200,394).»; one report found that cutting from daily to 2-3 sessions a week
  maintained ROM. [@garber2011acsm]
- **Balance, possibly.** «Flexibility exer- cises may enhance postural stability and balance (83), par-
  ticularly when combined with resistance exercise (38).» Two references, no grade attached to this
  sentence; ref 83 (Costa) is a study of a single acute stretching bout, not a training programme. [@garber2011acsm]
- **Injury, low back pain, soreness: no consistent link.** The next sentence: «No consistent link has been
  shown between regular flexibility exercise and a reduction of musculotendinous injuries, pre- vention of
  low back pain, or DOMS (9,116,163,248,355,401).»
  [@garber2011acsm]

**An inconsistency inside the stand.** Table 1 still lists flexibility exercise among the methods that «may
reduce the risk of CVD events and musculoskeletal injury during exercise» (grade **C**, bundled with
warm-up, cool-down and gradual progression), while the flexibility text says no consistent link has been
shown. The risks section puts both halves in one sentence: «Commonly used methods to reduce musculoskeletal
injury and complications, such as the warm-up and cool-down, stretching, and gradual progression of volume
and intensity, seem to be helpful at least under some circumstances, but controlled studies substantiating
the effectiveness of these methods are insufficient (9,123,124,164,248,355).» The Conclusions then list
warming up, cooling down, gradual progression and technique as effective strategies, and leave stretching
off that list. Read the stand as *not asserting* an injury benefit for stretching.
[@garber2011acsm]
 (the reconciliation is the wiki's reading).

| Hold, older adults | 30-60 s may give greater benefit | C |

| Coverage | a series of stretches for each major muscle-tendon unit | B |
| Type | static (active or passive), dynamic, ballistic and PNF are all effective | B |

| Progression | «Methods for optimal progression are unknown.» | none |

[@garber2011acsm]

- **The hold-time ceiling carries no range.** «Holding a stretch for 10–30 s at the point of tightness or
  slight dis- comfort enhances joint range of motion, with little apparent benefit resulting from longer
  durations (21,95).» The stand states neither the longest hold studied nor an effect size, so «little
  apparent benefit» above 30 s is a graded-C reading of two references, not a located plateau.
  [@garber2011acsm]
 (the no-studied-range reading applies the wiki's threshold rule).
- **Repetition and time course.** «Repeating each flexibility exercise two to four times is effective, with
  enhancement of joint range of motion occurring during 3–12 wk (21,95,264).»
  [@garber2011acsm]
- **Time cost.** «For most individuals, this routine can be completed within 10 min.» The routine is a
  series of stretches for the major muscle-tendon units.
  [@garber2011acsm]
- **Muscle temperature.** «Flexibility exercise is most effective when the muscle temperature is elevated
  through light-to-moderate cardiorespiratory or muscular endurance exercise or passively through external
  methods such as moist heat packs or hot baths, although this benefit may vary across muscle–tendon units
  (29,249,326).» [@garber2011acsm]
  The three references behind this grade-A row are a study of pre- versus post-exercise stretching
  (Beedle), a dynamic-versus-static warm-up study on power and agility (McMillian), and a critical review
  of stretching and injury (Shrier). That is not a body of RCTs on ROM, so treat the row as C-strength
  whatever its printed grade. [@garber2011acsm]
 (the re-grading is the wiki's reading of the reference list).

## Stretching before strength or power work: an acute cost

- «Stretching exercises can have a negative effect on subse- quent muscle strength and power and sports
  performances, particularly when strength and power are important (248,396).» No grade is attached to
  this statement. The stand qualifies it: limited evidence exists on stretching programmes of different
  durations and types for different activities, particularly in people exercising to improve fitness, and «Further research is needed before
  making universal recommendations concerning the timing of stretching».
  [@garber2011acsm]
- Its working position: «Nonetheless, based on the available evidence, whenever pos- sible, persons engaging
  in a general fitness program should perform flexibility exercise after cardiorespiratory or resis- tance
  exercise—or alternatively—as a stand-alone program.»
  [@garber2011acsm]
- And the warm-up comparison: «For most persons, a dynamic, cardiorespiratory endurance exercise warm-up is
  superior to flexibility exercise for en- hancing cardiorespiratory or resistance exercise (especially with
  high duration and repetitions) performance (29,124, 248,249,326).» Performance is the outcome here, not
  injury. [@garber2011acsm]
- Warm-up as an injury lever is a separate question, carried on [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]].
  The held warm-up meta-analysis does not test stretching. It compares structured injury-prevention
  programmes (strength, balance, landing work) against «warm-up as usual (e.g., only running and
  stretching)» in under-19s, and where a control arm is specified it includes stretching (static
  and dynamic in four of the five specified); the programmes cut injury rates by about a third (IRR 0.64).
  [@ding2022warmup]
 So a stretching-based warm-up is the *comparator* there, and the benefit comes from adding
  other content, which fits the stretching null in the Synthesis below; the trial design cannot show that stretching
  contributes nothing, since five of the programmes also contained stretches.

## Stretching against muscle soreness: a measured near-zero effect

The Cochrane review pools randomised trials of stretching soon before or soon after exercise against
no stretching, with delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) rescaled to 0-100. Twelve trials: eleven small
trials (10-30 people stretching), of which ten were laboratory trials using a standardised bout of
unaccustomed, mostly eccentric exercise of one muscle group and one was a cross-over trial of post-match
stretching in Australian rules footballers; plus one large pragmatic internet trial (Jamtvedt 2010, 2377
adults doing their own recreational exercise for 12 weeks). Almost all used static stretching.
[@herbert2011stretch]

| Timing of stretching | Soreness day 1, MD (95% CI) | Soreness day 2, MD (95% CI) | Trials / N (day 1) | GRADE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before exercise | -0.52 (-11.30 to 10.26) | 0.72 (-11.20 to 12.64) | 3 / 70 | low |
| After exercise | -1.04 (-6.88 to 4.79) | 1.12 (-4.63 to 6.87) | 4 / 127 | low |
| Either, pooled | -0.93 (-6.05 to 4.20) | 1.04 (-4.14 to 6.22) | 7 / 197 | low |
| Before and after, 12 weeks (field trial) | worst weekly soreness -3.80 (-5.17 to -2.43) | — | 1 / 2377 | moderate |

Negative = less soreness with stretching, on a 100-point scale; control-group means were 29-49 on day 1 in
the small trials and 25 for weekly worst soreness in the field trial; day 3 pools are also near zero
(pooled -0.28, -6.79 to 6.22). Every pool had I2 = 0%. [@herbert2011stretch]

**Magnitude, in the authors' words.** «The estimates were quite precise so, in the primary analyses, it
was possible to rule out the possibility that the average eﬀect of pre-exercise or post-exercise
stretching was to reduce soreness by more than six points on a 100-point scale.» On the field trial's
4 points: «This eﬀect, though statistically significant, is very small.»
[@herbert2011stretch]
 The six-point bound holds, roughly, for the combined before-or-after pools (lower CI
limits -6.05, -4.14 and -6.79 on days 1-3). The pre-exercise-only pools are 2-3 small trials and cannot exclude a benefit of about 11-16
points (lower limits -11.30, -11.20, -15.82); post-exercise alone reaches -7.49 on day 3.

**The verdict.** «The evidence from randomised studies suggests that muscle stretching, whether
conducted before, afer, or before and afer exercise, does not produce clinically important reductions
in delayed-onset muscle soreness in healthy adults.» The review states no minimal important difference;
*clinically important* is the authors' judgment, not a pre-set threshold.
[@herbert2011stretch]
`[searched: minimal / minimum / worthwhile / smallest / clinically important across chunks 01-02]`

**The same field effect looks larger as a share of people.** In Jamtvedt 2010, «the risk of experiencing
bothersome soreness in a week was 24.6% in the stretch group and 32.3% in the control group (OR 0.69;
95% CI 0.59 to 0.82; Analysis 4.2).» The protocol behind it: seven lower-limb and trunk muscle groups,
each held at least 30 s, before and after vigorous exercise, for 12 weeks.
The review lists this trial's stretching as 900 s per session, the top of its 40-900 s range.
[@herbert2011stretch]
 That is about 8 percentage points fewer weeks with bothersome soreness (32.3% - 24.6%), bought
with at least about 7 minutes of stretching per session by the protocol (7 x 30 s before and after; the
review lists 900 s) for 12 weeks. On the continuous scale the authors call the
same effect very small; as a share of people-weeks it reads larger. Whether the trade is worth it is a
Layer-3 preference; the evidence does not decide it.

**Laboratory and field agree.** «However the estimates from 10 laboratory- based studies and two
community-based study suggest there are similar (near-zero) eﬀects in both settings.» The second field
trial (post-game stretching in Australian rules footballers) pointed the other way: soreness 3 points
higher at 15 h and 4 points higher at 48 h, both CIs crossing zero.
[@herbert2011stretch]

**Why the authors think the low GRADE may not undercut the null, and what that argument leaves out.**
The laboratory pools were downgraded for three reasons: risk of bias, indirectness («Such studies provide
an indirect evaluation of the eﬀects of stretching on soreness caused by recreational exercise.»), and
too few studies to assess publication bias. No trial was blinded (soreness is self-reported) and
«Allocation concealment was certain in Jamtvedt 2010 alone.» The authors' argument, which they
introduce as «some reasons to believe that the findings may not be seriously biased»: first, consistency
of estimates across studies and comparisons; and «Also, biases due to non-concealment of
allocation, lack of blinding, loss to follow-up and failure to analyse by intention-to-treat typically
produce exaggerated eﬀects (Chalmers 1983; Colditz 1989; Moher 1998; Schulz 1995) yet this review found
consistent evidence of near-zero eﬀects.» [@herbert2011stretch]
 The argument has two limits: it answers the risk-of-bias downgrade only, not
indirectness, which is why soreness from everyday training rests on the field data; and it covers
biases that inflate a benefit, not a bias toward the null such as poor adherence in an unsupervised
field arm. In Jamtvedt, «outcome reporting was in- complete (72% of all required reports).»
[@herbert2011stretch]

**One sensitivity analysis gives a larger number.** Adding a small trial with remarkably small SDs
(mean 3.8 on 100, about one-sixth of the other trials'; excluded from the primary analysis because it would have taken 50-96% of the weight) moves day-1 soreness
to -6 points (95% CI -9 to -4). [@herbert2011stretch]

**Who was studied.** «In all but one study participants were young healthy adults (age less than 40 years
or mean age less than 26 years); only Jamtvedt 2010 had substantial representation of older adults (mean
age 39.9 years, SD 12.6).» No trial targeted older adults.
[@herbert2011stretch]

**The original rationale is gone; one mechanism leaves a gap.** The practice may have arisen from the
1960s-70s belief that soreness was muscle spasm, a view since discredited. «Contemporary beliefs about the mechanisms of muscle
soreness do not suggest any means by which stretching before or afer exercise could reduce the soreness
that follows that exercise session.» The authors then qualify it: if soreness comes from over-lengthened
sarcomeres, adding sarcomeres in series or making tendons more compliant could help, so «This suggests
the possibility that stretching may be more eﬀective for people who have very short muscles, or if the
stretching is repeated for weeks or months or years.» «The longest duration of stretching investigated
in any of the trials conducted to date (Jamtvedt 2010) is 12 weeks.»
[@herbert2011stretch]

**Out of scope for this review.** Stretching once soreness has set in: «Some evidence suggests that once
muscle soreness has developed, stretching may provide a transient relief of soreness (Reisman 2005).»
(one cited study, not pooled). «The current review does not provide any evidence of an eﬀect or otherwise
of stretching on risk of injury, performance or well-being.» The review did not assess harms (soreness was
its only outcome).
[@herbert2011stretch]

## Stretching among the other post-exercise recovery techniques

A meta-analysis of 99 studies compared single post-exercise recovery sessions in healthy adults aged
18-65 (any training status), pooling Hedges' g per technique for DOMS and perceived fatigue. Stretching
is the only technique whose interval does not reach into a reduction: SMD **0.15 (95% CI 0.00 to 0.29)**,
5 groups, 67 participants (positive = more soreness); electrostimulation, -0.28 (-0.59 to 0.03), and
hyperbaric therapy, 0.55 (-0.12 to 1.22), are also null. [@dupuy2018recovery]
The authors: «we did not ﬁnd any signiﬁcant inﬂuence of stretching or electrostimulation on DOMS and
fatigue.» [@dupuy2018recovery]
 That interval is implausibly narrow for 67 people compared between groups (roughly ±0.35 to
±0.5 SD would be expected, depending on whether 67 counts one arm or both), which points to within-person designs or repeated timepoints counted separately; read
its precision with caution, and the point estimate as "no reduction", not as a shown increase.

| Technique (single session) | DOMS SMD (95% CI) | n | Fatigue SMD (95% CI) | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Massage | -2.26 (-3.05, -1.47) | 158 | -2.55 (-3.49, -1.62) | 64 |
| Compression garments | -0.92 (-1.34, -0.50) | 160 | -0.88 (-1.34, -0.50)* | 28 |
| Active recovery | -0.94 (-1.61, -0.28) | 90 | 0.64 (-0.43, 1.70) | 33 |
| Water immersion (DOMS: all temperatures; fatigue: all 8 groups, which Table 2 lists as ≤15°C) | -0.47 (-0.77, -0.18) | 379 | -1.16 (-1.94, -0.39) | 75 |
| Cryotherapy | -0.53 (-1.04, -0.03) | 72 | not pooled | — |
| Electrostimulation | -0.28 (-0.59, 0.03) | 94 | (1 group) | 11 |
| **Stretching** | **0.15 (0.00, 0.29)** | 67 | -0.21 (-1.04, 0.62) | 30 |

[@dupuy2018recovery]
Contrast water therapy -0.40 (-0.73, -0.07) and hyperbaric therapy 0.55 (-0.12, 1.22) on DOMS complete the
table. *The compression fatigue CI repeats the DOMS CI, probably a copy slip in the source table; the
source page carries the full rows and the table's other transcription slips.

- **The authors' reading of stretching.** «The overall SMD in our study conﬁrmed that stretching had no
  positive impact on DOMS. Moreover, our results at <6 h after exercise indicated that stretching might
  even produce DOMS, as initially reported by Smith et al. (1993).» Smith 1993 compared static with
  ballistic stretching; the Cochrane review above lists it among its excluded studies.
  [@dupuy2018recovery]
  [@herbert2011stretch]
- **What the comparison cannot carry.** The paper never states what each technique's SMD is measured
  against, admits repeated-measure (within-person) designs, scores study quality (PEDro) but rates no
  certainty for the body of evidence, and says «the beneﬁcial
  eﬀect arising from the placebo eﬀect of recovery techniques could not be eliminated».
  [@dupuy2018recovery]
  `[searched: passive / control / placebo across chunk 01]`
 The ranking is therefore a ranking of unblinded single-session effects, specific and
  nonspecific together. Stretching's null is less exposed to placebo inflation specifically, since a
  nonspecific boost would push toward benefit, not away from it; that argument does not cover the unstated
  comparator or the mix of within-person designs, which can bias either way (the same limit noted for
  Herbert's bias argument above). The large massage figure is the most exposed (see
  [[Massage Therapy for Pain and Function]]; the general case is [[The Comparator Problem]]).

## Synthesis

**The stand and an RCT meta-analysis agree on injury, with shared backing, so not independent
confirmation.** The held RCT meta-analysis on [[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]]
pools three stretching trials to a null for injury: «no beneficial effect for stretching (RR 0.963
(0.846–1.095))»; its discussion reports the same estimate with slightly different figures, «RR=0.961 (0.836–1.106)». [@lauersen2013injury]
The stand's «no consistent link» points the same way. Parameter check before comparing:

| Parameter | Garber 2011 (stand) | Lauersen 2013 (RCT-MA) | Same quantity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure | regular flexibility exercise, any form | stretching programmes (before or after exercise) | broadly yes |
| Outcome | musculotendinous injury (also LBP, DOMS) | lower-limb (± trunk) injury in the three stretching trials | overlapping, not identical |
| Design | narrative statement over 6 refs (Amako 2003 trial, Thacker 2004 SR, Herbert & de Noronha Cochrane, 3 narrative reviews) | pooled RR, 3 RCTs | no — narrative vs pooled |
| Population | apparently healthy adults (general) | stretching subgroup: 2 army-recruit RCTs (Pope 1998, 2000) + 1 internet RCT in adults >18 (Jamtvedt), no athletes | partly |

[@lauersen2013injury]
Lauersen flags the narrow base itself: «this analysis only included two studies on army recruits and one
internet-based study on the general popu- lation.»
[@lauersen2013injury]

The comparison is a **direction agreement on an overlapping outcome**, not a type-E convergence. The two
share backing: Lauersen says its stretching result «corresponds to earlier reviews», and two of those
reviews (Thacker 2004; Herbert & Gabriel 2002, Lauersen refs 17-18) are also cited by the stand (refs 355,
164); Herbert also co-authors Pope 2000 and Garber refs 163-164. [@lauersen2013injury]
[@garber2011acsm]
Read it as type-F: the later RCT-MA turns the stand's hedged «no consistent link» into a measured null for
injury in recruits and general adults, and the stand adds the outcomes the MA did not cover (low back
pain, DOMS), at guideline grade only.

**In knee and hip osteoarthritis, a mixed flexibility/skill category beat usual care (low certainty; it
does not isolate stretching).** On [[Knee Osteoarthritis and Modifiable Levers]], an exercise-type network
meta-analysis finds a *flexibility/skill* category beating usual care on OA pain and function at 8 weeks
(low certainty), but the category pools stretching with balance and coordination work, so it does not
isolate stretching. The same page holds a guideline null on the ROM surrogate under a combined programme
in OA. Neither conflicts with this page: the stand's ROM grade is for healthy adults doing a specified
stretching dose; the OA results are a different population and a mixed exposure. A distinction, not a
tension.

**Soreness: the Cochrane update puts a size on the stand's «no consistent link».** The stand's DOMS claim
cites, among others, the 2007 edition of the Herbert & de Noronha Cochrane review (ref 163).
[@garber2011acsm]
The 2011 update reaches the same direction with numbers. Parameter check:

[@garber2011acsm]
[@herbert2011stretch]
 Type-F: the update turns the stand's hedge into a bounded estimate (the combined before-or-after
pools exclude benefits much beyond 6-7 points on 100; the field trial's estimate is 4 points, 95% CI 2.4 to
5.2), but it is the same review line, so it adds precision, not independent confirmation. The exposure row
is the seam: the stand speaks of a regular flexibility routine, the review mostly of stretching around a
single session. The one 12-week routine showed a small effect; routines longer than 12 weeks are untested.

**One trial carries both general-adult results.** The field trial behind the soreness estimate
(Jamtvedt 2010, internet-run, adults doing recreational exercise) is also one of the three trials in
Lauersen's pooled injury null, and the review's first author co-authored it. So for general adults outside
the laboratory, *stretching around exercise prevents neither injury nor meaningful soreness* rests largely
on one 12-week trial reporting two outcomes, not on two independent bodies of evidence.
[@lauersen2013injury]
[@herbert2011stretch]
The laboratory near-null on soreness after a standardised eccentric bout rests on the ten small lab
trials, not on Jamtvedt; the field estimate for everyday training (a small benefit) and the injury null in
general adults both rest on Jamtvedt.

with the Cochrane points.** Parameter check:

stretching participants, the same n as Dupuy's stretching row (five groups; its n column is not defined as arm or
total). It is consistent with shared trials but does not establish them.
[@herbert2011stretch]

## Decision relevance

**If the goal is joint range of motion** (a stiff hip or shoulder that limits something you want to do):
stretch each tight area 2-3+ days a week, 60 s total per stretch (e.g. 2 x 30 s or 4 x 15 s; 30-60 s
holds for older adults), after a workout or on its own, about 10 minutes for the whole body. Expect gains over
3-4 weeks and expect to lose them within 4-8 weeks of stopping. This is a graded B/C guideline dose, not
a measured optimum. (operationalizes the Table 2 rows above).

**Do not count on stretching to prevent injury.** The RCT meta-analysis found a null in recruits and
general adults. If injury prevention is the aim of a warm-up, the held evidence favours a structured
strength/balance/jumping warm-up programme over the usual warm-up (the review's example: running and
stretching), with about a third fewer injuries (IRR 0.64) in sport participants aged 18 or under (the Ding section above); for adults see
[[Exercise Interventions and Sports Injury Prevention]]. For back pain the guideline found no consistent link: unproven, not shown useless.


**A single bout of stretching around one session: skip it if next-day soreness is the only reason.**
It changes soreness over the next three days by about 1 point on a 100-point scale in the combined
pools (no primary pooled point estimate exceeds 2.5 points; the combined CIs reach about 6-7 points of
benefit, the small pre-exercise-only pools 11-16). In young healthy adults after a laboratory eccentric
bout this is a measured near-zero effect (indirect for everyday training), not missing evidence.
 (operationalizes the Herbert estimates above).

**A standing before-and-after routine: a small effect, worth it only if you value the trade.** Over 12
weeks it cut the share of weeks with bothersome soreness from about 32% to 25%, for at least about 7
minutes of stretching per session (the review lists 900 s). (operationalizes the Jamtvedt
estimate above).

**For a beginner, the lever on soreness is the dose of new work, not stretching.** The review describes
DOMS as «usually caused by unaccustomed exercise, particularly exercise that requires primarily eccentric
muscle action». [@herbert2011stretch]
 So the practical default is to start new exercises, especially lowering-heavy ones such as
downhill walking or slow eccentric lifts, at low volume and build up. This is standard practice, not
trial-tested against stretching; the repeated-bout idea behind it is a mechanism note on
[[Resistance Training Prescription - Load Sets and Frequency]].

**Untested, not refuted:** stretching for longer than 12 weeks, or for people with very short muscles,
which the review names as a conceivable route to an effect.
[@herbert2011stretch]

**For soreness after a session, do not use stretching; if you want something, pick from the techniques
that showed a signal and expect a modest effect at most.** In the recovery-technique meta-analysis,
massage, compression garments, active recovery, cold water (≤15°C; warm immersion showed no reduction),
contrast water and cryotherapy showed pooled single-session reductions in SD units; stretching,
electrostimulation and hyperbaric therapy showed none. Light active recovery or a cold soak are the cheap
options. The ordering is suggestive only: each technique was pooled from a different set of trials,
not compared head to head, the DOMS funnel plot leaned toward positive effects («23 studies out of 57
were distributed toward a positive eﬀect»), and the estimates are unblinded, against an unstated
comparator, carry no certainty rating and are untested for regular use; none can be sized in points on
a 100-point scale. Details: [[Massage Therapy for Pain and Function]], [[Cold-Water Immersion]] (which
also carries the open question of cold water after strength training). (operationalizes
the Dupuy section above) [@dupuy2018recovery]

**Before heavy lifting, sprinting or jumping, warm up with movement, not long static holds.** The stand
states the acute strength/power cost without a grade and calls the evidence for general exercisers
limited, so this is a low-cost default, not a firm finding.

**Layer-1 rank: low.** For a novice whose large lever is starting aerobic and resistance work at all,
stretching is optional. Its strongest case is a person whose limited ROM blocks an activity they value.
