> [!warning]
> This is a decision-scoped read for a person *considering* a GLP-1 for weight. The answer turns on baseline cardiometabolic risk; it is not population-wide advice to start or avoid the drug.

## One name, two drugs, split by baseline cardiometabolic risk

A GLP-1 receptor agonist does the same thing to everyone's appetite, but its *value*
forks on who takes it. Two people can take the identical dose and face two different
decisions, because the benefit that matters — a prevented heart attack, stroke, or death —
scales with the risk a person already carries. Sort by baseline cardiometabolic risk and
the drug resolves into two answers wearing one name.


## High baseline risk: a proven hard-outcome drug, and a Big Rock

For a person with class II/III obesity AND established cardiovascular disease — a prior
heart attack, stroke, or symptomatic peripheral artery disease — semaglutide cut major
cardiovascular events on top of statins and antiplatelets: MACE 6.5% vs 8.0% over \~3.3
years, HR 0.80 (95% CI 0.72-0.90)
[@lincoff2023]. That is an
absolute reduction of \~1.5 percentage points, NNT \~67 over \~3.3 years
[inferred from @lincoff2023]. All-cause mortality also moved, HR 0.81 (0.71-0.93), though as a
non-confirmatory secondary endpoint it is a directional signal, not a licensed claim
[@lincoff2023]. In the
adjacent very-high-risk stratum — type-2 diabetes with chronic kidney disease — kidney
failure is the hard outcome that moves -> [[Semaglutide and Kidney Outcomes in Chronic Kidney Disease]].
The absolute benefit is large here precisely because baseline risk is high — route (a),
no subgroup claim needed.
For this person the drug is a large lever, a Big Rock, not a refinement.

## Low or cosmetic baseline risk: real weight change, unproven hard-outcome benefit

For a person near-normal in weight with no cardiometabolic disease, the picture inverts.
The well-evidenced change is large and reliable, but it is a surrogate: \~15% weight loss
(-14.9% vs -2.4% placebo; half lose >=15%, a third lose >=20%), lower waist, blood
pressure and CRP, and 84% reversion from prediabetes
[@wilding2021]. Any reduction in heart
attacks, strokes, or death is NOT evidenced: the cardiovascular-outcome trial
enrolled only established-CVD patients and did not test primary prevention, so that benefit
is unproven — insufficient evidence, not disproven
[@lincoff2023]. And even if the
relative effect transported, the absolute benefit shrinks with baseline risk — roughly
5-fold at a 5% ten-year risk
[inferred from @lincoff2023]. The gastrointestinal
tolerability tax, meanwhile, is the same one the high-risk person pays — and in *serious*-event
terms the balance runs against the low-risk person, who nets more serious harm where few hard
events stand to be averted (the safety ledger below). So a lower-risk person buys a reliable
surrogate change at full cost, with the patient-important payoff unproven.

## The class is not uniform

"GLP-1" names several drugs, and they do not carry the same evidence. Tirzepatide, a dual
GIP/GLP-1 agonist, takes off more weight than semaglutide but has no dedicated
cardiovascular-outcome trial, so a bigger number on the scale does not carry a bigger proven benefit
[@jastreboff2022]. A network meta-analysis of 262
trials (99 791 participants, 19 drugs) now ranks the class against a common reference — lifestyle
modification alone — supplying the head-to-head the separate single-drug trials structurally cannot: at
one year, tirzepatide takes off -14.9% of body weight (95% CI -16.0 to -13.9) against subcutaneous
semaglutide's -9.8% (-10.6 to -9.1), both at moderate-to-high certainty
[@nong2026obesity].

The weight ranking is not the hard-outcome ranking, and that inversion is exactly why an agent is read
for its own evidence rather than the class label. Across all 19 drugs, subcutaneous semaglutide is the
only one to reach a mortality or myocardial-infarction signal, and the NMA flags that limb as «largely
informed by cardiovascular outcome trials in high risk populations»
[@nong2026obesity] — the SELECT evidence
already covered above, re-pooled into the network, not new independent corroboration
-> [[Comparing Obesity Drugs]]. Tirzepatide, despite the larger weight loss, reaches only a
heart-failure signal (HR 0.49, 0.27 to 0.88) and no mortality or MI benefit, and its dedicated hard-CV
outcomes trial has not yet reported
[@nong2026obesity]. The same drug also
sheds the most lean mass in the class — tirzepatide -8.3% (moderate certainty)
[@nong2026obesity]
-> [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]]. So which agent ranks first depends on the outcome a person weights: maximal
weight loss points to tirzepatide, an evidenced mortality/MI reduction to subcutaneous semaglutide — a
layer-3 choice the ranking informs but does not settle. Read each agent for its own evidence, not for
the class label -> [[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]].

## Benefit is a maintained state, not a cure

The effect lasts only while the drug does. When treatment stopped, participants regained
about two-thirds of their lost weight within a year (a mean 11.6 percentage points regained
vs 1.9 on placebo), and their cardiometabolic gains drifted back toward baseline
[@wilding2022]. This is defended physiology, not
weak resolve: after weight loss the body's satiety signals stay suppressed and its hunger
signals stay elevated, and the drug works by resupplying one of those signals from outside
-> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]
[@wilding2022]. Remove the exogenous signal and
the deficit reasserts. The realistic comparison to a lifestyle change must therefore price
lifelong cost, tolerability, and reversibility — not a one-time correction.

## The harm ledger: gastrointestinal is the robust one; the feared cancers are not

The reliable harm is gastrointestinal — nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea (umbrella odds
ratios \~2.5, 2.8, and 1.9) driving real discontinuation (7.0% vs 3.1% in the
primary-prevention trial)
[@yang2026]
[@wilding2021]. The most-feared harms are
not borne out on current human evidence: thyroid cancer shows no robust signal (OR 1.43,
95% CI 0.95-2.13, non-significant), and the pancreatic and other cancer signals are held,
and unconfirmed, on [[GLP-1 Non-Cardiometabolic Effects and Safety]]
[@yang2026]. Muscle is lost with
the fat — but roughly as any fast, large weight loss loses it, and fat falls more than lean
[@wilding2021]; the lean-mass question,
and semaglutide's partial exception, live on [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]]. A
protective fracture signal (OR 0.67, 0.52-0.87) partly offsets the fall worry
[@yang2026].

## The net weighing is yours

The wiki states which outcomes the drug moves, in which direction, and how certainly. It
does not price weight change against gastrointestinal burden, lifelong cost, or a
heart-attack reduction that is proven for one stratum and unproven for another — that
trade-off is irreducibly the person's. Everything below turns on that split, and
it starts with the one thing the drug reliably does: it makes you eat less.

## What the drug reliably does: a large, real weight loss that is surrogate-only and holds only while dosed

The best-evidenced effect is on body weight, and the magnitude depends on the agent and the
population it was measured in. In STEP-1 — semaglutide 2.4 mg once-weekly in a primary-prevention
obese population (BMI >=30, or >=27 with a weight-related condition, non-diabetic, mean age 46) —
mean body weight fell **-14.9%** vs **-2.4%** on placebo to week 68, a placebo-adjusted **-12.4
percentage points (95% CI -13.4 to -11.5)** [@wilding2021]. In SELECT — the same drug in an older secondary-prevention population (age 45+,
established cardiovascular disease, BMI >=27, non-diabetic) — the weight effect was smaller: **-9.39%**
vs **-0.88%**, a difference of **-8.51 (95% CI -8.75 to -8.27) percentage points** at week 104
[@lincoff2023]. So the reliable
semaglutide figure is roughly **\~15% in a primary-prevention obese adult and \~9-10% in an older
secondary-prevention one**.

**Response is heterogeneous — responders and non-responders both exist.** STEP-1's categorical
readout makes the spread explicit: **69.1%** of the semaglutide arm lost >=10% (vs 12.0% placebo),
**50.5%** lost >=15% (vs 4.9%), and **32.0%** lost >=20% (vs 1.7%) [@wilding2021]. A mean of \~15% is composed of a large group losing a great deal and
a substantial minority (roughly 30%) not reaching even 10% — the mean is not the individual estimate.

**Tirzepatide is a distinct drug class, and its magnitude must not be pooled with semaglutide's.**
Tirzepatide is a **dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist** — it adds a second receptor axis rather than
intensifying GLP-1 agonism (its GIP-receptor affinity equals native GIP, while it binds the GLP-1
receptor about five times more weakly than native GLP-1) [@jastreboff2022]. In SURMOUNT-1 (n=2539, the same non-diabetic primary-prevention obese
class as STEP-1, 72 weeks), the treatment-regimen weight change was **-15.0%, -19.5%, -20.9%** at
5/10/15 mg vs **-3.1%** placebo — placebo-adjusted **-11.9 to -17.8 percentage points** by dose —
with **50%** and **57%** of the 10 mg and 15 mg groups losing >=20% (vs 3% placebo)
[@jastreboff2022]. The source itself bars a
head-to-head read: «no direct comparison of these trials can be made, since trial populations and designs differed» [@jastreboff2022]. So
tirzepatide is the larger weight lever on a cross-trial contrast, but it is a *related-but-different*
exposure, not a bigger dose of the same one.

**Every one of these numbers is a surrogate, not a patient-important outcome.** Body weight is the
coprimary or primary endpoint in STEP-1 and SURMOUNT-1, but weight is a marker: whether moving it
moves an outcome a person actually cares about (a heart attack, a death, years of function) is a
**separate evidenced step**, never assumed from the weight change -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]. This matters precisely because the weight effect is so large and reliable — a record
weight number is exactly the place where the temptation to read the surrogate *as* the outcome is
strongest, and the surrogate discipline forbids it.

**The mechanism explains why the effect is real while dosed — and only while dosed.** After
diet-induced loss the body's appetite-regulating signals shift to defend the higher weight: satiety
hormones (GLP-1, PYY, amylin, leptin) fall and the hunger signal (ghrelin) rises, a coordinated
pressure toward regain that persists for at least a year -> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]. A GLP-1 receptor agonist does not persuade someone to want less food; it
**pharmacologically resupplies one of the depleted satiety signals**, overriding the defended set
point from the outside. That is why the loss is durable while the drug is taken and reverses when it
stops — the exogenous signal is withdrawn and the endogenous deficit reasserts. The effect is a
maintained state on chronic drug, not a one-time correction.

**The STEP-1-vs-SELECT gap is a population-and-design artifact, not a claim that the drug works better
in healthier people.** The two trials differ on exactly the axes that move weight response: STEP-1
measured at week 68 in a younger population (age 46, 74% female, no CVD required); SELECT measured at
week 104 in an older, sicker one (age 61.6, 72% male, established CVD)
[@wilding2021]. Different age, sex mix, comorbidity, and measurement window
confound the comparison, so the \~15% vs \~9-10% split is a difference in *who and when was measured*,
not evidence that the drug delivers more in a prevention setting.

Weight is the lever; the outcome a person actually cares about is whether pulling it moves a hard
endpoint — and there the answer forks by stratum.

## Hard cardiovascular events: proven in one population, untested in the other

SELECT is the landmark: a single event-driven RCT (n=17,604) that gave semaglutide 2.4 mg, on top of
standard secondary-prevention care, a first demonstration that treating obesity pharmacologically
reduces hard cardiovascular events. The primary MACE composite (CV death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal
stroke) fell from 701 of 8801 placebo patients (8.0%) to 569 of 8803 (6.5%) — «hazard ratio, 0.80; 95% confidence interval, 0.72 to 0.90; P<0.001» over a mean 39.8 months
[@lincoff2023]. That is a
1.5-percentage-point absolute reduction over \~3.3 years, an NNT of about 67
[inferred from @lincoff2023] — a real, incremental benefit on top of guideline therapy (90% on
statins, 86% on antiplatelets), not instead of it.

**Who was enrolled fixes what the number means.** SELECT admitted only patients 45 or older with
preexisting cardiovascular disease and a BMI of 27 or greater, without diabetes — a
**secondary-prevention** population running at a placebo event rate of \~2.4%/year
[@lincoff2023]. The trialists
state the boundary in their own words: «An important limitation of this trial is that we included only patients with preexisting car-diovascular disease. The effects of semaglutide on primary prevention of cardiovascular events in persons with overweight or obesity but without previous atherosclerotic disease were not studied.» [@lincoff2023]

### Why the low-risk benefit is unproven, not merely unstated

The gap does not close by transporting the relative effect. Follow the route-(a) logic on
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]: absolute benefit is the relative reduction applied to
a person's own baseline risk, so a constant hazard ratio buys a shrinking absolute return as baseline
risk falls. A primary-prevention person at \~5% 10-year risk sits roughly five-fold below SELECT's placebo
rate; apply the same HR 0.80 and the \~1.5-pp reduction shrinks toward a fraction of a percentage point.
The relative effect is unlikely to differ by stratum, but it is also the fragile assumption — mechanism
differs where plaque is already present versus not — while the absolute shrinkage is arithmetic and
certain. Either way the hard-CV benefit for a low-risk, near-normal-BMI person is **expected-small and
never tested**: the insufficient-evidence state, not a demonstrated null
[inferred from @lincoff2023].

### Weight loss does not carry a guaranteed CV benefit with it

SELECT cannot settle whether the benefit is the *weight* or the *drug*. The lifestyle-route evidence on
[[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]] is the reason to keep that question open: Look AHEAD,
the largest, longest randomized test of intentional lifestyle weight loss, was **null** on its CV
composite (HR 0.95, 0.83-1.09) despite real weight loss and a large drop in inflammation, and the Ma 2017
54-RCT meta-analysis generalizes the CV-event null (RR 0.93). Weight loss per se is not a guaranteed
cardiovascular lever. SELECT's own benefit separated early, before much weight was lost, so part of it
plausibly runs through drug- or route-specific channels rather than the pounds themselves — attribution
between weight and drug stays **open**, and the low-risk person cannot bank a CV-event reduction on the
weight loss alone [inferred from @lincoff2023].

### The composite is carried by non-fatal events

Only the primary composite is a confirmatory result. The pre-specified hierarchical gate tested CV death
next and it **failed** — «hazard ratio, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.71 to 1.01; P = 0.07»
[@lincoff2023] — so «superiority testing was not performed for the remaining confirmatory secondary end points»
[@lincoff2023]. Everything below the
gate is a point estimate, not a confirmed effect:

| Endpoint | HR (95% CI) | Confirmatory status |
|---|---|---|
| Primary MACE | 0.80 (0.72-0.90), P<0.001 | Confirmatory — hit |
| CV death | 0.85 (0.71-1.01), P=0.07 | **Failed the pre-specified gate** |
| All-cause mortality | 0.81 (0.71-0.93) | Non-confirmatory / exploratory |
| Heart-failure composite | 0.82 (0.71-0.96) | Non-confirmatory / exploratory |
| Non-fatal stroke | 0.93 (0.74-1.15) | Not reduced |
| Non-fatal MI | 0.72 (0.61-0.85) | Favourable, exploratory |

[@lincoff2023]

The CV-death miss is not evidence of no effect on CV death — its interval still spans a meaningful
benefit, and the endpoint was gatekept and underpowered; it means the trial did not license a
confirmatory claim there. All-cause mortality (0.81) and heart failure (0.82) are directionally
favourable but sit below the failed gate, so they are exploratory, neither inflated to *significant* nor
dismissed. And because non-fatal MI (0.72) and revascularization carry most of the composite while stroke
was not reduced and CV death did not clear the gate, **MACE fell but deaths did not** — *MACE was reduced*
must not be read as *deaths were reduced*
[inferred from @lincoff2023].

### Tirzepatide has no hard-CV trial at all

The class's strongest weight lever is dual GIP/GLP-1 agonism, but semaglutide is the only agent with a
hard-CV-outcome trial. Tirzepatide's pivotal obesity RCT measured **weight, a surrogate**, and its
CV morbidity-mortality outcome trial does not yet exist:. A larger weight loss does **not** license a larger inferred CV benefit — the causal step from
weight to events is a separate evidenced link, and Look AHEAD is the standing worked case that a
record-breaking surrogate move can buy no measurable event reduction. Until that trial reports,
tirzepatide's hard-CV effect is insufficient-evidence, not no-effect
.

Cardiovascular events are the one hard endpoint with a positive trial; the other organ outcomes are a mix
of one strong kidney signal and a set of surrogate moves.

## Kidney, glycaemia, liver: three more organ benefits, each gated by baseline risk

Cardiovascular events and weight are not the whole drug. Semaglutide moves hard kidney outcomes,
slows progression to diabetes, and improves fatty liver — but each benefit was
demonstrated in a stratum where baseline risk is already high, and none of them
transports to a low-risk, near-normal-BMI person by relative effect alone.

### Kidney: FLOW proved a hard outcome in T2D + CKD — a different stratum, not the obese one

FLOW is the first dedicated hard-outcome kidney trial of a GLP-1 receptor agonist,
and it enrolled a narrow, high-risk population: type 2 diabetes with established
chronic kidney disease (eGFR 25-75 ml/min/1.73m2, elevated albuminuria), already on a
maximal-dose RAS inhibitor, mean eGFR 47, mean BMI 32, mean age 66.6; semaglutide
1.0 mg weekly (the glycaemic dose, not the 2.4 mg obesity dose) vs placebo, n=3533,
median 3.4 years, stopped early for efficacy
[@perkovic2024].

On that stratum the effect is large and lands on patient-important endpoints:

- Primary composite of major kidney disease events: **HR 0.76 (95% CI 0.66-0.88),
  P=0.0003**, 5.8 vs 7.5 events/100 patient-years, **NNT 20 over 3 years (95% CI
  14-40)** [@perkovic2024].
- Death from any cause: **HR 0.80 (0.67-0.95), P=0.01**, **NNT 39 over 3 years**
  [@perkovic2024].
- Death from cardiovascular causes **HR 0.71 (0.56-0.89)** and MACE **HR 0.82
  (0.68-0.98), NNT 45 over 3 years** — the survival signal, not just the renal one
  [@perkovic2024].

Two qualifiers keep the headline honest. First, the primary composite is **not all
hard kidney failure**: it pools kidney failure (dialysis/transplant/eGFR<15) with a
surrogate >=50% eGFR-decline threshold and death, and the trial was not powered to
detect an effect on kidney failure alone — so the defensible claim is *reduced a
composite of kidney events and reduced death*, not *prevented dialysis* as a
standalone result [@perkovic2024].
Second, the benefit is **largely weight-independent** — it was unrelated to changes in
body weight, and creatinine- and cystatin-C-based eGFR agreed, so this is not merely
weight loss lowering serum creatinine; direct renal anti-inflammatory/anti-fibrotic
mechanism is proposed and marked as such
[@perkovic2024].

The load-bearing limit is transportability. FLOW's population is diabetic, albuminuric,
low-eGFR and old — a high absolute baseline risk, and the trialists flag the result
may not generalize to lower-risk people. Absolute benefit scales with that baseline
[[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]: for a low-risk person with
near-normal BMI, the relative effect might still hold, but the absolute return
collapses — an NNT of 20 becomes far larger when the event rate is a fraction of FLOW's
5.8-7.5 per 100 patient-years, and the relative effect itself is untested outside CKD
[@perkovic2024].

### Glycaemia: a sharp cut in progression to diabetes — a surrogate plus a progression endpoint

Two-thirds of SELECT was prediabetic, and progression to diabetes fell steeply:
progression to HbA1c >=6.5% ran at **HR 0.27 (95% CI 0.24-0.31)**
[@lincoff2023]. STEP-1
shows the same in a younger primary-prevention population: among prediabetic
participants, **84.1%** on semaglutide reverted to normoglycaemia by week 68 vs
**47.8%** on placebo
[@wilding2021]. For an obese,
impaired-fasting-glucose person this is a distinct and plausibly relevant benefit — but
it sits on a **glycaemic-threshold surrogate** (reversion / progression across a cut
point) plus a progression endpoint, not on a patient-important diabetes-complication
outcome; whether it lowers hard diabetes-related outcomes in primary prevention
inherits the same insufficient-evidence caveat as the CV case
[@lincoff2023].

### Liver: the same weight lever, deferred

Weight loss of the magnitude these drugs produce improves fatty-liver (MASLD) disease
in a dose-dependent way; the steatosis/inflammation/fibrosis thresholds live in
[[Body Fat]].

Each of these benefits is bought with the same body-wide catabolic state — which is
also why lean tissue comes off, not just fat.

## Muscle comes off with the fat — the ratio improves while absolute lean mass falls

The one dedicated synthesis is a systematic review and meta-analysis of 7 RCTs at obesity doses
(821 patients), and its finding has two faces that must be read together
[@laverde2026]. As a *proportion* of total
weight, lean mass rises: +1.81% (95% CI 1.1 to 2.52; p < 0.00001; I2 = 7%) — fat is shed faster than
lean, so body composition improves as a ratio. In *absolute* terms, muscle is lost: −1.74 kg (95% CI
−3.04 to −0.45; I2 = 98%), a −3.06% fall in lean mass (95% CI −5.10 to −1.02)
[@laverde2026]. Semaglutide is the outlier on
both counts: −5.44 kg (95% CI −7.07 to −3.81), −9.9%. The near-98% heterogeneity on the absolute
figure is itself a reason to hold these numbers loosely.

Laverde's own verdict lands on the ratio face — «lean mass loss should not be considered a limitation for the use of these drugs in patients with obesity»
[@laverde2026]. That is faithful to the
composition data and it is not a denial that absolute muscle falls; both are true at once. The
decision turns on which face governs for whom.

## The lost muscle is roughly what any rapid loss costs — not a drug-specific hazard

The sharper question: does the *drug* shed disproportionate muscle, or is this just what
fast weight loss does? Of total mass lost, about 30% is lean — «comparable to that observed after bariatric surgery» — which sits at or just inside the ordinary 20-30% diet-induced band
[@laverde2026]. Per agent: liraglutide
14-22%, tirzepatide \~26%, and semaglutide up to 45% — above the ordinary band, the one agent that
earns the specific worry. So on the class average the lean loss is generic to rapid weight loss
whatever drives it, not a novel drug toxicity — with semaglutide the exception.
[inferred from @laverde2026]

Gap: the wiki holds no head-to-head against a *matched, same-magnitude* non-drug weight loss, so the
attribution rests on comparing separate reference figures rather than a within-study contrast. It is a
directional read, not a settled one.

## Function is unmeasured — the streetlight gap

The load-bearing caveat, and the reason none of this licenses a strong recommendation: composition is
measured, but the outcome people actually care about is not. «In addition, the included studies did not evaluate functional outcomes, such as muscle strength or physical capacity.»
[@laverde2026]. Lean mass is a surrogate for
muscle mass, which is a surrogate for strength, gait speed, falls, and independence
-> [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]]. A reassuring lean-mass ratio does not prove function is
spared, and a scary absolute-kg figure does not prove function is lost — the patient-important
endpoint is dark on both sides. Hold this as *insufficient evidence on function*, not as safe or
harmful. This page's fabric confidence is low, and the unmeasured function is why.

## Benign in the young, a real cost in the old — the stratum inversion

Which face governs is a matter of stratum, and Laverde's population is the wrong one for the worry:
the trials ran in «relatively young populations with a low burden of comorbidities», with the effects
in older and multimorbid people left for future study — precisely those «at high risk for muscle deterioration» [@laverde2026].

- **Young, high-BMI adult with abundant muscle:** the ratio face governs — composition improves, the
  absolute loss is minor and recoverable. Rank the muscle cost low.
- **Older / sarcopenia-risk / multimorbid adult:** the absolute face governs — the same kilograms
  become a front-line patient-important harm (falls, fractures, lost independence)
  -> [[Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis]], [[Big Rocks (Elderly)]]. The counterfactual is not zero
  loss either: Laverde's placebo arms also lost lean mass, on the order of the \~0.3% to 1% per year of
  age-related decline — the stratum is losing muscle anyway, which sharpens rather than softens the
  case for defending it. [inferred from @laverde2026]

## The defense, judged as a complete strategy — and the collision inside it

Laverde arrives at the muscle cluster's own answer from the drug side: it is «essential to accompany drug treatment with nutritional and physical exercise interventions to preserve or improve muscle mass» [@laverde2026]. Resistance training is
the primary muscle-sparing lever under any energy deficit, with adequate protein as the adjunct
-> [[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]. GLP-1 weight loss *is* an energy
deficit, and a deficit raises — not lowers — the protein intake needed to retain fat-free mass
-> [[Protein Intake During Energy Restriction]].

The right unit is therefore the net effect of the whole strategy — drug plus resistance training plus
protein — never the drug's naive body-composition number -> [[Net Effect vs Intended Effect]]. But the
strategy carries a collision: the drug works by suppressing appetite and total intake, which is
exactly the intake the protein-and-training defense requires *more* of — hardest to achieve in the
older stratum where it matters most. So the defense is real but not free; monitor it on function, not
on weight alone.

Muscle is the cost you can mitigate; the next set is the cost ledger you weigh directly.

## The safety ledger: what to actually worry about

Read every entry below on two axes at once, because the umbrella grades them on two axes that
routinely disagree [@yang2026]. **GRADE
certainty** rates how well the effect itself is estimated (high / moderate / low / very low); the
umbrella's **credibility class** rates how robust the association is to bias — convincing (I) /
highly suggestive (II) / suggestive (III) / weak (IV) — from summary p-value, prediction interval,
heterogeneity and small-study effects. They are different questions and can point opposite ways: a
well-estimated effect can still collapse under a single trial. Within the same GI cluster, nausea is
GRADE-high but credibility-class IV (weak), while vomiting is GRADE-moderate but class II (highly
suggestive) — higher certainty, lower robustness — so a headline quoting one number is misleading.
Keep the two apart as [[Rating Certainty of Evidence]] requires; the letter alone hides the
judgment. [@yang2026]

The gastrointestinal cluster is the one harm that clears the bar. It is also where GRADE certainty is
highest: nausea OR 2.47 (1.84-3.34), vomiting OR 2.78 (1.91-4.06), diarrhoea OR 1.94 (1.52-2.49)
[@yang2026]. This is the harm that is
*established* rather than exploratory — and it carries a distinction that decides how much it should
weigh: it is largely **dose- and titration-dependent** (it tracks how fast the dose is escalated),
and it is mostly **transient and early** (it concentrates in the initiation window and fades with
continued use) [@yang2026]. Those are two
separate facts — one about *how much* drug and how fast, the other about *when* in the course — and
both cut the same way: the GI tax is a start-of-therapy, titration-sensitive cost, not a standing
lifetime harm. It is the dominant real-world adherence cost, and it is already priced into the drug
decision.

The serious-adverse-event *balance* inverts by stratum — the low-risk person nets more serious harm,
not less. In FLOW's high-risk diabetic-CKD population serious adverse events were *fewer* on
semaglutide (49.6% vs 53.8%) [@perkovic2024];
in STEP-1's lower-risk obese population they ran *higher* — 9.8% vs 6.4%, the excess driven by
serious gastrointestinal (1.4% vs 0%) and hepatobiliary (1.3% vs 0.2%) events
[@wilding2021]. Where many hard events stand to be
prevented the drug averts serious events on net; where few do, it adds them — the mirror image of the
high-risk benefit, and a cost the headline weight number hides.

The two most-feared harms do not survive the filter — but that is not the same as exoneration. Cancer
outcomes produced several nominal signals and **none** met the prespecified credibility thresholds
[@yang2026]. Thyroid cancer, the
rodent-C-cell scare, came back as an imprecise, non-significant estimate compatible with higher odds
— OR 1.43 (0.95-2.13), P=.08 — and the umbrella notes the rodent finding's human relevance remains
unconfirmed. Pancreatic cancer's nominal signal ran the *other* way, toward lower odds — OR 0.51
(0.30-0.85), P=.01 — but stayed exploratory. The correct reading is **no robust human signal on
current evidence**, held as insufficient/unsettled, not as proof the drug does not cause cancer. The
trials run months to a few years; a long-latency malignancy could not realistically have surfaced yet
(the expectancy test with a latency caveat), so absence of a robust short-to-medium-term signal is
not a long-term all-clear. [@yang2026]

Between the robust harm and the absent one sits an unsettled middle, and lumping its members is the
error to avoid — each fails the credibility bar for a different reason. Gallbladder or biliary disease
is a *statistically significant* elevated association, OR 1.34 (1.16-1.55), P<.001; it is
"exploratory" only because its prediction interval sits close to the null, a robustness caveat, not a
doubt about the harm [@yang2026].
Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease holds the same posture — significant but credibility-demoted — OR
2.19 (1.65-2.90), P<.001 [@yang2026].
Pancreatitis is different in kind: here the demotion is about *causality*, not robustness — the
scientific consensus on whether GLP-1 RAs cause pancreatitis is explicitly unsettled, with a
propensity-matched analysis finding no excess after adjustment against a regulatory update flagging
rare fatal cases [@yang2026]. The posture is
targeted monitoring in symptomatic or high-risk patients, calibrated to which of these three states
applies — not one blanket attribution.

The umbrella also surfaces off-target signals that point the *protective* way — and these are
candidate signals to confirm, not banked benefits. Fewer serious infections, OR 0.89 (0.87-0.92)
(GRADE high, and the one class-I *convincing* association among the review's highlighted outcomes);
fewer fractures, OR 0.67 (0.52-0.87), P=.003; and less all-cause dementia, OR 0.55 (0.35-0.87), P=.01
[@yang2026]. Two of these matter for
ranking: reduced fracture risk offsets the sarcopenia/fall worry that rapid weight loss otherwise
raises, and a large protective dementia effect, *if real*, is decision-relevant. But the umbrella
calls them hypothesis-generating, and none licenses a recommendation — they belong in the ledger as
directionally protective blanks being watched, not as established effects.
[@yang2026]

A second gold umbrella sharpens why *if real* is the right posture on the dementia signal, by showing
it is not specific to these drugs. Kuate Defo's umbrella review and meta-analysis of antidiabetic
classes against dementia (100 reviews plus 27 cohort/case-control studies, N=3,046,661) found GLP-1
receptor agonists associated with reduced dementia risk, RR 0.35 (0.16-0.78) — but so were metformin,
the thiazolidinediones/pioglitazone, and SGLT2 inhibitors, while DPP-4 inhibitors,
alpha-glucosidase inhibitors and insulin were null and meglitinides and sulphonylureas ran the
*other* way, toward increased risk
[@kuatedefo2023]. A protective signal
shared across several unrelated classes that all improve glycaemia, and reversed by the ones prone to
hypoglycaemia, points at glycemic control or confounding by indication rather than anything
GLP-1-specific — and the estimate carries near-total heterogeneity (I2 = 98.5%), with even metformin's
own signal holding only in Western, not Eastern, populations
[@kuatedefo2023]. This is also not a
second independent line: both umbrellas pool the same observational pharmacoepidemiology (confounding
by indication, channeling, prevalent-user bias), so they share primary cohorts and cannot
cross-confirm. At most it refines Yang's signal with drug-class context; the dementia entry stays a
watched protective blank, not a demonstrated GLP-1 benefit.
[inferred from @kuatedefo2023]

Two facts make any version of this ledger provisional. The first is mechanical fragility: in
leave-one-trial-out reanalysis, the direction of effect flipped in roughly 9 of 39 outcomes and
statistical significance was lost in 6 of 39 after removing a single trial
[@yang2026]. An association a single trial
can overturn is not one to dose a recommendation on. The second is a moving target: the mature
multi-year evidence sits on one older molecule (semaglutide), while prescribing moves to agents that
add receptor targets it never tested (tirzepatide adds GIP, retatrutide adds glucagon) and to higher
obesity-range doses, and the umbrella could not stratify harms by dose or duration
[@yang2026]. Accrued safety years de-risk
the *specific compound at the specific dose that accrued them*; the class label stays constant while
the exposure under it drifts. So the shared-mechanism entries (the GI cluster, the recognized
gallbladder and thyroid-C-cell signals) are informed by every agent, but whatever is molecule-,
dose-, or new-target-specific starts its safety clock over.

This class is neither the poison nor the panacea its two loudest
narratives claim. The one robust harm (GI) is a manageable, front-loaded adherence cost; the feared
cancers show no robust human signal but are not cleared for the long term; the regulatory-attention
middle splits into significant-but-credibility-demoted (gallbladder, GERD) versus
causally-unsettled (pancreatitis); and the protective signals are real enough to log and too fragile
to promise. [inferred from @yang2026]

Every number above assumes the drug is still being taken — which raises the question the trials mostly did not answer: what happens when you stop.

## Stopping the drug gives the weight back — and the benefit with it

The STEP-1 off-treatment extension quantifies what *reverses on stopping* means. When both
semaglutide 2.4 mg and the lifestyle programme were withdrawn at week 68, participants regained a
mean of 11.6 percentage points of body weight over the following year, versus 1.9 percentage points
in the placebo arm — about two-thirds of the prior loss, leaving a net \~5.6% below baseline and
fewer than half (48.2%) still holding a >=5% loss
[@wilding2022]. Cardiometabolic gains reverted
toward baseline in step, and the trialists conclude that ongoing treatment is required to maintain
the improvements in weight and health
[@wilding2022].

### The set point is defended, not reset

Read this as physiology, not relapse of resolve. After diet-induced loss the body's
appetite-regulating hormones shift in the direction that promotes eating and regain — leptin still
\~35.5% below baseline, ghrelin elevated — and those shifts persist a full year out, after regain has
already begun, so the high relapse rate has a strong physiological basis and is
«not simply the result of the voluntary resumption of old habits»
[@sumithran2011]. A GLP-1 receptor agonist
does not cure this adaptation; it pharmacologically resupplies one of the depleted satiety signals
from the outside, overriding the defended set point only while the drug is present
-> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]]. Remove it and the endogenous deficit
reasserts. One caveat the source itself flags: the extension withdrew the lifestyle support too, so
it cannot cleanly separate biological rebound from behavioural drift
[@wilding2022].

### The cardiovascular and kidney benefit is rented, not owned

The GI adverse effects are transient — concentrated during dose escalation, mostly mild to moderate.
The benefit runs the opposite way: it is continuation-dependent. SELECT's MACE reduction (HR 0.80,
95% CI 0.72 to 0.90) [@lincoff2023] and
the drug's kidney benefit ([[Semaglutide and Kidney Outcomes in Chronic Kidney Disease]]) are
properties of the **on-drug state**, and no trial has yet shown either persisting once the drug is
stopped — the withdrawal data show weight and every cardiometabolic surrogate drifting back toward
baseline when treatment ends. So a GLP-1 course is not a one-time correction that banks a durable
gain; it is a maintained state whose benefit decays on the timescale of stopping — the cumulative,
continuation-dependent counterpart to the transient GI tolerability tax.
[inferred from @wilding2022; @lincoff2023]

### Indefinite therapy, behavioural maintenance, or surgery — the lifetime choice

This makes the real decision a lifetime one, across three routes to a *sustained* result. Unaided
behavioural maintenance is the weakest — it works against the defended physiology above, so regain is
the modal outcome. Indefinite drug therapy holds the weight and the benefit only for as long as it is
taken. Bariatric surgery is the durable-structural comparator: in SOS, sustained loss of 14-25% at 10
years came with an all-cause mortality reduction — adjusted HR 0.71 (P=0.01), unadjusted 0.76
(P=0.04) — that does not depend on daily adherence
[@sjostrom2007]
-> [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]. SOS is non-randomized (self-selected surgical
arm) and carries its own peri-operative harm, so it is not a clean head-to-head; but it anchors the
one thing the drug route structurally lacks — a durable outcome that survives the intervention being
over.

### Name the axis — cost, access, supply, adherence

Indefinite therapy loads heavily on a second axis the health evidence cannot price: drug cost,
access, supply constraints, and the demand for lifelong adherence. Per
[[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]] and the *one axis, named — never blended* rule, the
discipline is to record *that* the trade-off exists and *which way* it runs — the
continuation-dependent route is the one most exposed to it, since its benefit is bought only as long
as the therapy is paid for and tolerated — and to stop there, without pricing it or netting it
against the mortality and event findings. Whether lifelong cost-and-adherence outweighs a rented
benefit is a layer-3 judgment for the person, not a number this evidence supplies.


That leaves the boundary of what any of this evidence can currently say.

## What the current evidence cannot yet see

These are gaps, not nulls. A gap is *insufficient evidence* — the question has not been
answered because the study to answer it has not run long enough or been built at all; it is
not *evidence of no effect*. The distinction is load-bearing here because the evidence horizon
and the use horizon are mismatched, and the mismatch is structural — more searching now cannot
close it.

**Lifetime and long-latency outcomes are unmeasured, and silence is not reassurance.** The
umbrella evidence base runs on follow-up «ranging from 3 months to 5.4 years or longer»
[@yang2026], while the use
horizon for weight and metabolic maintenance is measured in decades. So a cluster of outcomes
sits in the *insufficient-evidence* state, not the *no-effect* one: cancer over a full latency
window; bone and fracture risk over decades (the near-term signal is a *reduced*-fracture
association, itself exploratory, on [[GLP-1 Non-Cardiometabolic Effects and Safety]]); pregnancy
and fetal exposure; adolescent growth and development; and cardiometabolic status *after
discontinuation*, where regain and its sequelae are largely unstudied. The **expectancy test**
governs how to read the quiet here: for a long-latency harm, absence of a signal in a few-year
trial base is exactly what you would see *whether or not* the harm is real — the studies could
not yet have caught it — so the current quiet is uninformative, not exculpatory. It licenses
neither alarm nor an all-clear.

**Attribution is unresolved: drug-specific vs generic-to-the-loss.** Several concerns raised
against GLP-1 drugs — lean-mass loss ([[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]]), facial and skin adipose loss —
may be effects of *rapid, large weight loss by any route* rather than of the molecule. The
evidence cannot separate the two, because doing so needs a matched comparator losing the same
magnitude of weight at the same rate by other means, and no such comparator is held. Until one
is, "the drug does X" and "losing this much this fast does X" remain observationally entangled —
an open attribution question, not a settled drug harm.

**Unknown-unknowns are unquantifiable by construction — so name the two honest proxies.** By
definition, no list exists of the harms nobody has thought to measure. What *can* be stated is
the shape of the exposure that makes them likely to exist and unlikely to have surfaced: (i) the
**evidence-horizon vs use-horizon** mismatch above, and (ii) the **moving-target problem** — the
exposure drifts under a constant label. Mature multi-year evidence sits on older, lower-dose
semaglutide, while prescribing moves to newer molecules (added GIP, glucagon targets) at higher
obesity-range doses that the mature record never tested; the umbrella flags that «inconsistent reporting in primary trials precluded detailed stratification by dose or treatment duration»
[@yang2026]. So accrued years
de-risk the specific compound and dose that accrued them, not the class label; the safety clock
partly resets for whatever just changed -> [[GLP-1 Non-Cardiometabolic Effects and Safety]].

None of this argues against the drug; it bounds how far the current evidence can carry a
decision, and hands the weighing back to the reader.

## Start from your baseline risk, not the scale

If you already have established cardiovascular disease — or type-2 diabetes with kidney
disease — a GLP-1 is a large, proven lever on the outcomes that matter (events, and in the
diabetes-kidney stratum, kidney failure), and it ranks as a Big Rock. If you are
near-normal weight with no cardiometabolic disease, you are buying a reliable weight and
surrogate change whose hard-outcome payoff is unproven, at the same adverse-event cost.
Place yourself in the right stratum before you decide anything else.


## Expect a maintained state, not a course

Plan for the drug as an ongoing commitment, not a fixed-length treatment: stopping returns
about two-thirds of the lost weight within a year, because the body defends the higher
weight. Budget the cost, tolerability, and reversibility over years, and compare that
against a sustained lifestyle change on the same terms — not against an idealized one-time
fix.

## If you proceed, protect muscle

Fast, large weight loss takes muscle with the fat. Resistance training and adequate protein — the
ordinary mitigations for any large deficit — lower the functional
cost without touching the weight benefit -> [[GLP-1 and Lean Mass]]
.

## Weigh the trade-off yourself — the health axis and beyond

The evidence names what the drug moves; it does not tell you how to price weight loss
against gastrointestinal burden, lifelong cost, or an unproven event benefit. Two loads sit
off the health axis and are flagged here, not priced: cost and access, for which the wiki
holds no data to weight on your behalf. Bring your own weighting; the fabric supplies only
the directions and the certainties.
