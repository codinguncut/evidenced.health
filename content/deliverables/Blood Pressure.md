## Lower pressure prevents strokes and heart attacks at every starting level tested; how much you gain depends on your overall risk

**Lowering systolic pressure prevents strokes, heart attacks and heart failure, in proportion to the
drop.** Drug trials show a similar relative benefit for each millimetre across every starting pressure
they tested, from below 120 to 170 mmHg and above. The benefit holds in people with or without
existing heart disease.
[@bplttc2021]
In placebo-controlled trials it also brought modestly fewer cases of dementia (see *A second outcome:
dementia*). [inferred from @peters2022bp]

**How much that is worth to you depends on your overall cardiovascular risk, not on the reading
alone.** The trialists say treatment decisions should turn on a person's overall risk of future
cardiovascular events, not on the reading alone.
[@bplttc2021] So the
same drop buys a person at high risk far more than it buys a person at low risk.
([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]])

**Drug trials carry the proof of fewer events; lifestyle changes mostly carry proof of a lower
reading.** For most lifestyle changes, the payoff in events is assumed: it follows only if the
drug-trial rate applies to pressure lowered by other means. Among the diet changes appraised here for
their blood-pressure effect, only salt substitution has a trial that counted fewer events alongside a
measured drop in pressure
[@neal2021]; the Mediterranean diet's
event result from PREDIMED is not tied to blood pressure. For cutting heavy drinking, a risk model
projects the events; no trial counted them
[@roerecke2017alcohol].

Among weight-loss routes, one drug has measured an event benefit, and only in people who already had
cardiovascular disease. In people with type 2 diabetes, Look AHEAD's lifestyle weight loss initially
moved blood pressure and other markers but did not detectably reduce events
([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]], [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]).


**Structured exercise and, in heavy drinkers, cutting back on alcohol give the largest well-measured
lifestyle drops** in people with high blood pressure. Weight loss and DASH-style eating with less salt
lower pressure too, but the held lifestyle weight-loss figures are modest (DiRECT -3.4 mmHg
[@lean2019]; the best named diets, Atkins and DASH,
about 5 mmHg at 6 months, largely gone by 12
[@ge2020]), and the larger
weight-loss drops come from drugs.

The alcohol effect appears only in heavier drinkers; people at two drinks a day or fewer saw no
significant change [@roerecke2017alcohol]. Exercise
lowers pressure by an amount similar to drugs in hypertensive trials. But no trial has compared the two
directly, and no exercise trial has counted events
[@naci2019exercise]. Most exercise trials
were at high risk of bias and the comparison is indirect, so the certainty is low to moderate
.

**Do not add the drops together.** Where it was tested (DASH plus less salt), two good changes together
lowered pressure by less than their sum, and for other pairs nobody has measured the combination.


**Garlic supplements, beetroot nitrate, magnesium and relaxation practices such as mindfulness and yoga
report drops that are either small or rest on weak trials.** The biggest headline numbers in this
group either shrink under stricter tests (yoga, slow breathing, and mindfulness in unmedicated
patients) or rest on weak trials (garlic). None has event data. Here, the bigger the reported drop,
the weaker the evidence behind it tends to be.
[inferred from @ried2008; @ma2025garlic; @geiger2025yoga; @chaddha2019; @chen2024mbi; @norouzzadeh2025nitrate; @zhang2016magnesiumbp; @dibaba2017mg]

**Who should act depends on who you are.** For someone with high pressure and high overall risk, lowering
it is one of the largest health levers available; drugs carry the proof of fewer events, and for other
routes the event payoff is assumed. Lifestyle changes still lower the reading on top of drugs, by less
in the DASH trials, and no held trial measures how much the two add together for events. **For a
low-risk person with normal pressure, the same millimetres buy little absolute benefit.** That is a
finding in its own right: a reason to stop optimizing, not a sign that blood pressure does not matter.
([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]])

This appraisal covers diet, weight, physical activity, alcohol, specific nutrients and supplements, and
stress management, with blood-pressure drugs as the realistic alternative. Choosing, dosing and adjusting
a drug is a prescriber's job and is not covered. Salt and potassium appear here only in outline; their
detail lives in [[Sodium]].

All of this rests on one step being well proven: that a lower reading actually turns into fewer events.

## A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by about a tenth, at every starting pressure tested

#### Two steps, two kinds of evidence

**Any blood-pressure lever has to clear two separate steps.** First it must lower the reading; then the
lower reading must prevent strokes, heart attacks and heart failure. A trial that measures only the
first step is measuring a surrogate, and GRADE treats that as a real cost: relying on a surrogate
outcome generally means rating the evidence down by one or two levels. [@grade]
A drop in pressure counts only once the second step is shown ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).

**The evidence on blood pressure is strongest where it matters least.** A BP response can be randomised and
measured in weeks in small trials; strokes and deaths cannot. So the reading is usually the better-graded
quantity. WHO's sodium guideline shows the pattern: high certainty for the effect on blood pressure, lower
(mostly low or very low) for the effect on hard outcomes. [inferred from @who2012] A
reader who acts on *the
strongest evidence* is pulled toward the reading by the grading itself. For blood pressure, unusually, the
second step has also been measured directly, in drug trials.

#### What the drug trials show

**The Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration (BPLTTC) pooled individual data from
344,716 people in 48 randomised trials.**
[@bplttc2021] Its
headline: «a 5 mm Hg reduction of systolic blood pressure reduced
the risk of major cardiovascular events by about 10%, irrespective of previous diagnoses of cardiovascular
disease, and even at normal or high–normal blood pressure values.»
[@bplttc2021]

- **Without prior cardiovascular disease:** HR 0.91 (95% CI 0.89-0.94) per 5 mmHg lower SBP.
- **With prior cardiovascular disease:** HR 0.89 (0.86-0.92).
- **By component, per 5 mmHg:** stroke down 13%, heart failure 13%, ischaemic heart disease 8%,
  cardiovascular death 5%.

[@bplttc2021]

**The effect did not fade at lower starting pressures.** Across seven baseline-SBP bands, from below 120 to
170 mmHg and above, the trialists found «no reliable evidence of heterogeneity», and in no band did the
hazard ratio rise above 1.0.
[@bplttc2021] **That
is a statement about the relative effect of each millimetre, not about how many
millimetres a given lever delivers.** Exercise lowered pressure less in trials with normal starting
readings; for DASH the held evidence is mixed. The section *Overall cardiovascular risk decides who
gains most; starting pressure, age and kidney function come next* takes that up.

#### The proof comes from drugs; for lifestyle it is mostly assumed

**Every one of these event figures comes from drug-lowered blood pressure.** For a lifestyle lever, the
event payoff is assumed to follow at the same rate per millimetre, not observed.
[[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]] states the limit plainly: the magnitude applies to
pharmacological lowering and should not be transferred silently to another route. The sections
that follow therefore present each lever on that condition: *if the drug-trial rate applies*.

**One diet change has counted events alongside a measured drop in pressure: salt substitution.** In
SSaSS, 20,995 high-risk adults in rural China given a potassium-enriched salt substitute had SBP
3.34 mmHg lower and fewer strokes (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.96).
[@neal2021] At BPLTTC's 13% per 5 mmHg
for stroke, that pressure drop predicts an RR near 0.91. **The observed point estimate is larger than
the blood-pressure channel predicts, but the prediction sits inside the confidence interval, so the
excess may be chance.** If real, it would fit an effect of the electrolytes beyond blood pressure, or a
sustained BP fall larger than the one measured.
[inferred from @neal2021] The derivation, and the
sodium-potassium detail, are in [[Sodium]].

Over a mean 4.74 years the substitute also cut major cardiovascular events (rate ratio 0.87, 0.80 to
0.94) and deaths (0.88, 0.82 to 0.95); 72.6% of participants had already had a stroke
[@neal2021]. The result carries two
limits. The substitute lowers sodium and raises potassium at once, so the benefit belongs to the combined
switch, not to either electrolyte alone. And with a population this sick, the absolute benefit will be
much smaller at lower risk. [inferred from @neal2021]

#### The relative effect is constant; your absolute gain is not

**The absolute gain changes from person to person, and it scales with overall cardiovascular
risk.** GRADE notes that the relative effect is usually similar across baseline risks while the case for
treatment differs by them [@grade]. The absolute reduction equals baseline
risk times (1 minus the hazard ratio). Applied to a 5 mmHg drop, using the
primary-prevention HR of 0.91:

- **At 10% ten-year risk:** about 9 fewer major events per 1,000 people over 10 years (CI roughly 6 to 11).
- **At 2.5% ten-year risk:** about 2 fewer per 1,000 (roughly 1.5 to 3).

[inferred from @bplttc2021; @esc2021]
The two baselines are illustrations; where they fall on ESC's age-banded thresholds is in
*Overall cardiovascular risk decides who gains most; starting pressure, age and kidney function come
next* ([[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]).

**Three assumptions sit inside those numbers, and the reader can reject any of them.** The hazard ratio is
assumed to hold constant over a full 10 years. SCORE2's endpoint (fatal and non-fatal myocardial
infarction and stroke) [@esc2021] omits the heart
failure that BPLTTC's major-event composite counts
[@bplttc2021], so the
hazard ratio is applied to a different composite. And the BP drop is assumed to come from a drug,
because that is where the rate was measured. [inferred from @esc2021]

**The trialists themselves warn against reading their absolute numbers as policy.** They «caution against
using the reported absolute risk differences from clinical trials for making policy decisions», because
randomised trials are strongest on relative effects.
[@bplttc2021] Their
trial populations ran at about 32 major events per 1,000
person-years in the control arms without prior CVD, well above a low-risk person's rate.
[@bplttc2021] The
same paper says a uniform
relative effect does not mean every patient is worth treating; the decision should rest on overall risk,
side effects, cost and preference ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).
[@bplttc2021]

#### At low risk, a proven effect still buys little

**Of the four interventions the wiki compares for low-risk people, blood-pressure lowering is the
clearest test, because its relative benefit on hard events in primary prevention is proven.**
Semaglutide and lifestyle
weight loss are unproven in that group, and statins, whose relative benefit is real, fall below action
thresholds at low risk. Even so, a proven 10% per 5 mmHg on a low baseline is a small
absolute gain ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]).

preDIVA shows how small. Its 6-year nurse-led vascular-care programme (median follow-up 6.7 years) in
3,526 unselected adults aged 70-78 lowered SBP by only 2.06 mmHg and found no effect on
cardiovascular disease (HR 1.06, 0.86-1.31).
[@mollvancharante2016] Through the BPLTTC
rate, a 2 mmHg contrast predicts an HR of about 0.96, far too small for a trial of this size to detect.
So preDIVA's null says more about how little pressure it moved than about who was treated.
[inferred from @mollvancharante2016]

#### A second outcome: dementia

**Lowering blood pressure probably lowers the risk of dementia modestly.** An individual-participant
meta-analysis of
five double-blind placebo-controlled drug trials (28,008 people) found an adjusted odds ratio of 0.87 (95%
CI 0.75-0.99) for incident dementia, with a mean BP reduction of 10/4 mmHg. Dementia occurred in 2.9% of the
treated and 3.3% of the placebo groups. [@peters2022bp]
That is about 4 fewer cases per 1,000 over the trials' follow-up, likely an underestimate because the
trials stopped early on their cardiovascular endpoints and dementia accrues more slowly.
[inferred from @peters2022bp] In observational data, people with untreated
hypertension had a 42% higher dementia risk than healthy controls (HR 1.42; 1.15-1.76); among treated
people the excess was attenuated or lost. [@livingston2024]

These five trials sit inside the BPLTTC pool, so the dementia result extends the outcome list; it is not
independent support for the per-millimetre effect ([[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]]).
[inferred from @peters2022bp; @bplttc2021]
As with heart attacks and strokes, the dementia benefit is shown for drug-lowered pressure; that a
lifestyle-lowered drop reaches dementia is assumed.
[inferred from @peters2022bp]

If each millimetre is worth roughly the same, the practical question becomes which changes deliver the
most millimetres, and how sure we are that they do.

## Exercise and cutting heavy drinking give the largest well-measured lifestyle drops; weight loss is best measured with drugs

Structured exercise and, in heavy drinkers, cutting back on alcohol have the best-measured lifestyle
effects on the reading; weight loss lowers it too, with the larger figures coming from drug trials.
**Each lowers systolic pressure by a few to about 9 mmHg in the people studied.** None of the lifestyle
versions has trial evidence of fewer events of its own; each figure below is a BP drop (see *The proof
comes from drugs; for lifestyle it is mostly assumed*).

### Exercise

**In hypertensive trial participants, structured exercise lowered systolic pressure about as much as
drug treatment did, judged indirectly.** The evidence is one network meta-analysis of 391 RCTs: 197
trials of exercise (10,461 people) and 194 of antihypertensive drugs (29,281 people). Endurance and
dynamic resistance training were among the exercise types tested. No trial compared exercise with a
drug directly, so every exercise-versus-drug figure is inferred across separate sets of trials.
[@naci2019exercise]

The headline numbers mislead unless you match the starting pressure. Across all populations, the drug
trials' systolic change was -8.80 mmHg (95% CrI -9.58 to -8.02) and the exercise trials' -4.84 (-5.55 to
-4.13), a -3.96 mmHg (-5.02 to -2.91) advantage to drugs. But the exercise trials began at a mean of
132 mmHg, and
the drug trials started above 150. **Restricted to people starting at 140 or higher, exercise changed
systolic pressure by -8.96 mmHg (-10.27 to -7.64), and the gap to drugs was 0.18 (-1.35 to 1.68), no
difference.** That matched estimate rests on 56 exercise trials and 3,508 people.
[@naci2019exercise]

Exercise's drop grew with the starting pressure, so the unmatched contrast compared two different
quantities. This is a between-trial subgroup comparison, not an interaction test
([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).
[inferred from @naci2019exercise]

The authors hedge the match twice, and the hedges stand. Their conclusion holds only «Assuming equally
reliable estimates» [@naci2019exercise]. The estimates
are not equally reliable: 17 of 20 sampled exercise trials were at high risk of bias from lack of
blinding, and the authors call the network's linking assumption tenuous because the two sets of trials
started at different pressures. [@naci2019exercise]
The authors also note a bias running the other way: they may have underestimated exercise, because they
excluded ineffective or suboptimal drug doses but no exercise trials on that basis
[@naci2019exercise]. Exercise cannot be blinded,
so that weakness is built in.

**Single exercise types lowered pressure about equally; combining endurance and resistance training did
best.** Naci also compared exercise types with one another: «Overall, different types of structured
exercise interventions achieved similar reductions from baseline (table 2).» «One excep- tion was the
combination of endurance and resistance training, which was more effective in reducing baseline SBP
than dynamic resistance alone (−2.98, 95% CrI −5.04 to −0.93).»
[@naci2019exercise] In trials of people starting at
140 mmHg or higher, the combination beat endurance, resistance and isometric training alike, and lowered
systolic pressure by 13.51 mmHg (95% CrI -16.55 to -10.45) against control.
[@naci2019exercise]

Three cautions keep that edge provisional. Only 30 of the 197 exercise trials compared one type with
another, so most type contrasts are network estimates inferred across trials. The type groups started
at different pressures, a mean 125 mmHg in resistance trials and 135 in combination trials
[@naci2019exercise]. And Naci
itself flags how thin the combination evidence is: «For example, only a small subset of studies
identified in our review tested the effectiveness of the combi- nation of endurance and dynamic
resistance training. Although these types of interventions were often effective, especially in
hypertensive populations, their frequency and duration were not consistently reported to determine the
optimal formulation and dose of physical activity to maximise its benefit.»
[@naci2019exercise]

Since a higher start brings a larger drop, part of the all-populations edge may be baseline; the edge
held in the 140-plus trials, which partly controls for that. Part may also be more total training
rather than the mix itself. Among single types, the one a person will keep doing is a reasonable choice;
for a hypertensive person who can do both, indirect evidence leans toward the combination
([[Is the Exercise Modality Doing Any Work]]).

**The fair summary: a similar systolic drop in hypertensive trials, at low-to-moderate certainty, with
no event data for exercise.** Naci measured no strokes or heart attacks. Exercise also improves
fitness, glucose control and weight, benefits a BP-only comparison leaves out
([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]], [[Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality]]).
[inferred from @naci2019exercise]

### Alcohol

**Cutting back lowers blood pressure only in people drinking three or more drinks a day.** A 36-trial
meta-analysis (2,865 participants) found that people at two drinks a day or fewer saw no significant BP
change when they cut to near abstinence. Above that, the drop grew with the amount drunk.
[@roerecke2017alcohol]

| Usual intake before cutting | Systolic change (mmHg, 95% CI) |
|---|---|
| 2 drinks/day or fewer | -0.18 (-1.02 to 0.66), not significant |
| 3 drinks/day | -1.18 (-2.32 to -0.04) |
| 4-5 drinks/day | -3.00 (-3.98 to -2.03) |
| 6 or more drinks/day | -5.50 (-6.70 to -4.30) |

[@roerecke2017alcohol]

Across all trials the systolic change averaged -3.13 mmHg (-3.93 to -2.32), with high heterogeneity that
starting intake almost entirely explained. The trial strata averaged 15, 30, 49 and 76 g of alcohol a
day, so the curve was measured across real drinking levels, not extrapolated. The authors rate the
certainty of the dose-response as high.
[@roerecke2017alcohol]

Genetic evidence agrees that alcohol raises pressure. A Mendelian randomization study uses gene
variants that change how much people drink as a natural experiment. In Chinese men, systolic pressure
rose about 4.3 mmHg (3.7 to 4.9) per 280 g a week; in women the variants did not raise intake. The same
study found alcohol causally raises stroke risk.
[@millwood2019]

**The event payoff is modelled, not measured.** Using a risk-assessment model, Roerecke projected more
than 7,000 UK hospital admissions and 678 cardiovascular deaths avoided each year. The model assumed
people kept the lower intake and that the benefit arrived without delay.
[@roerecke2017alcohol] At the drug-trial
rate set out in *A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by about a tenth, at every starting pressure tested*,
a heavy drinker's 5.5 mmHg drop would predict roughly 10% fewer major cardiovascular events. That figure
is the wiki's arithmetic. It holds only if pressure lowered by drinking less transmits like
drug-lowered pressure.
[inferred from @roerecke2017alcohol; @bplttc2021]

This BP lever is separate from the old claim that light drinking protects the heart. That protective
dip in mortality curves disappears once researchers stop counting people who quit because they were
already ill as abstainers, and genetic analyses do not reproduce it
([[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]).

### Weight loss

No source held here gives a figure for mmHg per kilogram lost, so the evidence comes as separate
results from drug trials and lifestyle trials. Weight loss also shifts diet, so its BP effect
overlaps with the diet
changes in *DASH and less salt each lower pressure, and together less than their sum*, which explains
why the drops should not be added. ([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]])

#### The drug route

**The weight-loss drugs lowered systolic pressure by roughly 3 to 8 mmHg, and only one trial has shown
fewer events.** In STEP-1, semaglutide in adults with obesity, without diabetes and mostly without heart
disease lowered systolic pressure by 5.10 mmHg relative to placebo at 68 weeks.
[@wilding2021] In SELECT, the same drug
lowered systolic pressure by 3.31 mmHg in people with established cardiovascular disease, and cut major
cardiovascular events by 20% (HR 0.80, 0.72 to 0.90). The benefit appeared early after treatment
started, and the authors suggest changes beyond weight loss may explain part of it. They offer that
mechanism as speculation, so no single marker, BP included, is known to carry the benefit.
[@lincoff2023]
([[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]])

Tirzepatide, in two 52-week trials of people with obesity and sleep apnoea (469 participants,
manufacturer-funded), changed systolic pressure at week 48 by -7.6 mmHg (-10.5 to -4.8) in one trial
and -3.7 (-6.8 to
-0.7) in the other. These trials had no cardiovascular endpoint; the authors say the ongoing
SURMOUNT-MMO outcome trial may supply event data.
[@malhotra2024surmount]

**The pressure drop lasts only as long as the drug.** When STEP-1 participants stopped semaglutide and
the lifestyle programme at week 68, they regained two-thirds of the weight they had lost within a year.
By week 120 systolic and diastolic pressure were back to baseline. The trialists concluded that the
findings «suggest ongoing treatment is required to maintain improvements in weight and health».
[@wilding2022] For a drug, adherence is part of the
effect.

#### The lifestyle route

**Look AHEAD shows that markers can improve while events do not.** In 5,145 adults with type 2 diabetes
and overweight or obesity, an intensive diet-and-activity programme improved HbA1c and produced greater
initial improvements in fitness and every cardiovascular risk factor except LDL cholesterol, blood
pressure included. After a median 9.6 years, cardiovascular events were no lower (HR 0.95, 0.83 to
1.09). [@look2013] Read Look
AHEAD as a caution about trusting a bundle of surrogates, not as a refutation of BP transfer. The
gap in weight between the
arms shrank to 2.5 percentage points by the end, and no source held here gives a figure for how far the
arms' blood pressure actually differed.
([[Surrogate Outcomes]])

A meta-analysis of 54 weight-loss RCTs (30,206 adults with obesity, almost all on low-fat
weight-reducing diets) found the same on the heart: in the 24 trials (15,176 participants) that reported
new cardiovascular events, the risk ratio was 0.93 (0.83 to 1.04), high-quality evidence
[@ma2017]. Look AHEAD is among Ma's trials, so
this generalizes its null rather than independently confirming it
([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]).

In DiRECT, a total-diet-replacement programme for people with short-duration type 2 diabetes lowered
systolic pressure at two years (difference -3.43 mmHg, 95% CI -6.70 to -0.16), with fewer people on
blood-pressure drugs
(47.3% versus 60.1%). [@lean2019]
([[Total Diet Replacement and Type 2 Diabetes Remission]])

#### Sleep apnoea: the device showed no detectable drop in pressure

Treating sleep apnoea with CPAP did not detectably lower systolic pressure in pooled trials: -0.20 mmHg
(-2.29 to 1.89). [@yu2017cpap]
Tirzepatide's 7.6 mmHg
drop came in the same kind of patient, people with sleep apnoea. **The weight-loss drug moved the
reading; in these trials the device did not detectably.** The tirzepatide trials enrolled heavier
people (mean BMI about 39 versus
30), so the contrast is suggestive, not a head-to-head result
([[Sleep Apnea Treatment and Cardiovascular Risk]]).
[inferred from @yu2017cpap; @malhotra2024surmount]

## DASH and less salt each lower pressure, and together less than their sum

Food changes move blood pressure even when body weight stays put. In the DASH-Sodium feeding trial,
investigators held every participant's weight constant and still saw pressure fall on both diet and
salt [@sacks2001dashsodium].
**DASH is among the best-studied patterns for blood pressure, and it is the one lever where a trial
tested what happens when two good changes are combined** ([[DASH Diet and Blood Pressure]]).

### DASH lowers systolic pressure by about 3 to 5 mmHg, depending on how you pool the trials

DASH means more fruit, vegetables, low-fat dairy and whole grains, and less red meat, sweets and
saturated fat. Two meta-analyses of DASH against a control diet give **two different systolic
estimates, and the held evidence cannot say which is closer to the truth.**

- **Siervo pooled 20 RCTs** (1917 people, 2-24 weeks): SBP -5.2 mmHg (-7.0 to -3.4), DBP -2.6 (-3.5 to
  -1.7), with high heterogeneity (I2 76%), a small LDL fall (-0.10 mmol/l) and no significant effect on
  glucose or HDL.
  [@siervo2015]
- **Filippou pooled 30 RCTs** (5545 people, mean follow-up about 15 weeks, average starting pressure
  134/85 mmHg): SBP -3.2 mmHg (-4.2 to -2.3), DBP -2.5 (-3.5 to -1.5). Some trials lacked blinding or
  complete outcome data, so certainty is moderate: DASH probably lowers pressure by about this much.
  [@filippou2020dash]

The two figures are not the same quantity. Filippou compared pressure at the end of the trial; Siervo
mostly compared change from baseline. Only 15 of Filippou's 30 trials appear in Siervo. Nothing held
separates the effect of the metric from the effect of the different trial sets, so this guide quotes
both and pools neither. Filippou's pool also admitted trials that paired DASH with salt
restriction, weight loss or exercise, so part of its -3.2 may come from those add-ons.
[inferred from @filippou2020dash; @siervo2015]
Durability is unknown past roughly 15 weeks: Filippou states the short follow-up means the effect
cannot be assumed to last longer.
[@filippou2020dash]

The trials test the whole pattern, so no single food or nutrient can be credited. Siervo's
meta-regression found that the incidental sodium difference between the DASH and control arms did not
predict the BP change
[@siervo2015];
a null meta-regression has little power, so this bounds sodium's share
rather than excluding it.

**Do not expect a bigger DASH drop just because your pressure starts higher.** The two pooled
analyses disagree on whether the drop grows with baseline pressure (figures in *Overall cardiovascular
risk decides who gains most; starting pressure, age and kidney function come next*).
[inferred from @filippou2020dash; @siervo2015]

**No trial has counted heart attacks or strokes on DASH.** Cohorts link closer DASH-style eating to
lower incident CVD (RR 0.80, 0.76-0.85) and stroke (RR 0.81, 0.72-0.92), in an umbrella review whose
cohort layer spans 15 cohorts and 942,140 people; the umbrella review rates both associations low
certainty. [@chiavaroli2019] The often-quoted
figure of about 13% lower 10-year risk is a Framingham risk-score projection from the marker changes,
not counted events [@siervo2015]. A
DASH score in a
cohort is also likely to track other healthy habits.

The umbrella review's own blood-pressure figure is Siervo's estimate re-imported, so it is the same
number, not a second confirmation of it.
[inferred from @chiavaroli2019; @siervo2015]
For DASH, the step from pressure to events is assumed, not measured. If the drug-trial rate from
*A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by about a tenth, at every starting pressure tested* applies to
diet-lowered pressure, DASH's few mmHg would buy a proportionate cut in events.

### DASH plus less salt beats either alone, but falls short of the sum

DASH-Sodium randomized 412 adults with systolic pressure of 120-159 mmHg (41% with stage-1
hypertension, none on BP drugs) to DASH or a typical US diet, then fed each person high, intermediate
and low sodium for 30 days each. All food was provided. Achieved urinary sodium was about 142, 107 and
65 mmol/d. [@sacks2001dashsodium]
The trial sits inside both the Siervo and Filippou pools, so it refines them rather than adding a second
witness to the pooled DASH effect.
[inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium; @filippou2020dash; @siervo2015]

**Each lever did less when the other was already in place:**

- DASH lowered SBP by 5.9 mmHg (-8.0 to -3.7) against the control diet at high sodium, but only 2.2
  (-4.4 to -0.1) at low sodium. [@sacks2001dashsodium]
- Cutting sodium from high to low lowered SBP by 6.7 mmHg (-5.4 to -8.0) on the control diet, but only
  3.0 (-1.7 to -4.3) on DASH.
  [@sacks2001dashsodium]
- DASH with low sodium, against the control diet with high sodium, lowered SBP by 8.9 mmHg (-6.7 to
  -11.1) and DBP by 4.5 (-3.1 to -5.9).
  [@sacks2001dashsodium]

Adding the two single effects from the same starting point (-5.9 and -6.7) would predict about -12.6
mmHg; the trial measured -8.9. [inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium] The
investigators put it directly: «The reductions in blood pressure caused by the combination of dietary
interventions were smaller than they would have been if the effects of each dietary intervention were
strictly additive (P<0.001 for the interaction).»
[@sacks2001dashsodium]

**So estimate a combined DASH-and-salt effect from the -8.9 cell, never by adding two separate
numbers.** Sub-additivity is shown here only for DASH and salt, in one 30-day feeding trial; until other
pairs are tested, assume their drops may not add either. No held trial tests weight loss, DASH and lower
sodium together, so the drops in this section and the weight-loss figures should not be summed.
[inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]

This is efficacy under controlled feeding, on blood pressure only, over 30-day periods. The authors
note that «long-term health benefits remain to be demonstrated and will depend on the ability of people
to make long-lasting dietary changes, including the consistent choice of lower-sodium foods.»
[@sacks2001dashsodium]

### Sodium and potassium in one line each

- **Cutting sodium lowers resting SBP by 3.39 mmHg (2.46 to 4.31) at high certainty**, across 36 RCTs
  [@who2012], with a larger fall in
  people with hypertension [@he2013].
  Direct evidence on heart attacks and strokes from cohorts is very low certainty
  [@who2012].
- **More potassium lowered SBP by 5.32 mmHg (3.43 to 7.20) in 16 trials of people with
  hypertension**, against 0.09 (-0.77 to 0.95) in 3 shorter trials of people without it; pooled across
  all adults the fall was 3.49 mmHg (1.82 to 5.15), at high certainty.
  [@aburto2013]
- **Who should cut:** for a person with hypertension eating a lot of salt, both sides of the
  sodium-policy dispute agree to reduce it. For a person with normal pressure at moderate intake (3-6 g
  of sodium a day), the evidence is insufficient either way
  -> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]].

The figures, dose-response, and the kidney and drug contraindications live in the [[Sodium]] deliverable
([[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]], [[Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure]]).

### Other diet patterns: small drops, fading drops, or weak evidence

- **Mediterranean diet:** about -3/-2 mmHg against minimal intervention, at moderate certainty but
  from 2 RCTs and 269 people; against another healthy diet, an imprecise -1.5 mmHg at low certainty.
  [@rees2019medcochrane]
  Its event evidence comes from PREDIMED, not from pressure
  ([[Mediterranean Diet and Cardiovascular Events]]).
- **Named diet programmes:** low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets each lowered SBP by about 5 mmHg against
  usual diet at 6 months (5.14 moderate certainty, 5.05 low), above the review's 3 mmHg importance bar.
  Unlike the DASH-Sodium drops, these came with weight loss, of 4.63 and 4.37 kg. And
  «Macronutrient diet related improvements in both blood pressure and blood lipids disappeared
  almost completely at 12 months.»
  [@ge2020]
  Differences between named diets were trivial ([[Named Diet Programs Compared]]).
- **Vegan diets:** SBP -2.56 mmHg (-4.66 to -0.45) against omnivorous diets, from observational data at
  very low certainty.
  [@landry2024vegetarian]
  ([[Vegetarian Dietary Patterns and Mortality]])
- **Coffee:** no significant effect on blood pressure in trials; the pooled estimate was marginally
  favourable but did not reach significance.
  [@poole2017]
  A possible signal in slow caffeine metabolisers belongs to *Overall cardiovascular risk decides
  who gains most; starting pressure, age and kidney function come next*
  ([[Coffee Consumption and Health]]).
- **Time-restricted eating:** one controlled-feeding trial of an early eating window in 8 prediabetic
  men, weight held stable, reported SBP/DBP -11/-10 mmHg (p=.03)
  [@sutton2018], and its authors warn that
  morning-only readings may overstate it. A free-living trial of a late window found no between-group
  BP difference [@lowe2020]. These are different
  exposures,
  so neither result is a claim about eating windows in general ([[Time-Restricted Eating]]).

### Fibre, whole grains and sweeteners barely move the reading

**Extra fibre lowers systolic pressure by about 1 mmHg; whole grains and non-sugar sweeteners show no
clear effect.** These come from general-population trials. Reynolds admitted people with mild to
moderate hypertension but excluded those on medications affecting the outcomes, and gives no
hypertensive-only estimate
[@reynolds2019], so the figures are not
directly comparable with the hypertensive figures for exercise or garlic.

| Trial contrast | Systolic change (mmHg, 95% CI) | Certainty |
|---|---|---|
| More vs less dietary fibre, general population (15 trials) | -1.27 (-2.50 to -0.04) | Moderate |
| More vs less fibre, people with diabetes or prediabetes (12 trials) | -1.86 (-4.85 to 1.12), not significant | -- |
| More vs less whole grain (8 trials) | -1.01 (-2.46 to 0.44) | Moderate |
| Whole-grain vs refined-grain diets (SACN) | 0.2 (-1.6 to 2.0), p=0.85 | Moderate (SACN: no effect) |
| Non-sugar sweeteners (14 trials) | -1.33 (-2.71 to 0.06) | Moderate |

[@reynolds2019]
[@reynolds2020]
[@sacn2015]
[@who2023nonsugar]

SACN, the UK's advisory committee, put fibre and blood pressure in its table of insufficient trial
evidence, which it keeps separate from its table of inconsistent evidence
[@sacn2015]. SACN normally files evidence
as insufficient when fewer than three studies of the same type qualify, and sometimes when more exist but
are of poor quality [@sacn2015]. So the fibre entry
means too few or too weak qualifying trials, not conflicting ones. Its chapter summary states: «8.161 Randomised controlled trials
indicate there is no effect of total or mixed dietary fibre intake on the cardiovascular or type 2
diabetes mellitus risk factors considered here.»
[@sacn2015] With blood pressure filed as insufficient,
that null need not cover it.

Splitting fibre by type, SACN found one
effect: «8.79 An effect is demonstrated for higher oat bran and β-glucan consumption on reducing
systolic blood pressure (-2.86 mmHg, 95% CI -4.87, -0.85; p<0.01).»
[@sacn2015] The gap with Reynolds may come from that
split and different inclusion rules rather than from reading the same trials differently.

SACN's whole-grain null rests mostly on one trial, which contributed 92% of the systolic estimate,
raised whole-grain intake by less than a serving a day, and also produced weight differences between
groups [@sacn2015].

Sweeteners split by study design. In WHO's review, cohorts linked sweetener use to hypertension (HR
1.13, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.17, low certainty), while the trials showed no rise in systolic pressure
[@who2023nonsugar]. WHO names one explanation: «Reverse
causation suggests that those already at elevated risk of disease initiated or increased use of NSS
because of their risk status, rather than NSS leading to increased risk in otherwise healthy or
low-risk individuals.» [@who2023nonsugar] It then limits that
reading: «However, efforts taken by the authors to address reverse causation and confounding in most
studies suggest that these phenomena are not the sole causes of observed associations and may not even
play a significant role in many of the studies.»
[@who2023nonsugar]

In its body-weight discussion WHO notes that most sweetener trials lasted 3 months or less
[@who2023nonsugar]. If the blood-pressure trials were as short,
they could not detect a harm that takes years to show up as hypertension, so the short-term question
has a moderate-certainty null and the long-term one stays open. None of the three exposures moves
pressure enough to rank as a blood-pressure lever. At the drug-trial rate, fibre's -1.27 mmHg would
predict roughly 2.5% lower relative risk of cardiovascular events, and only if the drop is sustained and
diet-lowered pressure transmits like drug-lowered pressure. Their value, where they have any, runs through other outcomes
([[Dietary Fibre and Health]], [[Whole Grains Refined Grains and Pulses]], [[Non-Sugar Sweeteners]]).
[inferred from @reynolds2019; @bplttc2021]

## Supplements and relaxation practices report big drops that shrink under stricter tests

Beyond whole diets, several single foods, supplements and relaxation practices report systolic drops
as large as a drug's, on much weaker evidence. **Among the non-drug levers, the biggest headline
drops come from the least-tested levers.** Garlic, mindfulness
courses and yoga post some of the biggest office-BP numbers, yet none has been tested on strokes or
heart attacks. Each mind-body drop fails a stricter test somewhere: against an active control
(yoga), in low-risk-of-bias trials (slow breathing) or in unmedicated patients (mindfulness).
[inferred from @ried2008; @ma2025garlic; @geiger2025yoga; @chaddha2019]

The only non-drug lever with a trial that counted fewer events alongside a measured blood-pressure
drop, salt substitution, lowered systolic pressure by a modest 3.34 mmHg (95% CI -4.51 to -2.18) in SSaSS
[@neal2021], so the pattern rests on
that one contrast. (PREDIMED's Mediterranean diet cut events too, but its benefit is not tied to a
blood-pressure drop.) Drugs sit outside the pattern: they lowered systolic pressure by 8.80 mmHg (95% CrI
-9.58 to -8.02) against control across Naci's drug trials, which started from higher pressures (class
means of roughly 156-160 mmHg, against 125-135 in its exercise trials)
[@naci2019exercise], and their effect on events is
proven ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]).
[inferred from @neal2021; @bplttc2021]

So **rank these levers on certainty and event proof, not on the headline mmHg.** No trial of any lever
in this section counted cardiovascular events; the event payoff is assumed, as set out in *The proof
comes from drugs; for lifestyle it is mostly assumed* ([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).

### Dietary nitrate (beetroot juice)

Nitrate supplements, given as beetroot juice or nitrate salts, **lower systolic pressure by about
4.4 mmHg** (95% CI -5.9 to -2.8) against placebo, pooled from 16 small crossover trials (254 people).
Diastolic pressure did not move significantly (-1.1 mmHg, CI crossing zero). The trials lasted between
2 hours and 15 days. Most enrolled young, healthy participants, mostly men, and seven were
exercise-performance studies; removing those did not change the systolic result.
Beetroot juice and nitrate salt gave about the same drop, so the nitrate itself does the work.
[@siervo2013nitrate]

A larger, GRADE-rated dose-response analysis of 75 trials (1,823 people) puts the dose needed for at
least a 2 mmHg systolic fall at **about 8 mmol of nitrate a day**. It rated the acute and short-term
systolic effects moderate certainty at best, medium-term systolic effects low, and risk of bias serious
for every BP outcome. The slope looked steeper in people with hypertension, but that comes from a
subgroup analysis and stays a candidate finding.
[@norouzzadeh2025nitrate]
The second analysis includes the first one's trials, so it refines the estimate rather than confirming
it independently.
[inferred from @norouzzadeh2025nitrate; @siervo2013nitrate]

**The drop was not confirmed on 24-hour ambulatory monitoring** in the three trials that used it,
which included older, overweight and obese people with type 2 diabetes, the group a BP lever would most
need to help. [@siervo2013nitrate]
Ambulatory monitoring is the more reliable measure.

The proposed mechanism needs bacteria: nitrate «may be reduced by facultative anaerobic bacteria on the
dorsal surface of the tongue to nitrite», which is further reduced to nitric oxide
[@siervo2013nitrate]. If that route carries
the effect, daily antibacterial mouthwash or antibiotics would be expected to blunt it; no trial in the
pool tested that.

To link the drop to events, the authors borrow a Joint National Committee
estimate for a systolic fall «of at least 5 mm Hg (as observed here)»
[@siervo2013nitrate]. The pooled fall was
4.4 mmHg, below that threshold, and the source does not say what kind of pressure difference the Joint
National Committee estimate rests on.

Overall: a real short-term effect on resting pressure, at low-to-moderate certainty, with no event data
([[Dietary Nitrate and Blood Pressure]]).

### Garlic supplements

In people with hypertension, garlic supplements **lowered systolic pressure by about 8 mmHg** against
placebo: -8.38 mmHg (-11.13 to -5.62) across 4 trials of 12-23 weeks
[@ried2008],
and -8.12 mmHg (-10.95 to -5.28) in a 2025 update of 12 all-hypertensive trials
[@ma2025garlic].
**The two figures are not independent confirmations.** The later analysis re-pools the older trial
base, and a third of its trials come from the earlier review's first author.
[@ma2025garlic]

In people with normal pressure the drop was small and not significant (-2.28 mmHg, -4.61 to 0.05).
Almost all trials tested standardised dried garlic powder at 600-900 mg a day, not garlic eaten as food,
and within that narrow 600-900 mg range dose was not associated with the size of the effect.
[@ried2008]
The trials measured office BP only, and none recorded a cardiovascular event. Publication bias remains
unresolved: the 2025 paper's results and limitations sections contradict each other on it
([[Garlic and Blood Pressure]]).
[@ma2025garlic]

### Magnesium supplements

Magnesium supplements **lower systolic pressure by about 2 mmHg** against placebo. A pool of 34
double-blind trials (2,028 adults, median 368 mg a day for 3 months) found -2.00 mmHg (-3.58 to -0.43)
systolic and -1.78 mmHg diastolic.
[@zhang2016magnesiumbp]
A second analysis, restricted to people with insulin resistance, diabetes or heart disease, found
-2.22 mmHg between groups.
[@dibaba2017mg]
That second analysis shares 8 of its 11 trials with the first, so it is the same evidence sliced by
stratum, not a replication. Its larger, often-quoted figure is the before-and-after change in the
supplemented arm alone, not a placebo-controlled effect. Compared like with like, the two strata respond
about the same. That is a between-pool comparison.
[inferred from @zhang2016magnesiumbp; @dibaba2017mg]

Zhang also ran formal interaction tests within its own pool. It compared subgroups of its trials on the
same placebo-controlled quantity, a firmer check than comparing two pools. The effect did not differ
between people with normal and raised pressure (P for interaction 0.79 systolic, 0.59 diastolic). Point
estimates were larger with low baseline magnesium and in people on medication, but neither
interaction was significant (systolic P 0.21 and 0.11)
[@zhang2016magnesiumbp]. So a
person with hypertension should not expect a bigger drop. Correcting a deficit is the likeliest place
for a larger effect, and it is unproven.

In cohorts, each extra 100 mg a day of dietary magnesium tracked lower stroke (RR 0.93, 0.89-0.97) and
type 2 diabetes risk (RR 0.81, 0.77-0.86), but not total cardiovascular disease (RR 0.99, 0.88-1.10) or
coronary heart disease (RR 0.92, 0.85-1.01)
[@fang2016magnesium].
The authors «cannot exclude the possibility that other nutrients and/or dietary components correlated
with dietary magnesium may have been responsible, either partially or entirely, for the observed
associations»
[@fang2016magnesium],
so eating magnesium-rich foods is already covered by the DASH advice.
**Magnesium is a small refinement, not a main lever**, with no event data
([[Dietary Magnesium and Cardiometabolic Outcomes]]).

### Mindfulness courses, yoga and slow breathing

**Yoga and slow breathing lose their effect under a stricter test; mindfulness holds against active
controls but shows no drop in unmedicated patients** ([[Stress Management and Cardiometabolic Health]]).
[inferred from @chen2024mbi; @geiger2025yoga; @chaddha2019]

- **Mindfulness courses (MBSR / MBCT, 6-8 weeks):** across all 12 trials (715 people) systolic
  pressure fell -9.12 mmHg (-12.18 to -6.05), with extreme heterogeneity (I2 92%), mostly unblinded
  trials and moderate to high risk of bias. The drop held against active controls (-8.78, -15.98 to
  -1.57) but was not significant against a waitlist. It was largest in trials of medicated patients
  (-15.30, -17.85 to -12.74), and in the 2 trials of unmedicated patients there was none (+0.53, -1.89
  to 2.95). The authors suggest the course may work partly by improving drug adherence, so unmedicated
  patients might benefit less.
  [@chen2024mbi]
- **Yoga:** against an active control the systolic drop was -4.16 mmHg (-10.76 to 2.44), not
  significant, and on 24-hour ambulatory monitoring the systolic effect disappeared, though only 4 trials
  used it and the authors note this limits its power. Against a waitlist the headline is -7.95 mmHg
  (-10.24 to -5.66; 26 trials, 2,007 people), at very low GRADE certainty.
  [@geiger2025yoga]
- **Slow breathing (mostly device-guided):** in the low-risk-of-bias trials the systolic drop was
  -2.14 mmHg (-4.76 to 0.48), not significant. The headline across 17 trials (1,017 people) is
  -5.62 mmHg (-7.86 to -3.38), on office readings over 4-8 weeks, with high heterogeneity.
  [@chaddha2019]

**Treat these practices as an adjunct at most.** They cannot replace medication or salt reduction.


### Urate-lowering drugs

In people with raised uric acid, urate-lowering drugs (mostly allopurinol, 15 trials) lowered systolic
pressure by a standardized mean difference of 0.321 (0.145 to 0.497). That is a standardized effect,
not mmHg, and it shrank to 0.211 after correcting for publication bias. **The often-cited *about
3 mmHg* is borrowed** from an older analysis of 10 longitudinal studies, so it should be discounted.
[@qu2017urate]

The trials also cannot say whether lower urate does the work. Allopurinol blocks an enzyme that may
affect blood vessels directly [@qu2017urate]; a
trial of a drug that lowers urate by another route would settle it
([[Uric Acid Lowering and Blood Pressure]]).
It is a drug, so whether to use it is a prescriber's decision, outside this appraisal.

The dietary link sits upstream. With high certainty, sugar-sweetened drinks raise uric acid even at
equal calories [@ayoubcharette2021fructose]
([[Free Sugars Intake]]). But no study has followed the whole chain from cutting sugary drinks to lower
urate, lower pressure and fewer events in people with normal uric acid.

Put every lever, and the drugs, on the same three axes: size of drop, certainty, and proof of fewer
events. The ranking then changes with the person.

## Ranked side by side, drugs and the big lifestyle levers overlap; supplements trail

Lined up on the same three axes, the levers sort into rough tiers rather than a strict order. **A
blood-pressure drug, structured exercise, cutting heavy drinking, a weight-loss drug, and DASH eating
with less salt each lower systolic pressure by a few to about 9 mmHg in the people studied.** They
differ in how sure we are of the drop and in whether anyone has counted the events it prevents.
**Only blood-pressure drugs have measured fewer events at every starting pressure tested.** Among the
other levers, only a salt substitute and, in people who already had heart disease, semaglutide have
counted events alongside a measured BP drop; PREDIMED's Mediterranean-diet events are not tied to
pressure. Supplements and relaxation practices report drops of the same headline size on far weaker
evidence. None has been tested on events.

### A drug can stand in for the blood-pressure part of a lever, not the whole lever

When a safe, effective drug can deliver the same blood-pressure drop, a lifestyle lever's extra value
*for blood pressure* shrinks. Its value on other outcomes does not. **Exercise and weight loss work on
more than one channel.** Exercise also improves fitness, glucose control and weight, which a BP-only
comparison ignores -> [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]. Diet and weight change also act on what
drives the pressure up in the first place, while a drug manages the reading. The comparison also has to
subtract the drug's own costs: tolerability, lifelong use, and leaving the underlying driver in place
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

The trialists behind the per-mmHg evidence list the same costs. Treating raised pressure, they write,
requires weighing «an individual's overall risk of future cardiovascular events, potential risk of
adverse effects, the cost of treatment, and patient preferences»
[@bplttc2021].

**The weight-loss drug carries its own costs.** In SELECT, adverse events led 16.6% of patients on
semaglutide to stop it permanently, against 8.2% on placebo, including gastrointestinal disorders in
10.0% against 2.0% of patients
[@lincoff2023]. And the
blood-pressure gain is a state you maintain, not one you bank. When people in STEP-1 stopped the drug
and the lifestyle programme, blood pressure returned to baseline within a year (figures under *Weight
loss*). The smaller weight loss in SELECT than in STEP-1 reflects older, sicker participants and a
different time point, not a weaker drug
-> [[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]].

### Drugs and lifestyle stack, and the choice between them is yours

**Lifestyle changes still lower pressure in people already taking blood-pressure drugs.** In 7 DASH
trials of treated hypertensives, systolic pressure fell -2.1 mmHg (-2.5 to -1.8), against -5.9 (-9.9 to
-1.8) in 8 trials of untreated hypertensives; the difference was not significant (P=0.07)
[@filippou2020dash]. The point
estimates suggest the add-on is smaller than the effect in untreated people, but the trials do not show
it. The overlap of trial lists across Filippou's low-effect subgroups makes the cause unclear
-> [[DASH Diet and Blood Pressure]].

For a person with hypertension, structured exercise is a credible companion to a drug, and a candidate
alternative where a drug is declined or not yet indicated
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]. **Which route to take, or how to
combine them, is a decision for the person and their prescriber.** The figures here size the options;
they do not choose among them. Picking an agent or a dose is a prescriber's job and outside this
appraisal.

### A population's top killer is not automatically your top lever

High systolic pressure was the leading risk factor for deaths worldwide in 2019, «accounting for 10·8
million (9·51–12·1) deaths» (19.2% of all deaths that year), ahead of tobacco
[@gbd2020rf]. That ranking counts deaths attributed
across a whole population, and it rises with how common the exposure is. **It does not tell any one
person how much they would gain by lowering their own pressure.** That depends on their own overall risk
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

Who gains most depends first on overall risk, and only then on how high the pressure starts.

## Overall cardiovascular risk decides who gains most; starting pressure, age and kidney function come next

Three different reasons can make a recommendation differ between people, and they need different evidence.
**Under route (a), baseline risk, the benefit per mmHg is the same for everyone, and the absolute gain
grows with each person's risk.** That needs only a risk estimate, with no claim that the treatment works
differently in anyone [@grade]. **Route (b), effect modification, says a
lever genuinely works better in some people.** That needs positive interaction evidence; a plausible
mechanism is not enough, and a contrast between separate sets of trials is not an interaction test.
Route (c), contraindication, covers a harm that fires only in one group
-> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]].

### Overall cardiovascular risk (route a)

**Because the per-mmHg event reduction does not change with starting pressure, 10-year
cardiovascular risk is the main stratifier.** It needs no subgroup claim; the per-1,000 arithmetic is
in *A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by about a tenth, at every starting pressure tested*.
The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) turns a SCORE2 10-year risk into an action with thresholds
that rise with age:

| 10-year SCORE2 risk | under 50 | 50-69 | 70 and over |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-to-moderate: treatment of risk factors generally not recommended | under 2.5% | under 5% | under 7.5% |
| High: treatment should be considered | 2.5 to under 7.5% | 5 to under 10% | 7.5 to under 15% |
| Very high: treatment generally recommended | 7.5% or more | 10% or more | 15% or more |

[@esc2021]

So the same 8% means *very high* at 45 and *high* at 72. ESC-EAS's 2025 lipid guidance uses
different, non-age-banded cutoffs (very high at 20% or more, high at 10 to under 20%, moderate at 2 to
under 10%). Under those, 8% is *moderate*: a risk category is specific to the guideline that assigns
it [@esceas2025]. SCORE2 and its older-age version
cover people aged 40 to 89 without known cardiovascular disease, and not people already on
lipid-lowering drugs [@esceas2025]. The base model
also excludes diabetes; SCORE2-Diabetes fills that gap [@score2diabetes2023].

**A person's category flips on inputs that age and sex alone do not supply.** Here is ESC's chart
for men aged 60-64 in its moderate-risk region (which includes Germany, Italy and Sweden), with non-HDL
cholesterol of 4.0-5.9 mmol/L:

| Assumed inputs | 10-year risk read off the chart | ESC category (age 50-69) |
|---|---|---|
| non-smoker, SBP 140-159 | 10-11% | very high: treatment generally recommended |
| non-smoker, SBP 120-139 | 8-9% | high: treatment should be considered |
| smoker, SBP 140-159 | 15-17% | very high |

[@esc2021]

Smoking, the blood-pressure band, cholesterol and region each move the answer, so no single row speaks
for a stratum. ESC attaches two conditions. The charts apply only where risk factors are
untreated or have been stable for several years, and risk should be adjusted upward as a person nears
the next age band [@esc2021]
-> [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]].

### Normal versus raised pressure: two different questions

**The relative benefit of each mmHg holds at normal pressure** (see *A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by
about a tenth, at every starting pressure tested*).

**The number of mmHg a lever delivers often looks smaller at normal pressure, but the evidence for that
comes from comparing separate sets of trials.** In Naci's network analysis, exercise trials started from
lower pressures than the drug trials. Restricting them to hypertensive participants nearly doubled
the estimated drop (figures under *Exercise*)
[@naci2019exercise]. A higher start may leave room
for a larger fall, so two levers can only be compared at matched baselines
[inferred from @naci2019exercise]

Garlic's drop is not significant in normotensives (*Garlic supplements*;
[[Garlic and Blood Pressure]]). Sodium reduction lowers pressure more in hypertensives. Potassium
lowered it in 16 hypertensive trials but not in 3 short
normotensive trials (figures under *Sodium and potassium in one line each*). Each of these is a
between-trial subgroup comparison with no interaction test, so each is a route (b) candidate, not a
finding; details in [[Sodium]]. Magnesium, like DASH below, has a formal
within-pool interaction test by blood-pressure status, and it found no significant interaction (all
P > 0.05) [@zhang2016magnesiumbp] (*Magnesium
supplements*).

**DASH is the exception.** Filippou pooled 30 trials and found the same SBP fall in hypertensive and
normotensive trials, -3.9 mmHg in each (P=0.96), and no difference between baselines of 140 and above
versus below (P=0.70)
[@filippou2020dash]. Siervo's
earlier pooling found a larger fall at higher baseline pressure
[@siervo2015].
But that finding came from a between-trial meta-regression, a design prone to ecological confounding and, on
change-from-baseline metrics, to regression to the mean
[inferred from @siervo2015].

DASH-Sodium's larger combined fall in its hypertensive participants is a subgroup comparison, not an
interaction test [inferred from @sacks2001dashsodium]. **The safe default is route
(a): do not promise someone a bigger DASH drop because their pressure is higher**
[inferred from @filippou2020dash; @siervo2015]
-> [[DASH Diet and Blood Pressure]].

One argument for restricting sodium advice to hypertensives rests on a premise from Mente: that
lowering pressure helps only above 140 mmHg systolic. Mente draws it from BP-lowering meta-analyses in people
with diabetes, HOPE-3 and secondary-prevention trials
[@mente2016pure]. BPLTTC, a
later and larger analysis, contradicts that premise. It does not settle the separate dispute over very
low sodium intake
[inferred from @mente2016pure; @bplttc2021]
-> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]].

### Age

**For younger adults, a low 10-year risk understates the case.** ESC notes that women under 50 and men
under 40 are almost invariably at low 10-year risk, even when their risk factors sharply raise
longer-term risk. It offers a lifetime-benefit frame, including a grid for a 10 mmHg SBP reduction, as a
route into treatment from the lowest band
[@esc2021].

ESC bounds that frame in the same passage: «CVD risk predictions, as well as predictions of lifetime
benefit of risk factor treatment, are likely to be imprecise at very young age (<40 years). At that
age, lipid-lowering and BP-lowering drug treatment are not usually considered, except for patients
with FH or specific BP disorders. A healthy lifestyle that is maintained throughout life is more
relevant for the very young.» [@esc2021]
-> [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]].

**At older ages the logic runs the other way.** ESC sets higher risk thresholds from 70 because
risk-factor gradients weaken with age and competing non-cardiovascular mortality rises
[@esc2021]. Lower pressure still pays at
older ages, including for dementia (benefit under *A second outcome: dementia*; no U-shape at any age under
*Targeting systolic below 120 prevented more events in high-risk trial patients, with more side
effects*).

preDIVA is the trial in unselected 70-78-year-olds described under *At low risk, a proven effect still
buys little*. Its authors read it as easing the worry that treating blood pressure in old age harms
cognition: «Our results mitigate fears that antihypertensive treatment in older age groups evokes
cognitive decline. Moreover, they show that such an intervention is safe, which is in accordance
with findings from the recent SPRINT trial.»
[@mollvancharante2016] Because the
trial moved systolic pressure by only about 2 mmHg, it tests that worry only weakly.

Its overall dementia result was null (HR 0.92, 0.71-1.19). But in a subgroup of adherent participants
with untreated hypertension at baseline, dementia was lower (HR 0.54, 0.32-0.92)
[@mollvancharante2016]. That is a
hypothesis that the benefit lies where hypertension goes untreated, not a finding: adherence is
measured after randomisation, so the subgroup is not a randomised comparison.

**DASH's apparent age effect is a hypothesis.** Trials with a mean age under 50 showed a larger SBP fall
(-4.9 vs -2.0 mmHg, P<0.001). Filippou calls this a hypothesis, warns that meta-regressions are
cross-sectional tools, and notes it is unknown whether individual patients sat above or below the age
cut-off [@filippou2020dash]. Trial
age is tangled up with sodium level and with one large trial that sits in every low-effect subgroup.
So this does not license telling an older person that DASH works less for them
[inferred from @filippou2020dash].

### Kidney disease

**Chronic kidney disease raises baseline risk (route a), so the same mmHg buys more.** ESC's 2025
lipid guidance places moderate CKD in its high-risk and severe CKD in its very-high-risk category,
whatever the score [@esceas2025]. ESC's
2021 prevention guideline grades CKD the same way
[@esc2021].
Preventing kidney disease itself is covered in [[Chronic Kidney Disease and Modifiable Exposures]].

> [!warning] Kidney disease or potassium-sparing drugs
> Potassium supplements and potassium-enriched salt substitutes are a route (c) exception. People who
> cannot excrete potassium normally risk hyperkalaemia, and they were left out of the potassium review
> [@aburto2013]. The large
> salt-substitute trial found no excess hyperkalaemia, but it screened out known kidney disease and
> potassium-sparing or potassium-supplement use, so it confirms the caution rather than lifting it
> [@neal2021]. Details: [[Sodium]].

### Genotype

Poole's umbrella review cites one report, by Palatini and colleagues, in which the hypertension risk
linked to coffee varied by CYP1A2 genotype: «Those with alleles for slow caffeine metabolism were at
increased risk of hypertension compared with those with alleles for fast
caffeine metabolism.» [@poole2017] **This is a
route (b) candidate, not yet a basis for advice** -> [[Coffee Consumption and Health]].

### What guidance does with strata

WHO's 2012 sodium guideline declined to stratify, citing both the high prevalence of hypertension and
a smaller but still significant effect in normotensives (-1.38 mmHg, 95% CI -2.74 to -0.02) — partly a
public-health reason, partly an evidential one
[@who2012]
-> [[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]].

For those already on treatment, the remaining question is how far down to go.

## Targeting systolic below 120 prevented more events in high-risk trial patients, with more side effects

**One large trial tested a lower target head-on. The randomised evidence shows no excess
cardiovascular events or deaths at the lower pressures studied, though adverse effects rose.** A
prescriber chooses a target for a particular person, with their history and medicines in hand. What
follows is what the trials found.

### SPRINT: a target below 120 cut events and deaths in high-risk patients

SPRINT randomised 9,361 people aged 50 or older with a systolic pressure of 130-180 mmHg, high
cardiovascular risk, no diabetes and no prior stroke to a systolic target below 120 mmHg or below
140 mmHg. Achieved pressures were about 121 and 136, and the trial stopped
early, at a median of 3.26 years [@sprint2015].

- **The lower target prevented more major events:** the composite of heart attack, acute coronary
  syndrome, stroke, heart failure and cardiovascular death fell with HR 0.75 (0.64-0.89)
  [@sprint2015].
- **It also lowered all-cause mortality:** HR 0.73 (0.60-0.90)
  [@sprint2015].
- **In absolute terms,** 61 people had to be treated to the lower target for 3.26 years to prevent one
  primary-outcome event [@sprint2015].

**The extra benefit came with extra harm.** Serious hypotension, fainting (syncope), electrolyte
abnormalities and acute kidney injury or failure were all more common on the lower target; injurious
falls were not [@sprint2015]. So the target
is a net-effect judgement, not a rule that lower is always better.

**SPRINT's result is what the per-mmHg drug-trial rate predicts.** A gap of about 15 mmHg, at the rate
from the pooled trials described in *A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by about a tenth, at every
starting pressure tested*, predicts a composite HR near 0.75, which is what SPRINT
observed
[inferred from @sprint2015; @bplttc2021].
Whether this is an independent check is unclear. SPRINT's investigators appear among BPLTTC's
collaborators, but the pooled trials were published between 1972 and 2013 and SPRINT in 2015, so
whether SPRINT is inside the pool cannot be read from the held text
-> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]].

**The result holds for the people SPRINT enrolled.** They were high-risk, without diabetes, followed for
about three years, and reached an average of about 121 mmHg; more than half of the intensive group
stayed above 120. The authors themselves name the groups it does not cover: people with diabetes, prior
stroke, or under 50, and it did not enrol nursing-home residents
[@sprint2015]. It does not show what happens
when pressure is held well below 120.

### The low-pressure J-curve does not survive randomisation

Observational data once suggested that pushing pressure too low is harmful. A registry of people with
ischaemic heart disease found the lowest event risk at around 130/75 mmHg, which was read as a reason not
to lower pressure further
[@bplttc2021].
BPLTTC notes that one randomised trial in people without cardiovascular disease and a systolic pressure
below 140 came to a similar conclusion, and its pooled drug trials found no fading of benefit down to
a starting systolic below 120 (see *A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by about a tenth, at every starting
pressure tested*)
[@bplttc2021].

The authors conclude that their findings «do not substantiate concerns about a J-shaped association
between blood pressure and cardiovascular outcomes in observational studies»
[@bplttc2021].

Note the scope: these strata are *starting* pressures. People who began below 120 still benefited from
a further drop; the strata do not by themselves show the effect of holding pressure at any given level
.

**Dementia shows the same pattern.** Observational cohorts in older people show a U-shaped link between
blood pressure and dementia. An individual-patient analysis of five placebo-controlled trials found
«no evidence of a U-shaped relation of the effect at any age, nor any increase in risk of dementia
with treatment in the oldest age» [@peters2022bp].
Plotting achieved pressure across both arms, it found «a linear relationship between lower risk of
dementia and lower BP, down to at least 100 mmHg systolic and 70 mmHg diastolic»
[@peters2022bp].

**That 100/70 is where the data run out, not an optimum**: the relationship is monotone over the
studied range, and the trials say nothing about pressures below it. The treatment effect on
dementia itself is given in *A 5 mmHg drop cuts major events by about a tenth, at every starting
pressure tested*
-> [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]].

**Run through the U-curve diagnostics, the low arm fails as a treatment-effect concern.** A
harmful-looking low arm in observational data is suspect when sick people's pressure falls for reasons
of their own: reverse causation, frailty, and the falling pressure of the dementia prodrome. Covariate
adjustment and dropping early follow-up are weak checks; a randomised or genetic test is the strong
one. Randomised evidence removes the low arm as a treatment-effect concern down to a starting systolic
below 120 for cardiovascular events, and shows no U in the treatment effect on dementia. **The
achieved-pressure J-curve is most plausibly an artifact, but no randomised test of holding pressure well
below about 120 is held**, and none of the named mechanisms was shown to operate in these data
 -> [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]].
The same diagnostic removed alcohol's protective arm on mortality ->
[[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]].

**This refutation does not carry over to salt.** The J-curve for low *sodium intake*, seen in the PURE
cohort, is a different exposure. Its low arm has so far faced only the weak checks and remains
unadjudicated [inferred from @mente2016pure]
-> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]], [[Sodium]].

## Know your overall risk, pull the big levers, and treat the extras as extras

The decision comes down to a few steps. The numbers behind each are in the sections above; this list
adds none.

- **Start from your overall cardiovascular risk, not the reading alone.** The same drop buys a
  large absolute gain at high risk and a small one at low risk
  ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]],
  [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]]).
- **If you drink three or more drinks a day, cutting back is a well-measured way to lower your
  pressure.** The drop grows from about 1 mmHg at 3 drinks a day to about 5.5 at 6 or more. At two
  or fewer, cutting back made no significant difference to the reading
  ([[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]]).
- **Regular exercise, cutting heavy drinking, weight loss (best measured with drugs), and
  DASH-style eating with less salt are the large lifestyle levers.** They may overlap: DASH plus
  lower sodium lowered pressure by less than the sum of each, so do not add those two drops
  ([[DASH Diet and Blood Pressure]], [[Sodium]]).
- **At high risk, drug treatment is the route with measured event reductions; lifestyle still
  lowers the reading on top of drugs, by less in the DASH trials.** Whether to use a lifestyle
  change, a drug, or both is your decision; discuss the drug side with a clinician
  ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]],
  [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]).
- **Treat garlic, magnesium, beetroot nitrate and relaxation practices as unproven extras.** Their
  reported drops are either small or rest on weak trials, and none of these trials counted events
.
- **At low risk with normal pressure, the remaining gain is small.** That is a reason to stop
  optimizing, not a sign that blood pressure does not matter
  ([[Cardiometabolic Interventions and Hard CV Outcomes in Low-Risk People]]).

One question stays open across all of this. **The fewer-events rate comes from drug-lowered pressure,
and for lifestyle levers it is assumed, not counted.** If the drug-trial rate applies, a lifestyle drop
should pay off the same way, but outside salt substitution no trial has shown it
([[Surrogate Outcomes]]).

## Several questions that shape the decision are still open

The evidence here cannot yet answer several things. None points in a known direction; each could move
a choice, and a specific study would settle it.

### How big each lever is, and how levers combine

- **No trial has put exercise head to head against a blood-pressure drug.** Naci states it plainly:
  «No RCTs compared directly exercise against medications.»
  [@naci2019exercise] Every exercise-versus-drug
  comparison is therefore indirect. A randomized comparison in people with hypertension would settle
  it. -> [[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]
- **No source held here says how many mmHg a kilogram of weight loss buys.** The figures held come
  from drug trials and marker bundles, not a dose-response. A systematic review of weight change
  against blood-pressure change would supply it. ->
  [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]
- **Nobody knows what weight loss, DASH-style eating and lower salt do together.** The only
  measured combination is DASH with lower sodium, which came out smaller than the sum of its parts.
  A factorial trial adding weight loss would show how much of each drop is shared. ->
  [[DASH Diet and Blood Pressure]]
- **Isometric training (handgrip, wall-sits) appears only as one arm inside Naci's exercise-drug
  network.** No dedicated review of it is held, so its size cannot be ranked with confidence.

- **DASH's effect has not been followed for long.** Filippou's trials averaged about 15 weeks, too
  short to say the drop lasts (see *DASH lowers systolic pressure by about 3 to 5 mmHg, depending on
  how you pool the trials*)
  [@filippou2020dash]. The
  DASH-Sodium comparisons ran 30-day controlled feeding periods. Trials lasting a year or more, on
  self-chosen food, would show whether the drop holds.

### Whether a lower reading means fewer events

- **Only salt substitution has counted events after a lifestyle-lowered blood pressure.** For every
  other lever, the event payoff assumes the drug-trial rate applies. Event trials of the large
  lifestyle levers would test that assumption. -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]]
- **The Look AHEAD report held gives no between-arm difference in achieved blood pressure**, so
  nobody can check whether its null on events was predictable from the blood-pressure channel. ->
  [[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]
- **No cardiovascular-event result for tirzepatide is held.** The held trial reports point to
  SURMOUNT-MMO, its outcome trial, which was ongoing when they were published; SELECT's benefit
  belongs to a different drug in a different population. ->
  [[Semaglutide for Cardiovascular Risk in Obesity]]
- **Uric-acid-lowering drugs may lower blood pressure for a reason other than lower urate.** Qu:
  «It also remains unclear if a decrease in BP does occur, if it can be attributed to allopurinol
  itself or a decrease in UA level.»
  [@qu2017urate] A trial of a uricosuric drug
  would separate the two. -> [[Uric Acid Lowering and Blood Pressure]]

### Who benefits, and how low to go

- **No measure of salt sensitivity is held**, so the evidence here cannot pick out the people for
  whom cutting salt matters most. -> [[Should Sodium Reduction Be Population-Wide or Targeted]]

- **No coffee source held here estimates caffeine's own effect on blood pressure.** It offers only a
  signal that coffee's link to hypertension differs by CYP1A2 genotype. ->
  [[Coffee Consumption and Health]]
- **The evidence for intensive targets comes from high-risk people without diabetes.** How low to
  go with diabetes, or when older and frail, is not settled by what is held.
- **The reasoning behind shifting hypertension thresholds is not held.** Why guideline bodies drew
  the line where they did, and which objectives moved it, cannot be read from the sources here. ->
  [[Which Objective Moved This Recommendation]]
