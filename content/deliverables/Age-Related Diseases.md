**The diseases of ageing share a modifiable core, though not every lever reaches every disease.**
Body fatness, tobacco, inactivity and heavy alcohol raise the risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer
and dementia alike; blood pressure, blood glucose and blood lipids drive cardiovascular disease and
dementia but are not established cancer levers. Where a lever overlaps, pulling it buys protection
against more than one disease at once -- but those gains sit on different outcome scales and cannot be
added into a single number.

**Physical activity is the one lever that reaches almost every disease on the list -- the widest,
though rarely the largest for any single one.** It reaches cardiovascular disease, several cancers,
dementia, Parkinson's, frailty, falls, and even one age-related eye disease, cataract. But widest is
not universal: for macular degeneration the activity evidence is insufficient -- an unproven
eye-specific bet, not a lever.

**The disease-specific add-ons that dominate public discussion move little, move uncertainly, or do
harm.** Eggs, red meat, single supplements and single *superfoods* are the items in question — and one
supplement among them does measurable harm.

**For some diseases the honest finding is a low ceiling.** Parkinson's has few credible levers and
mostly weak evidence, and several prevention bundles move a marker without yet preventing the disease
they target. Reporting that little is left to optimise is itself a result.

**The ranking shifts with age, not the levers.** In midlife the cardiometabolic levers carry the risk
for later disease; in later life falls, frailty and muscle loss become the outcomes that decide
independence. Absolute benefit rises with age because baseline risk does.

## The diseases of ageing share a common core of levers

Dementia, Parkinson's disease, the major cancers, and cardiovascular disease look like four
separate problems, and the clinics that treat them are separate. But when you line up the
*modifiable* exposures that raise or lower the risk of each, the same short list keeps reappearing,
and mostly in the same direction. A handful of levers — physical activity, body fatness, glucose and
blood pressure, blood lipids, smoking, alcohol — do most of the modifiable work across all four.

### The same exposures recur, disease after disease

Read the matched-exposure table **down a column**, one disease at a time. Each cell is a separate
relative risk for a separate outcome, drawn from a different literature.

| Exposure | Dementia | Parkinson's | Cancer | CVD |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Physical activity** | protective, RR 0.80 (0.77-0.84) | protective, RR 0.79 (0.68-0.91) | protective (colon convincing; breast, endometrium probable) | established lever |
| **Body fatness (midlife)** | harm, RR 1.31 (1.02-1.68) | not established | convincing/probable cause at 12 of 17 sites | established lever |
| **Diabetes / high glucose** | harm, HR 1.24 per 5-yr-earlier onset | harm, RR 1.31 (1.10-1.57) | via adiposity | established lever |
| **High blood pressure** | harm (untreated), HR 1.42 (1.15-1.76) | not established | — | established lever |
| **LDL cholesterol** | harm, HR 1.33 (1.26-1.41) above 3 mmol/L | not established | — | causal lever |
| **Smoking** | harm, midlife RR \~1.3 | *protective* RR 0.64 (artifact-suspect) | convincing cause, many sites | established lever |
| **Alcohol** | harm above \~21 units/wk | — | a cause, no safe threshold | established lever |

[@livingston2024] [@chen2021pd] [@wcrf2018ter]

That repetition is the whole point: **there is no separate anti-dementia, anti-cancer, and anti-heart
diet to assemble.** Most of what a person could modify to lower the risk of one of these diseases is
the same thing that lowers the risk of the others. The pattern is not perfect — smoking, protective
for Parkinson's incidence in the crude data, is the standout exception, and that protective arm is
almost certainly a reverse-causation and selective-survival artifact rather than a reason to smoke.
The exceptions are instructive, but they do not dislodge the shared core.

### Co-membership is not the same as a shared effect size

Two cautions keep the shared core honest. First, **read down a column, never across a row.** That
physical activity appears in four columns says it *touches* four diseases; it does not say the effect
is the same size in each — each RR is measured against a different outcome on a different scale, so
the numbers in a row are not commensurable and must never be summed or averaged. Co-membership is a
map of *where* a lever reaches, not a magnitude.

Second, the population arithmetic behaves the same way. Livingston's 2024 *Lancet* Commission
estimates that the 14 leading modifiable factors together account for a large share of dementia — «an
estimate of nearly half of all cases of dementia being associated with 14 potentially modifiable risk
factors». [@livingston2024] That 45% is a **modelled,
population-attributable fraction**, not a personal risk reduction: it assumes the observed
associations are fully causal, it is discounted for *communality* because the factors overlap and
cluster, and for that reason **the individual factor fractions do not add up** to it. It describes
what share of disease in a population is statistically tied to the whole cluster — not what any one
person removes by pulling one lever. [@livingston2024]

### Why the shared core changes the decision

If the same handful of exposures drives several diseases of ageing at once, then the ranking rule
that governs the whole document — rank a lever by expected effect size times certainty, and prefer
levers with structural reach — is *strengthened*, not complicated. A lever that moves three or four
patient-important outcomes at once outranks one that moves a single outcome at an equal per-outcome
effect. Pulling the shared cardiometabolic-and-activity core once buys down risk across the cluster,
which is why the rest of this document is organized around the levers rather than around the
diseases. [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]

Ranked by how many of these diseases it touches, physical activity is first.

## Physical activity is the widest lever across the diseases of ageing

No other modifiable exposure reaches as many of the diseases of ageing as physical activity. It is
protective for dementia (RR 0.80, 0.77-0.84), for Parkinson's disease (RR 0.79, 0.68-0.91), and for
several cancers (colon convincing; breast and endometrium probable on WCRF grading); it lowers the
risk of falls and frailty, and it is an established lever against cardiovascular disease.
[@livingston2024] [@chen2021pd] [@wcrf2018ter]
Activity earns its place here on **breadth** — the sheer count of outcomes it touches — not on having
the largest effect on any single disease.

### The reach extends to one eye disease, but stops at another

A 2026 meta-analysis of prospective cohorts extends activity's reach to age-related eye disease, and
in doing so marks its boundary. For **cataract**, the review grades physical activity a *probable*
cause of reduced risk on WCRF criteria: RR 0.90 (0.86-0.94), a roughly 10% reduction.
[@aune2026vision] For
**age-related macular degeneration**, the *same paper* reaches no such conclusion: RR 0.92
(0.84-1.01), an interval that crosses 1.00, which the authors grade «limited, and no conclusion could
be drawn». [@aune2026vision]

That last result is **insufficient evidence, not a demonstrated null** — a wide interval resting on
few cohorts cannot distinguish a small real effect from none, so it must not be read as "activity
does not work for AMD," and the AMD arm must not borrow the cataract arm's significance. The
eye-specific detail belongs later; for the ranking, even a lever this wide has an honest edge, and
stating the reach means naming that edge.

### Two limits keep the breadth claim honest

**The mortality evidence is softer than the function evidence.** Activity's effect on all-cause
mortality comes from observational cohorts ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]), where
healthy-user confounding cannot be fully removed; its effect on *falls*, by contrast, is established
by randomized trials — pooled fall-rate reduction RaR 0.77 (0.71-0.83), high certainty
[@sherrington2019] — as is its effect on frailty and physical
function [@racey2021frailtypa]. So the
widest lever is not uniformly the best-evidenced lever; its firmest evidence is for keeping people
functional, not for extending life.

**The effective type is outcome-specific.** "Move more" is underspecified, because the kind of
activity that works depends on the target: balance and strength training prevent falls, resistance
training preserves muscle and function, and aerobic or mixed activity carries most of the
cardiometabolic benefit. Breadth is a property of activity in general; delivering it requires
matching the *type* to the outcome a given person is trying to move.

So treat physical activity as the default lever across the diseases of ageing — the one to reach for
first — and match its form to the outcome in view. If physical
activity is the widest lever, the cardiometabolic cluster is the heaviest.

## The cardiometabolic cluster is the heaviest shared lever, and CVD adds no increment beyond it

The exposures that do the most damage across the diseases of ageing are the cardiometabolic ones,
and they do it in midlife. Blood pressure, glucose, blood lipids, and body fatness raise the risk of
dementia — untreated hypertension HR 1.42 (1.15-1.76), diabetes HR 1.24 per 5-years-earlier onset,
LDL cholesterol above 3 mmol/L HR 1.33 (1.26-1.41) with each 1 mmol/L increment carrying an 8% rise
(RR 1.08, 1.03-1.14), and midlife obesity RR 1.31 (1.02-1.68).
[@livingston2024] These are, item for item, the classic
cardiovascular risk factors ([[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]).

### The CVD column is an informative null, not an empty cell

Because the cardiometabolic exposures that drive dementia *are* the cardiovascular risk factors,
cardiovascular disease has no separate, disease-specific list of its own to add. Its answer to "what
modifiable exposures raise the risk?" is the shared cardiometabolic list already in hand. That is an
**informative null**: not a gap in the table, but the finding that there is no distinct CVD lever
left to hunt for once the shared cluster is named. The same action — lowering midlife blood pressure
and glucose — buys down two diseases at once.

### The lipid lever comes with a surrogate caution

LDL cholesterol (and apoB) is a genuinely causal target for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease —
the causal transmission from marker to arterial disease is evidenced, not assumed. But its role as a
proxy is **route-specific and outcome-specific**: it is a strong causal target for ASCVD events and a
weaker proxy for all-cause mortality, and the validated way to move it downstream to hard outcomes is
lipid-lowering drug therapy, not every intervention that happens to lower the number.
[[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]] [[Surrogate Outcomes]] So the lever is real, but it should be
pulled for the outcome its evidence actually covers, and a change in the marker is not by itself a
promise on mortality or cognition.

### Timing is part of the lever

The cardiometabolic exposures do their damage over decades, which makes *when* they are addressed
part of the effect. The Livingston Commission puts the timing rule plainly: «Prevention approaches
should aim to decrease risk factor levels early (the earlier, the better) and keep them low
throughout life (the longer, the better)», adding that it is «never too early or too late».
[@livingston2024] The decision-change is concrete: because
the cluster is shared and acts early, lowering midlife blood pressure and glucose is a single action
that buys down two diseases, and there is no separate cardiovascular lever to chase beyond it.

Body fatness is the shared lever that bites hardest on cancer.

## Body fatness raises the risk of a dozen cancers, and alcohol carries no safe dose

Cancer is where the cardiometabolic cluster's leading member — body fatness — does its heaviest
work, and where a second shared exposure, alcohol, behaves unlike any other lever on this list: it
has no threshold below which it is safe.

### Body fatness is the broadest dietary cancer lever

The World Cancer Research Fund grades greater body fatness a **convincing or probable cause of many
cancers** and puts it first among its recommendations, because the evidence is «particularly strong
... and has grown stronger over the last decade».
[@wcrf2018ter] The footprint is what makes
it the widest diet-adjacent cancer exposure: «12 of the 17 cancers reviewed by the CUP are linked to
greater body fatness» [@wcrf2018ter] —
oesophagus (adenocarcinoma), pancreas, liver, colorectum, kidney, endometrium, postmenopausal
breast, gallbladder, ovary, stomach cardia, advanced prostate, and mouth/pharynx/larynx.

IARC, reviewing independently, adds three more sites: thyroid, multiple myeloma and meningioma.
[@wcrf2018ter] WCRF does not attach a
per-person risk ratio to this — the grade is a strength-of-evidence judgment across sites, not a
single effect size — so the lever's claim rests on breadth and consistency rather than one number.

**The risk is graded, not a switch that flips at BMI 25.** «For some cancers the increase in risk is
seen with increasing body fatness even within the so-called ‘healthy’ range. Nevertheless, most
benefit is to be gained by avoiding overweight and obesity.»
[@wcrf2018ter] So the exposure rises with
fatness across the whole distribution, with the decision-relevant mass of benefit in staying out of
overweight and obesity — and body fatness is marked by BMI, read alongside waist circumference or
waist-to-hip ratio [@wcrf2018ter], which
track the visceral fat the mechanisms below implicate.

Those mechanisms inform the **direction** of the effect without proving it — the grades rest on the
cohort epidemiology, not the pathways. Body fatness does not act through one channel; it «may alter
the systemic metabolic milieu» across three linked routes:
[@wcrf2018ter] elevated fasting insulin
signalling through mTOR/PI3K/AKT to drive proliferation; elevated oestradiol driving
oestrogen-receptor-positive tissues (breast, endometrium); and chronic inflammation, «a chronic
inflammatory state that predisposes to cancer», tracked by C-reactive protein, which is elevated
with obesity and «reduces with weight loss».
[@wcrf2018ter] That last link is the
closest the report comes to a reversibility signal, but CRP is an inflammation marker, not a cancer
outcome — a surrogate, not proof that losing weight lowers cancer.

**The causal grade runs from body fatness to cancer *incidence* in *populations* — not from an
individual's weight loss to that individual's risk.** WCRF finds the survivor evidence on whether
changing body fatness after diagnosis alters the clinical course explicitly «limited», so the
reversal loop is open, exactly as it is for cardiovascular events
([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]): the largest, longest lifestyle-weight-loss
trial, Look AHEAD, was null on hard cardiovascular events (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83-1.09)
[@look2013], even though weight-loss
interventions pooled across 54 trials do lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.82, 0.71-0.95, high quality)
[@ma2017].

Reducing adiposity is therefore a broad lever on the disease-*incidence* axis — it
buys down cancer, cardiovascular and dementia risk on one action — but "body fatness causes cancer
in populations" is not the same claim as "your weight loss will cut your cancer," and the deliverable
does not stretch the population grade into the individual promise. (Weight loss is not a universal
good either; where it turns harmful — in the already-frail — is taken up with the frailty lever
below.)

### Alcohol has no cancer-safe floor

Alcohol is the sharpest illustration that a lever can behave differently on different outcomes. On
the all-cause mortality axis the pooled signal is graded and threshold-like: the largest updated
meta-analysis of 107 studies covering 4.8 million people finds all-cause mortality elevation reaching
significance around 45 g/day pooled (45-64 g/day RR 1.19, 1.07-1.32) and, for women, from 25 g/day
(RR 1.21, 1.08-1.36) [@zhao2023] — but
those grams mark where the mortality signal becomes unmissable in the data, **not** a dose below
which drinking is safe (the vascular and cancer risks rise from far lower intakes) ->
[[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]].

For cancer there is no such floor at all. WCRF grades alcohol a cause of mouth/pharynx/larynx,
oesophageal (squamous cell), breast, colorectal, liver and stomach cancer, and IARC classes
alcoholic drinks a **Group 1 (established) human carcinogen**.
[@wcrf2018ter] Its threshold verdict is
blunt: «there is no level of consumption below which there is no increase in the risk of at least
some cancers.» [@wcrf2018ter] The recommendation
follows directly — «For cancer prevention, it’s best not to drink alcohol.»
[@wcrf2018ter]

The population-burden data put a number on the low-dose tail: of the 741,300 cancers attributable to
alcohol worldwide in 2020,
«moderate drinking still contributed one in seven alcohol-attributable cases and more than 100 000
cancer cases worldwide», and even intake up to 10 g/day (roughly one drink) contributed tens of
thousands more. [@rumgay2021]

One genuine protective association exists and is instantly swamped: alcohol «helps protect against
kidney cancer (at least up to 30 grams or two drinks per day), but this is far outweighed by the
increased risk for other cancers.» [@wcrf2018ter] A real single-site benefit does not license drinking when the whole-body cancer ledger is
negative — the net effect is the one that decides, not the intended one.

**The decision is the same at both levers, on the outcome each demonstrably moves.** Reduce visceral
adiposity — the benefit accrues across a dozen cancer sites and pays even inside the "healthy" BMI
range, and the one action lowers cancer, cardiovascular and dementia risk together, which is why the
Layer-1 ranking places body fatness high. For alcohol there is no cancer-safe threshold to aim at:
the honest target is less, toward none, and the six-figure moderate-drinking case count means "a
little is fine" is not a cancer-prevention position.

## Screen muscle function, not the scale

Late-life musculoskeletal decline is where the disease-specific evidence is richest and the levers
clearest. Two conditions decide independence in old age — sarcopenia (the loss of muscle) and
osteoporosis (the loss of bone) — and both tempt the same mistake: chase a number on a machine (a DXA
mass reading, a bone-density score) or a pill, when the evidence points at **function and load**
instead. The single most useful correction is about what to measure.

### Strength predicts death better than mass does

When researchers ask which muscle measurement forecasts who dies, **strength wins, not size**. The
2019 European sarcopenia consensus (EWGSOP2) rebuilt its case definition around this, moving muscle
strength «to the forefront, as it is recognised that strength is better than mass in predicting adverse
out- comes» [@cruzjentoft2019], with
mass demoted to a confirmatory role. The strength evidence backs it at scale: in UK Biobank
(n=502,293), each 5 kg lower grip carried higher all-cause mortality — hazard ratio 1.20 (95%
CI 1.17-1.23) in women, 1.16 (1.15-1.17) in men — and per standard deviation grip out-ranked systolic
blood pressure as a mortality predictor [@celismorales2018]. A 48-cohort meta-analysis (\~3.1 million adults) put the weakest third of grip at HR 1.58
(1.40-1.78) versus the strongest [@lopezbueno2022].

Grip earns a second role as a **cheap screening metric**: a hand dynamometer adds risk discrimination
(+0.012 on the C-index) to an office score of age, sex, blood pressure, BMI, smoking and diabetes — no
blood draw needed [@celismorales2018]. A chair-stand
test does similar work for the legs. **Screen strength; it is fast, reproducible, and forecasts more
than the scale.**

### Mass still carries a signal — but read it as confirmatory, not a target

Muscle mass is not disposable. Pooling nine cohorts of non-frail adults over 65 (n=10,028), those who
died carried about 0.18 standard deviations less appendicular muscle mass than survivors (ASMI
standardized mean difference −0.18, 95% CI −0.23 to −0.12), and the gap was **not explained** by
between-study grip-strength differences [@santana2021] — mass carries prognostic information strength does not fully absorb. But hold the independence
lightly: the grip moderator was a **non-significant null on thin data** (coefficient −0.451, 95% CI
−1.470 to 0.564), which the authors concede «might simply be a result of underpowered studies».
Strength *could* still explain most of the association; this analysis cannot tell. So mass is
*consistent with* an independent signal, not proof of one. The signal is strongest in the **overweight
and obese** older adult (sarcopenic obesity), where low muscle and excess fat compound each other.

The reading that follows: **screen on strength and function; treat the mass number as confirmatory**,
and do not read the DXA reading as a proven treatment target.

### Every muscle lever here is a predictor, not yet a proven target

One caveat runs under all of it. The strength and mass findings are **observational** — grip and mass
are partly *lowered by* occult illness before death, so they forecast mortality partly as markers of
underlying decline. Grip strength is roughly half heritable, and even the landmark analysis (excluding
the first two years) does not remove reverse causation. **No trial shows that raising muscle mass or
grip lowers mortality; the loop is open.** That does not make the levers worthless — function,
independence and sarcopenia are patient-important outcomes in their own right, and the muscle you build
is what keeps you off the floor. It means the honest claim is *a low value is a bad sign worth acting
on*, not *squeeze harder to live longer*.

### Train resistance, and reach enough protein

The lever that moves both strength and mass is **resistance training, with protein as a modest
adjunct**. Across 49 RCTs (1,863 adults), adding protein on top of resistance training bought a further
+2.49 kg on one-rep-max strength (95% CI 0.64-4.33) and +0.30 kg of lean mass (0.09-0.52) over the
training alone [@morton2018].
These are **RCT-grade** effects — but on **surrogates** (mass and strength), not on a mortality or
disease endpoint. The training is the driver; protein does not build muscle, it lets the training build
a little more. A separate 138-trial meta-analysis (5,866 adults) adds a point that matters for the
frail who cannot train hard: protein raises lean mass even *without* resistance training, by roughly
half a kilogram [@tagawa2020].

On how much protein, resist false precision. Morton's diminishing-returns break-point sits near **1.62
g per kg of body weight per day, but the fit is statistically non-significant** (p=0.079) with a wide
interval (95% CI 1.03-2.20) [@morton2018]; Tagawa's inflection lands lower, near 1.3 g/kg, its resistance-trained arm still gaining
above it. So the target is a **region of roughly 1.3-1.6 g/kg, not a threshold** — and a **floor to
reach, not a ceiling to stay under**. The number was measured in a mostly young sample where the
supplement's effect is *largest* and decays with age, so an older adult, whose muscle resists the
anabolic signal, needs at least as much and should not aim low on a non-significant young knee. (The
figure is per kilogram of *total* body weight, so it is ill-defined for a very heavy person.)

### Skip the bone pill in the replete — load the skeleton instead

Osteoporosis repeats the shape: the intuitive move — a vitamin D and calcium supplement for the bones
— **fails in the well-nourished, community-dwelling older adult**. The US Preventive Services Task
Force evidence review (11 RCTs, N=51,419, with the deficient, institutionalized, osteoporotic and
prior-fracture groups excluded) found vitamin D plus calcium moved total fractures by an absolute
−0.35% (95% CI −1.02 to 0.31) and hip fractures by −0.14% (−0.34 to 0.07) — a **null, graded low
strength of evidence for no benefit** [@kahwati2018].

Vitamin D alone is no better: the large VITAL trial found no reduction in total
(HR 0.98, 0.89-1.08) or hip (HR 1.01, 0.70-1.47) fractures
[@leboff2022]. The fracture benefit lives in the
*excluded* high-risk strata (about 1 fewer hip fracture per 1000 per year, 95% CI 0 to 2), and the
combination modestly **raised kidney stones** (RR 1.18, 1.04-1.35, moderate strength — the review's
firmest finding).

This is not "supplements don't work"; it is the **enhancement-in-the-replete arm of a two-arm curve**:
a repletion lever for the confirmed-deficient, not a community-wide one.

So for everyone whose vitamin D is adequate, the bone-protective lever is not the pill — it is
**weight-bearing and balance exercise**. (The loading channel raises bone density; the balance channel,
carried in the next section, prevents the falls that break bones.) Frailty is the next handle — both a
marker and a target.

## Treat frailty as a lever, and dose it down once someone is frail

Frailty — the syndrome of depleted physiological reserve, where minor stresses tip an older person into
disability or death — is easy to read as a verdict. The evidence reads it as a **lever**: it both
forecasts hard outcomes with unusual strength *and* moves when the right exercise is applied. Neither
literature makes the case alone: frailty forecasts hard outcomes, and exercise moves it — together they
turn frailty into a lever.

### Frailty forecasts hard outcomes — and exercise moves it

The prognostic leg is large. Pooling 31 prospective studies (158,764 community-dwelling adults over
65), frail adults faced roughly double the risk of dying (odds ratio 2.34, 95% CI 1.77-3.09; hazard
ratio 1.83, 1.68-1.98), of disability, and of fractures (OR 2.78, 1.30-5.99) versus robust peers
[@vermeiren2016frailty].
Crucially the risk climbs along a gradient — robust to *prefrail* to frail — so the prefrail state is
the earlier, more remediable handle. These are observational associations (heavily confounded, high
heterogeneity), so frailty here is a risk marker; the causal test is the intervention.

That leg holds. Across 26 studies (24 RCTs, 8,022 *identified* prefrail or frail adults over 65),
physical-activity interventions improved mobility (SMD 0.60, 95% CI 0.37-0.83), activities of daily
living (0.50, 0.15-0.84), cognition (0.35, 0.09-0.61), quality of life (0.60, 0.13-1.07) and frailty
status itself (SMD −1.29, −2.22 to −0.36; risk ratio 0.58, 0.36-0.93), at **moderate certainty**
[@racey2021frailtypa]. Two honest
limits ride along: the effective categories were **muscle-strengthening and mixed** programmes, not
activity in general, and some reversal figures rest on single small trials (the strongest,
frailty-reversal RR 0.21, is one 66-person study). Frailty was measured in only 4 of the trials, so
read the reversal number as a signal, not a treat-to-target promise.

### Dose the exercise down for the already-frail

The general exercise-for-older-adults prescription **does not transport to the frail**. Racey's authors
are explicit that «recommended physical activity ... may be too advanced or too intense for a frailer
population, put- ting them at risk for falls and injuries» [@racey2021frailtypa]. The programmes that worked were **supervised and
adjusted** — roughly 29 weeks, 30-60 minutes, 1-4 times a week — favouring strengthening and mixed
training.

The stratum split is sharpest on falls. In the **general** community-dwelling older adult, exercise
prevents falls with **high certainty**: a Cochrane review of 108 RCTs (23,407 participants, mean age
76) found a rate ratio of 0.77 (95% CI 0.71-0.83, 59 RCTs) — about 195 fewer falls per 1000 people per
year — driven specifically by **balance and functional training** (RaR 0.76, 0.70-0.81), while
**resistance training alone did not reduce falls** (RaR 1.14, 0.66-1.97) and walking programmes had
insufficient evidence [@sherrington2019]. Downstream,
fall-related fractures fell (RR 0.73, 0.56-0.95, low certainty). But in the **already-frail** stratum,
that falls benefit **does not hold** — Racey found no significant fall reduction (RR 0.80, 0.51-1.26,
very low certainty) even as mobility and frailty status improved [@racey2021frailtypa]. The lesson is a boundary, not a contradiction:
**balance-based fall prevention is best-evidenced before deep frailty**; once frailty is established,
dose the exercise down, favour strengthening and mixed work, and let the payoff shift to function and
mobility rather than falls.

### Do not prescribe weight loss to the frail

One shared lever **reverses direction** here. Reducing body fat is a broad benefit on the
disease-*incidence* axis (cancer, cardiovascular disease, dementia), but **weight loss is itself a
diagnostic criterion for frailty** and can harm the frail — it is not a general function lever. Naming
this does not contradict the broad-population case for shedding excess adiposity; it marks the stratum
where the sign flips. In the identified-frail older adult, the move is to preserve mass and function,
not to prescribe a deficit.

The decision-change for late life is therefore compact: **identify frailty early** (a high-baseline-
risk stratum with a lever that moves), **dose the exercise down** and supervise it, **favour
strengthening and mixed training over intensity**, and **do not prescribe weight loss to the frail**.
The most-studied dementia-prevention strategy is a cautionary tale about surrogates.

## Dementia-prevention bundles move the test score, not the disease

The most-studied way to prevent dementia is the cautionary tale of the whole
deliverable, because it is where a moving marker and an unmoved disease sit
side by side. Three landmark randomised trials tested the same idea — act on the
modifiable dementia risk factors *as a bundle* — and their results depend on
which endpoint you measure, who is in the trial, and how hard the intervention
pushes. Read together, they license pulling the levers, not promising the payoff.

### The surrogate moved once, then would not move again

**FINGER, the proof-of-concept trial, moved a cognitive-test composite by a real
but small amount.** In 1,260 at-risk but cognitively near-normal Finns, a
two-year diet-plus-exercise-plus-cognitive-training-plus-vascular-monitoring
bundle improved the neuropsychological test battery by **0.022 Z-points per year
(95% CI 0.002-0.042, p=0.030), a Cohen's d of 0.13** against an active-advice
control [@ngandu2015]. That composite is a **surrogate for dementia** — a marker measured because
it is fast and common, standing in for the disease people actually fear
[[Surrogate Outcomes]]. FINGER never measured dementia itself.

**MAPT then ran the closest replication and found nothing at the population
level.** Its bundle was the same lifestyle type on the same kind of
cognitive-composite endpoint, against a *weaker* placebo comparator that should
have made a difference easier to detect — yet the multidomain arm moved the
score by **0.079 (95% CI -0.012 to 0.170, adjusted p=0.179)** and the combined
arm's borderline **0.093** lost significance to multiple-comparison correction
(adjusted p=0.142) [@andrieu2017]. So the surrogate itself is shaky: FINGER barely moved it, and
MAPT did not move it at all. The two reconcile only inside higher-risk strata —
MAPT's own CAIDE>=6 and amyloid-positive subgroups reproduced FINGER's signal —
which sets up the responder question the hard endpoint has to answer.

### The hard endpoint did not move, and no responder rescued it

**preDIVA declined the surrogate and measured dementia directly — for 6.7 years,
in 3,526 unselected Dutch elderly — and found no reduction.** Nurse-led vascular
care lowered blood pressure modestly yet left dementia incidence flat at
**HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.71-1.19)** [@mollvancharante2016]. Read this as *not-yet-shown*
rather than *shown-absent*: the trial was powered for a 33% reduction, roughly an
order of magnitude larger than blood-pressure lowering plausibly delivers, so it
is underpowered for the effect that is actually plausible.

**The obvious rescue — target the highest-risk people — was tested on the hard
endpoint and failed.** Coley pooled the individual-participant data of MAPT and
preDIVA (**n=5,205, up to 12 years, 486 dementia cases**) and hunted for
responders two ways: 11 pre-specified risk-factor subgroups, and a data-driven
SIDES search free to combine any variables at any cut-point. The overall effect
was flat (**HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.80-1.21**), no pre-specified subgroup showed a
benefit — including the CAIDE>=6 and untreated-hypertension cells the surrogate
trials had singled out — and the algorithmic search, biased toward a false
positive by design, found nothing [@coley2025]. **The surrogate-level responder signal does not
survive to dementia incidence.** Personalising the bundle by baseline dementia
risk is not, on this evidence, a demonstrated way to convert it into prevention.

### The Mediterranean pattern is an association, and probably not an extra lever

A Mediterranean-diet pattern tracks lower Alzheimer's risk — **RR 0.89 (95% CI
0.84-0.93) per one-point rise on the 0-9 adherence score** — but the evidence is
observational, low-to-moderate certainty, and beset by healthy-user confounding
and reverse causation over dementia's long prodrome [@garciacasares2021]. More to the point, the benefit
runs largely *through* blood pressure, glucose, weight and lipids — levers
already counted among the cardiometabolic big rocks — so it is mostly a route to
pull those, not a fifteenth independent addition on top.

**The decision this section forces:** pull the bundle's levers for the outcomes
they have actually been shown to move — cardiovascular events, and the
cardiometabolic markers that drive them — because every one of them is
well-warranted on its own. Do not promise a person a cognitive or dementia
payoff on top of that. A marker moving the right way is not the disease
prevented, and here the two have been measured apart
[[Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention and Cognitive Decline]].

## Correcting hearing and vision, and preventing head injury, are dementia's own levers

Beyond the shared cardiometabolic core, dementia carries a handful of specific
levers the other diseases on this list do not — and they are genuine increments,
not the big rocks renamed.

### Hearing loss is a lever because the exposure is correctable

**Hearing loss is associated with dementia at HR 1.37 (95% CI 1.00-1.87)** in the
2024 Lancet Commission's meta-analysis of six high-quality cohorts, with a graded
exposure-response — every 10 dB of worse hearing raises risk by somewhere between
4% and 24% [@livingston2024]. The
lower confidence bound touches 1.00, and the estimate is deliberately
conservative, so treat it as a medium-confidence causal read rather than a
settled one. What makes hearing distinctive is that **the exposure can be
corrected**, and the correction sits plausibly on the causal path — so the
intervention question is answerable, not just the association.

The answer is stratum-dependent. Observationally, hearing-aid users run about
**17% lower dementia risk and 19% lower risk of cognitive decline** than the
uncorrected. The first randomised test, ACHIEVE, found **no overall effect on
three-year cognition (difference -0.002)** but a pre-specified **48% protective
effect in its higher-risk ARIC subgroup** (older, lower baseline cognition, more
cardiovascular risk) [@livingston2024]. That is a route-(b) effect-modification signal concentrated where baseline
risk is highest — one subgroup of one trial, so the aided-benefit case still
rests mainly on consistent observational evidence.

### Uncorrected vision and head injury complete the dementia-specific set

**Uncorrected vision impairment carries RR 1.47 (95% CI 1.36-1.60) and traumatic
brain injury RR 1.66 (95% CI 1.42-1.93)** for dementia [@livingston2024]. The decision is direct: **correct hearing
and vision — the treatment exists and uptake is the gap — and prevent head
injury.** For hearing and vision the largest absolute benefit falls where
dementia risk is already high, so the higher-risk stratum is where a clinician's
attention pays.

## Parkinson's is the thin end: physical activity is the one credible lever

Parkinson's is the deliberate contrast to dementia — a disease with few credible
levers and mostly weak evidence, where the honest finding is a low ceiling, and
reporting that ceiling is itself the result.

### The evidence quality is the headline, not the factor list

An umbrella review of 46 systematic reviews grades its own field brutally: under
AMSTAR-2, **none rated high or moderate quality, and 39 of 46 (about 85%) were
«judged to be of critically low quality»** [@chen2021pd]. Of the 50 exposures with a
significant association, **only 19 were supported by prospective cohort
evidence**; the other 31 rested on case-control or mixed designs too bias-prone
to conclude from. Forty-six low-quality reviews do not launder into confidence —
grade each factor by its design, not by the count of reviews behind it.

### One credible lever, several artifact-suspect signals

**Physical activity is the single credible shared lever: RR 0.79 (95% CI
0.68-0.91), zero heterogeneity, dose-dependent by intensity — though null in
women (0.91)** [@chen2021pd]. And it is already a big rock pulled for mortality,
cardiovascular disease and cognition, so it buys a *possible* Parkinson's benefit
as a rider, not as a new competing lever.

The famous protective signals are almost certainly not levers. **Smoking (RR
0.64) and coffee or caffeine (0.55-0.67) are artifact-suspect**, because
Parkinson's has a years-to-decades prodrome that lowers activity, smoking and
coffee intake *before* diagnosis — reverse causation that manufactures apparent
protection [[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]. Smoking's inverse association is
a curiosity to explain, never a recommendation, and its net harm dwarfs any
Parkinson's signal. The protective *drug* signals (statins, calcium-channel
blockers) are confounded by indication. The one dietary *risk* worth naming is
dairy or milk (**RR \~1.4-1.6**), a modest association of uncertain mechanism, not
a demonstrated cause. The heavily-discussed pesticide and occupational signals
rest on case-control evidence — real, but lower-certainty than their prominence
implies.

**The decision:** pull physical activity, which is warranted anyway; do not chase
the pesticide or coffee folklore; and treat smoking-to-protect as an artifact,
never an action. Three residual gaps keep even this modest picture provisional —
no effect-size meta-analysis for the physical-activity dose is held here, no
Mendelian-randomisation or lag analysis has been run to adjudicate the
reverse-causation arms, and Parkinson's carries no population-attributable or
absolute-risk framing at all, so the absolute benefit of any lever at a stated
baseline risk cannot be computed [@chen2021pd].

## Age-related eye disease adds no new lever

Age-related eye disease is the same thin-evidence pole as Parkinson's: one
confirmatory lever, one unproven bet, and no separate action for most people.
**Cataract** (the leading cause of blindness, but typically reversible by
surgery) has a physical-activity arm graded WCRF *probable* — **RR 0.90 (95% CI
0.86-0.94)**, monotone over the studied range. But its E-value of 1.46 means a
merely moderate unmeasured confounder could explain it away, so it reads as a
real-but-marginal *confirmatory* arm on a lever (physical activity) already
pulled for stronger reasons [@aune2026vision]. It bites hardest where cataract
baseline risk is high — diabetes, high BMI.

For **age-related macular degeneration** (central-vision loss, irreversible) the
same paper reaches **RR 0.92 (95% CI 0.84-1.01)** — the interval crosses 1.00,
graded «limited, and no conclusion could be drawn» [@aune2026vision]. This is
**insufficient evidence, not a demonstrated null**, and it must not borrow the
cataract arm's significance — they are distinct diseases graded separately in one
paper.

A *different* exposure reaches AMD: a Mediterranean-diet pattern tracks slower
AMD *progression* on the strongest observational design (cohort **HR 0.77, 95% CI
0.67-0.88**), but the warrant is low (observational, eight studies, self-reported
diet), the signal is for progression rather than development, and the
Mediterranean pattern is already a big rock [@marquescouto2025]. **So there
is no eye-specific action beyond «stay active»** — and, for someone already at
AMD risk, an eating pattern they would adopt on cardiometabolic grounds anyway.
Menopause changes which levers matter without adding one.

## Menopause shifts which levers matter without adding a new one

Menopause is a life-stage stratum, not a disease, and it changes the ranking of the levers already on
this list rather than introducing one of its own. The popular story — *menopause makes women gain
weight* — puts the emphasis in the wrong place, and correcting it changes what a woman should measure
and act on.

### What changes is where the fat sits, not how much there is

The largest meta-analysis of the question — Ambikairajah, 201 cross-sectional studies and \~1.05 million
women — separates two things routine advice runs together [@ambikairajah2019]. **Total fat gain across midlife tracks aging, not menopause:**
BMI rises about +1.14 kg/m2 from pre- to post-menopause, but a meta-regression finds the increase is
«predominantly attributable to increasing age with menopause having no significant additional
influence» [@ambikairajah2019]. What
menopause more likely does — on hedged, mostly cross-sectional evidence the authors call a *possible*
shift, not an established one — is **redistribute fat centrally**: trunk and visceral fat up, leg fat
down. The visceral gain is large cross-sectionally (+26.90 cm2, 95% CI 13.12-40.68) but roughly halves
in the more precise longitudinal studies (+12.95 cm2, 8.65-17.25) — soft magnitude, but a clear
direction.

Central fat, not total fat, is the cardiometabolic driver — the same meta-analysis notes CVD risk rises
about 2% per additional centimetre of waist — and this is why the right instrument shifts. **After
menopause, BMI and the scale are confounded by bone loss, muscle loss, and height shrinkage**, all of
which move BMI without tracking adiposity; waist circumference and trunk-fat percentage are the more
reliable measures [@ambikairajah2019].
A normal BMI therefore does not clear a post-menopausal woman of central-adiposity risk. The levers that
answer the redistribution are ones the wiki already ranks highly — visceral-fat reduction, resistance
training with adequate protein, weight-bearing and balance work for bone — pulled at a shifted baseline
(a route-(a) stratification), not a new intervention menu.

### Hormone therapy treats symptoms and bone, not body composition or ageing

Hormone therapy (HRT) is the realistic pharmacotherapy comparator for this stratum, and its evidence
sorts the popular *anti-ageing* framing from what the trials show. **On muscle, HRT does nothing:**
systematic reviews find it had «neither a beneficial nor harmful association with muscle mass», so
«interventions other than hormone therapy will have to be developed to aid in the retention of muscle
in aging women» [@nams2022]. On abdominal fat
its effect is «small» [@nams2022]. HRT's
genuine benefits are elsewhere — vasomotor symptoms and fracture prevention — and its harms turn on
**formulation**. Estrogen-plus-progestin carries a sustained breast-cancer signal at \~20-year
follow-up (HR 1.28, 1.13-1.45) that is **absent from estrogen-alone** (HR 0.78, 0.65-0.93, a
reduction); *oral* but not *transdermal* estrogen carries the stroke signal
[@nams2022].

The one place two gold guideline bodies read the same trials differently is timing: NAMS endorses a
cardiovascular and mortality benefit for women who start before 60 or within 10 years of menopause,
while NICE declines it, judging the subgroup signals non-significant. **Both agree on the point that
matters most here — HRT does not change all-cause mortality** (WHI HR 0.98, 0.82-1.18)
[@rossouw2002], so the decision rests on
symptom relief and bone against a rare breast-cancer, stroke, and clotting harm, not on a promised
lengthening of life -> [[Hormone Therapy After Menopause]], [[Menopause and the Shifting Levers]].

For a woman at this life stage the decision-change is concrete: **watch the waist, not the BMI**, and
treat HRT as a symptom-and-bone therapy whose formulation is weighed against a rare harm — never as a
body-composition, muscle, or anti-ageing lever.

## Supplements are a near-null lever for the well-nourished — and one does clear harm

The most-discussed dietary add-ons move little, and the supplement aisle is the sharpest case. For a
generally-nourished adult without a known deficiency, taking vitamins, minerals, or a multivitamin to
prevent cardiovascular disease, cancer, fractures, or death is — on the best evidence — a null lever,
and for one named agent a harmful one.

### Two different nulls, kept apart

The US Preventive Services Task Force graded supplementation for disease prevention in community-dwelling
adults without known deficiency, pooling the randomized-trial evidence so the healthy-user confound is
largely designed out [@uspstf2022supp]. Its
verdicts land in **two distinct evidence states**. Beta carotene and vitamin E get a **grade D —
recommend against**: the Task Force concludes «there is no net benefit of supplementation with vitamin
E for the prevention of cardiovascular disease or cancer» — a graded *no-effect* (and, for beta
carotene, net harm). Multivitamins and single nutrients get a **grade I — insufficient**: «the evidence
is insufficient to determine the balance of benefits and harms»
[@uspstf2022supp]. Insufficient evidence is
the fourth evidence-state, not a demonstrated null — the honest reading is *we cannot tell*, not *it
works*. Neither grade is benefit.

### The one clear harm: beta carotene in smokers (a Big-Rock avoid)

Beta carotene is the case where *more is better* is simply false, and it belongs on the avoid list
rather than the shrug list. In two trials of people who smoke or were exposed to asbestos, beta carotene
**raised lung cancer** (RR 1.18, 95% CI 1.03-1.36), and pooled across trials it raised **cardiovascular
mortality** (Peto OR 1.10, 1.02-1.19) [@uspstf2022supp]. This is a small but *certain* harm on hard outcomes — a dose above repletion moving the
wrong way — so a smoker considering an antioxidant supplement should treat beta carotene as a
contraindication, not a toss-up -> [[Vitamin and Mineral Supplements for Disease Prevention]].

### Enhancement in the replete is the null; correcting a confirmed deficiency is not

The load-bearing distinction is *who is taking the pill*. Every null above tested **enhancement in the
already-replete** — adding a nutrient to people who have enough — which sits on the flat plateau (buys
nothing) or past the upper bound (harms). **Correcting a confirmed deficiency is a different question
with real answers**: periconceptional folic acid cuts neural-tube defects (a hard outcome), and
repleting deficient people benefits on outcomes their replete counterparts do not
-> [[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]]. The two must not be collapsed — an enhancement-null does not
mean the nutrient is unimportant; it means more, in someone who already has enough, does not help. For a
well-nourished adult the decision-change is to **stop supplementing to prevent disease** and spend the
attention on the proven big rocks; the exception is to **correct a deficiency once it is actually
confirmed**, which needs a test, not a guess.

## Eggs and red meat are small or contested — and the attention is the anti-signal

Two diet-specific items attract discussion out of all proportion to their effect. Both sit low in the
ranking, and the volume of argument around them is a signal about the field, not about the food.

### Eggs: a non-lever for most, with two stratum exceptions

For the general population, eggs are close to a non-lever for cardiovascular disease. The largest
dose-response meta-analysis (\~2 million people) found no conclusive link, with risk estimates sitting
near 1.00 and confidence intervals kissing the null, at GRADE **low** certainty [@godos2020]. **Two stratified exceptions survive** (route-(b)
effect modification, both low-certainty): in **diabetic** cohorts the association reverses to a modest
harm (cardiovascular summary RR 1.22, 1.08-1.39 at about one egg per day), and **heart failure** shows a
high-intake signal (HR 1.15, 1.02-1.30) [@godos2020]. So the decision-change is narrow: a person without diabetes or heart failure can stop worrying
about eggs on cardiovascular grounds, while those two strata are the exception where intake is worth
watching.

### Red and processed meat: the same evidence, graded two ways

Red and processed meat carry a real but small colorectal-cancer signal that two gold guideline bodies
read in opposite directions — and the disagreement matters precisely because **it is not about the
numbers**. WCRF grades processed meat a **convincing** and red meat a **probable** cause of colorectal
cancer (red RR 1.12, 1.00-1.25 per 100 g/day; processed 1.16, 1.08-1.26 per 50 g/day), grades that in
its framework justify a public recommendation to limit intake
[@wcrf2018].

NutriRECS applies strict GRADE to
**the same cohort evidence**, calls it **low-to-very-low certainty**, and computes an absolute effect
WCRF never states: a realistic cut of three servings per week yields «a very small and often trivial
absolute risk reduction» — about 7 fewer cancer deaths per 1000 people over a lifetime — and so issues
only a weak recommendation that adults may continue current consumption
[@nutrirecs2019].

**The wiki does not pick a side, because the evidence does not force one.** Both bodies see the same
modest, mostly-colorectal association; they diverge on the standard of proof a public recommendation
requires and on whose values set the action threshold — a decision-theoretic disagreement, not an
empirical one -> [[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]]. The honest report is both halves at
once: the absolute effect is small and the certainty is low (NutriRECS's contribution), and a
precautionary reading remains available for a person who weights a possible cancer cause heavily
(WCRF's). A processed-meat cut rests on firmer footing than a red-meat cut — a significant association
and a stronger grade — so the two are not one exposure. The decision-change is to keep this in
proportion: the effect is small and contested, the attention it draws is the anti-signal, and the
weighting belongs to the person.

## Re-rank the same levers as a person ages

The levers do not change across the life course; their ranking does. Three facts move the order,
and none of them requires a new subgroup claim.

**Midlife is when the shared cardiometabolic levers carry the risk for later disease.** The dementia
body counts blood pressure, LDL, diabetes, adiposity and smoking as *midlife* exposures driving
*late-life* incidence, and its operative timing rule is to lower those risk factors early and hold them
low across life [@livingston2024] (the full timing rule is
quoted in the shared-core section above).

Acting late may arrive after the window
has closed: preDIVA, a 6.7-year multidomain vascular-care trial in an *unselected* population «aged
70–78 years», found no reduction in dementia incidence (HR 0.92, 0.71-1.19), and its own authors point
to the age mismatch — «most observational data show an association between midlife (age 40–60 years)
vascular risk factors and dementia»
[@mollvancharante2016]. The lever is
the same; started too late, it does less. -> [[Dementia Prevention and Modifiable Risk Factors]]

**Later life is when function and independence become the deciding outcomes.** Falls, frailty,
sarcopenia and osteoporosis do not weigh heavily on a 40-year-old and dominate the picture at 80,
where the outcome that matters is mobility, independence and staying out of a care setting rather than
a distant incidence figure. Low muscle mass is already a prognostic marker in community-dwelling older
adults [@santana2021] — a signal that simply is not
on the board earlier (its effect size is given in the muscle-mass section above). So the later-life ranking lifts resistance training, adequate protein and
balance/weight-bearing work alongside the cardiometabolic core.

**Absolute benefit rises with age because baseline risk does — this is arithmetic, not a subgroup
effect.** A roughly constant relative effect applied to a larger baseline risk yields a larger absolute
benefit, so the same lever is worth more in an older person purely by route (a), needing no interaction
claim -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]. **The scaling has one honest limit: it holds
only for a lever with a proven effect on the hard outcome.** Where a lever moves only a surrogate — the
muscle-mass signal is a predictor, not a demonstrated treatment target, and its loop is open — higher
baseline risk does not manufacture real benefit, so *raise your muscle mass to live longer* over-reads
the evidence in exactly the group where the temptation is strongest.


**The decision-change:** a younger adult should pull the shared cardiometabolic rocks early and hold
them low, banking decades of compounding exposure; an older adult should keep those rocks but weight
balance, resistance training, protein and function outcomes alongside them — the same regimen,
re-ordered, not a different one.

## Keep length and function on separate axes

The diseases in this survey are counted mostly as death or disease *events*, yet the outcome many
people weight most heavily is the *trajectory* — full function followed by a rapid end, versus a long
slow decline — and that shape is a different quantity from length of life. Falls, mobility, cognition
and independence are patient-important in their own right, often rated *critical*, and a clean
mortality or incidence result can be silent on them.

The dementia evidence is partly a trajectory finding: reducing dementia risk «increases healthy years
of life and compresses the duration of ill
health», pushing onset back *further* than it extends life expectancy
[@livingston2024]. But trials rarely measure the trajectory
directly — the streetlight falls on the event, not the shape — so where an exposure's effect on
compressed morbidity is unmeasured, that gap is named, not filled with the survival endpoint.


**The decision-change:** name which outcomes each lever moves, and flag where the trajectory is
unmeasured. The two axes are not collapsed into one score — there is no scalar that trades a year of
life against a year of function, because that weighting is irreducibly the person's, elicited case by
case rather than supplied here.

## Know what this evidence cannot tell you

The shared-core framing is powerful, but three real limits sit underneath it, and naming them is itself
a result.

**The overlap does not add up to a single number.** Each shared lever acts on a *different* outcome on
a *different* scale — a dementia hazard ratio, a cancer-incidence grade, a cardiovascular event rate —
so pulling one lever buys protection against several diseases at once, but those gains are
co-occurring separate effects, not commensurable magnitudes. There is no common metric that sums them
into a *diseases-of-ageing* effect, and the population-attributable fractions do not add either (they
overlap and are discounted for communality). This is a structural gap (`type-G`), not a missing
calculation: the number cannot be computed, so it must not be implied
-> [[Shared Modifiable Levers Across Age-Related Diseases]].

**The shared mechanism is a hypothesis, not a held finding.** The overlap is *epidemiological
co-membership* — the same exposures recur across the disease-specific risk lists — which is evidence
that the levers are shared, not evidence of a tested common pathway. The parsimonious reading, that a
common cardiometabolic-vascular substrate underlies several age-related diseases, is marked as
inference on the synthesis page and is not tested here. The broader geroscience *hallmarks-of-ageing*
thesis — that the diseases share upstream ageing biology — is a mechanistic backbone this corpus does
**not** hold (no such source is registered), so it must not be read into the overlap as though it were.

**Several of the largest modifiable cancer causes sit outside this corpus entirely.** After tobacco,
much of the preventable cancer burden runs through exposures the held sources never appraise —
ultraviolet radiation (skin), HPV (cervical), hepatitis B and C (liver), *Helicobacter pylori*
(stomach). These are modifiable and large, but no source held here covers them, so their absence is a
**coverage gap, not a finding that they are small** — the shared-core map is complete only for the
levers the corpus has read.

**The decision-change:** treat *one lever, several diseases* as multiplied coverage, never as a summed
total; do not read a common mechanism into the overlap; and hold the corpus's cancer blind spots as
known unknowns. (Parkinson's carries its own residual gaps — an unheld physical-activity effect-size
meta-analysis and the absence of any absolute-risk framing — named where that disease is discussed.)

## The bottom line

- **Pull the shared cardiometabolic levers first** — blood pressure, body fat and visceral adiposity,
  blood glucose, blood lipids, not smoking, and holding alcohol down. Blood pressure, glucose and lipids
  buy risk reduction across cardiovascular disease and dementia; body fat, tobacco and alcohol reach
  several cancers too. Midlife is when they matter most.
- **Make physical activity the default lever** — it is the one exposure with credible signal against
  the widest set of these diseases. Match the *type* to the target (balance for falls, strength for
  muscle, aerobic or mixed for cardiometabolic risk); "move more" alone is underspecified.
- **In later life, screen muscle *function* — strength and gait — not the scale**, train resistance
  work plus adequate protein, and identify frailty early, dosing its exercise down once it is
  established.
- **Do not spend attention on eggs, red meat, or a supplement stack** for a well-nourished person —
  they are small, contested, or null, and beta carotene harms a smoker. Correct a *confirmed*
  deficiency; do not supplement to enhance the replete.
- **Treat the prevention bundles as unproven on the disease itself** — the multidomain dementia bundle
  moves a marker, not yet dementia incidence; pull its levers for their proven cardiometabolic outcomes,
  not for a promised cognitive payoff.
- **At menopause, watch the waist, not the BMI** — use hormone therapy for symptoms and bone, not as a
  body-composition or anti-ageing lever, and weigh its formulation against a rare harm.
- **The eye-disease levers are marginal** — there is no separate action beyond staying active, and, for
  someone already at risk of macular degeneration, an eating pattern they would adopt on cardiometabolic
  grounds anyway.

### Caveats that bind every claim above

- **The loop is open.** This deliverable grades coherence and source-fidelity, never validity. No
  operation here checks any lever against a realized outcome; a clean audit is not a validated
  recommendation.
- **It appraises evidence; it does not prescribe.** Selecting, dosing, screening contraindications, and
  managing interactions are prescriber acts needing this person's labs, history, and medications.
- **Estimates are population-level by default; stratify per person.** Personalize beyond the population
  estimate only on positive effect-modification evidence (as in the diabetic-egg reversal), not on
  mechanistic plausibility. Route-(a) baseline-risk stratification -- scaling absolute benefit by age or
  risk -- needs no subgroup claim.
