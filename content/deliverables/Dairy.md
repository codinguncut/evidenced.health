Milk, cheese, butter and yoghurt carry similar saturated fat, yet the outcomes they touch — heart
disease, early death, diabetes, bone fracture, cancer, dementia — point in different directions.
Read one food and one outcome at a time, and almost every effect turns out small.

**For heart disease, stroke and early death, dairy is close to a wash.** Eating more, or less, barely
moves the needle, and full-fat versus low-fat is not the fault-line that guidance implies. **The one
diabetes signal is real but small, and it belongs to yoghurt and low-fat dairy** — milk, cheese and
high-fat dairy show nothing, and a genetic study confirms milk has no causal effect. **The scary
*milk shortens your life* result is a statistical artifact**, traceable to a single Swedish cohort that
takes the association with it when removed. Dairy does not build bone or protect the brain either.
Cancer is the one endpoint this evidence cannot settle. None of this makes dairy a lever worth much
worry — the noise around it runs far ahead of the effect.

## Dairy is several foods, not one — judge each category and endpoint on its own

The single word *dairy* hides categories that behave almost like different foods. Split them three ways
before ranking anything ([[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]):

- **Matrix-intact vs matrix-stripped** — cheese and yoghurt keep the milk's structure; butter and cream
  strip it down toward the fat. The saturated fat is similar; the food around it is not.
- **Fermented vs unfermented** — yoghurt and cheese carry live or once-live cultures; milk does not.
- **Full-fat vs low-fat** — the axis guidance leans on, and (as below) the one that turns out to matter
  least.

Guo's meta-analysis is the worked case: it splits dairy five ways and the answers **differ by cell**,
while the aggregate *total dairy* hides them [@guo2017]. So a whole-milk finding must never be read as a yoghurt finding, nor a
butter finding as a cheese finding. Every claim below attaches to a named category and a single endpoint.

## For heart disease and early death, dairy barely moves the needle

Across roughly 940,000 people, Guo found **total dairy neutral for both death and cardiovascular
disease**: per 200 g/day, all-cause mortality RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.96-1.03) and CVD RR 0.97 (0.91-1.02)
[@guo2017]. Milk alone was
null too (per 244 g/day, RR 1.00 for mortality), though with extreme study-to-study heterogeneity
(I2 = 97.4%) that the next section explains.

**Full-fat and low-fat dairy were both null** — high-fat CVD RR 0.93 (0.84-1.03), low-fat 0.98
(0.95-1.01) [@guo2017]. Guo ran
no direct swap of one for the other, so this does not endorse the low-fat guidance default; it simply
finds no difference in the observed contrast. The large food-group meta-analysis series agrees where it
overlaps: dairy was null for all-cause mortality (RR 0.98, 0.93-1.03) and for coronary heart disease and
stroke [@schwingshackl2017mort]. **The
one exception is a small heart-failure signal** — RR 1.08 (1.01-1.15) per 200 g/day
[@bechthold2019] — low
certainty, observational, and named here rather than inflated into a headline.

### Butter, the near-pure-fat category, is at worst weakly positive

Butter is the case where the saturated-fat worry should bite hardest. It does not, much. A butter
meta-analysis reported through Guo found per 14 g/day only RR 1.01 (1.00-1.03) for mortality, no
significant heart-disease association, and an *inverse* signal for diabetes (RR 0.96, 0.93-0.99)
[@guo2017].

A direct study (Zhang 2025, 221,054 adults) later found highest-versus-lowest butter intake carried 15%
higher total mortality (HR 1.15, 1.08-1.22) with no significant CVD-mortality link
[@zhang2025butter]. The two are consistent once you match
the comparator. The near-flat per-gram slope measures butter against a junk-food-heavy Western diet; the
positive contrast turns into benefit only when butter is **replaced by a named better food** — swapping
10 g/day of butter for plant oils tracked 17% lower mortality (HR 0.83, 0.79-0.86)
[@zhang2025butter]. The lesson is a substitution one, not a
verdict on butter in isolation ([[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]]).

### The one source that reads dairy as actively protective — and why it does not overturn neutral

PURE's healthy-diet analysis (Mente 2023) counts whole-fat dairy among only six protective foods and
suggests up to \~185 g/day «can be included with other beneficial foods as part of a healthy diet»
[@mente2023pure]. Read it with three guards. It measures dairy
*as one component of a whole-diet score*, tangled with the other five protective foods and the
healthy-eater gradient — not the same quantity as Guo's dairy-specific dose-response. It shares authors
and cohort machinery with the wider PURE programme, so it is not an independent second witness. And it is
part-funded by dairy-industry councils, exactly where such a sponsor would push
[@mente2023pure]. It nudges the range toward
neutral-to-mildly-favourable for whole-fat dairy; it does not license a *full-fat dairy is
cardioprotective* claim.

## The *milk shortens your life* scare is a single-cohort artifact

Public discourse's scariest dairy claim — high milk intake, higher death — comes from Swedish cohorts
where «higher milk consumption was associated with a doubling of mortality risk»
[@guo2017]. Run the artifact
checks ([[The U-Shaped Association Artifact]]) and it collapses:

- **The pooled milk-mortality estimate is null (1.00).** The Swedish cohort adds only heterogeneity;
  removing it drops I2 from 97.4% to 70.1% and leaves RR 0.99 (0.96-1.01)
  [@guo2017].
- **The high-milk drinkers were confounded**, being the group with the most smokers and the most people
  living alone [@guo2017].
- **The same outlier cohort manufactures the opposite pole too** — the protective fermented-dairy and
  cheese signals also vanished once it was removed
  [@guo2017].

One confounded cohort generates both the milk scare and the cheese halo. That is the cleanest sign the
signal carries no information. The verdict rests on leave-one-out sensitivity, a weak check, with no
genetic instrument in this source — but for milk that gap is closed below.

## Fermented dairy carries a faint, fragile heart signal

The only inverse cardiovascular signals in dairy sit in the fermented category. A fermented-dairy
meta-analysis (Zhang 2019, 385,122 people) found highest-versus-lowest intake associated with lower CVD
(OR 0.83, 0.76-0.91), with cheese 0.87 (0.80-0.94) and yoghurt 0.78 (0.67-0.89)
[@zhang2019]. Two facts shrink it.
Heterogeneity was extreme (I2 = 94%), so the pooled figure is a washed-out average across very different
studies. And Guo's per-unit slope makes the effect marginal — about 2% per 20 g/day — and traces it to
that same Swedish cohort, without which the fermented and cheese signals disappear
[@guo2017]. The honest reading:
fermented dairy is at best weakly and fragilely favourable for the heart, and it is no reason to start
eating cheese for your arteries ([[Fermented Foods and Health]]).

## Dairy nudges type-2 diabetes down — and it's yoghurt and low-fat doing the work

Diabetes is the one endpoint where dairy shows a consistent, if small, benefit — and it disaggregates
cleanly. Gijsbers' dose-response meta-analysis (22 cohorts, 43,118 cases) found total dairy at RR 0.97
per 200 g/day (0.95-1.00), a 3% lower risk, borderline and heterogeneous
[@gijsbers2016]. But the whole-dairy number
hides where the signal lives:

- **Yoghurt carries it, and it saturates early** — a 14% lower risk at 80 g/day (about one small pot;
  RR 0.86, 0.83-0.90), with no further drop above that
  [@gijsbers2016].
- **Low-fat dairy is suggestive** (RR 0.96 per 200 g/day, 0.92-1.00) but not significant.
- **Milk, cheese and high-fat dairy are null** — milk RR 0.97 (0.93-1.02), cheese 1.00 per 10 g/day,
  high-fat dairy 0.98 (0.93-1.04) [@gijsbers2016].

The high-versus-low and food-group analyses land in the same place — Mishali at \~10% lower T2D
[@mishali2019], Schwingshackl
at RR 0.97 per 200 g/day (0.94-0.99) [@schwingshackl2017t2d] — but they **re-pool the same cohorts**, so the agreement is robustness of pooling, not
independent confirmation. Two cautions travel with the signal. Mishali's effect ran only in women (men
were null), a subgroup claim with no identified mechanism, so treat it as a hypothesis rather than a
sex-specific rule. And nearly every dairy-diabetes analysis clusters in industry-adjacent research groups
— a uniformity tell about the field, discounted symmetrically rather than used to delete the finding.

### A genetic study confirms milk has no causal effect — but tests milk only

The observational milk-null always carried an escape hatch: residual confounding could hide a real
effect either way. A Mendelian-randomization study breaks that ambiguity, because a birth-fixed genotype
cannot be confounded by the lifestyle that tracks milk drinking. Vissers used the lactase-persistence
gene as an instrument for milk intake in 21,820 people (9,686 diabetes cases) and found genetically
predicted milk **causally null** for diabetes: HR 0.99 per 15 g/day (0.93-1.05)
[@vissers2019dairymr]. The authors read the
observational inverse signal as «likely caused by residual confounding»
[@vissers2019dairymr].

The boundary matters as much as the result. The gene raises milk intake and essentially nothing else in
the dairy group — yoghurt and cheese are individually null on the instrument
[@vissers2019dairymr] — so the study is a
clean test of the **milk** arm and silent on yoghurt. That is exactly where the observational protective
signal lives, and this genetic test cannot refute it. Reading the milk-null as sinking the yoghurt signal
would be the category error the whole decomposition exists to prevent. So the composite is: milk has no
causal diabetes effect, yoghurt keeps a small observational one, and neither is a big lever.

## Milk is not a bone-fracture lever

The intuitive story — dairy is rich in calcium and protein, so more dairy means stronger bones — does not
survive the strongest study designs. Malmir's meta-analysis (34 studies, 616,000 people for fracture)
found the protective associations **only in cross-sectional and case-control studies**, the designs most
prone to reverse causation; in prospective cohorts, dairy and milk were null for both osteoporosis and
hip fracture [@malmir2019]. The
milk-fracture paradox is stark: weaker designs say high milk drinkers have 25% *less* hip fracture,
while cohorts show a 9% *higher* risk per 200 g/day (RR 1.09, 1.07-1.11)
[@malmir2019]. That cohort harm
signal is largely driven by the same Michaelsson Swedish cohort behind the milk-mortality scare, so read
it as confounded and unadjudicated, not as evidence milk breaks bones ([[Dairy and Bone Health]]).

Two loose ends stay honest. A dose-response J-shape put dairy above 250 g/day at higher osteoporosis risk,
but it rests on the same reverse-causation-prone designs and is unadjudicated. And the food route lands
where the supplement route does. Calcium plus vitamin D is also null for fracture in replete adults, so
neither the glass of milk nor the pill moves fracture risk in someone already well-nourished
([[Deficiency Repletion vs Enhancement]]). What prevents falls and fractures in older adults is exercise,
not dairy intake.

## Dairy is not a brain-health lever either

Villoz's meta-analysis (15 cohorts, 312,580 people, no trials) is the only dose-response evidence on dairy
and the brain, and its headline is null: highest-versus-lowest intake RR 0.94 (0.82-1.07), with a
prediction interval (0.61-1.45) so wide a future study could as easily find harm as benefit
[@villoz2024dairy].

The one apparent signal — a dose-response *nadir* around 150 g/day — is a **pooling artifact**. The
protective arm below 150 g/day is drawn by low-intake Asian cohorts (RR 0.83), the flat arm above it by
high-intake European cohorts (RR 1.01, null), and the nadir sits exactly on the seam between two
populations sampled at non-overlapping doses
[@villoz2024dairy]. Milk alone and cheese alone were
both null, and the whole association vanished in the studies that adjusted for overall dietary pattern —
dairy reads as a marker of a healthier diet, not a cause
[@villoz2024dairy]. For a reasonably healthy adult in
a Western intake range, there is nothing here to worry about or optimize ([[Dairy and Cognitive Decline]]).

## Dairy and cancer is the one endpoint this evidence can't answer

This is a genuine gap, not a null. The dairy chapters of the major cancer report were never extracted into
this fabric, so no magnitude for either cancer is held here. What is known at the grading level runs in
**two opposite directions**, and they must stay un-netted. The World Cancer Research Fund grades dairy and
dietary calcium as *probably protective* against colorectal cancer, and dairy as *probably associated with
higher* prostate cancer risk ([[Red and Processed Meat and Cancer]]). No dose-response magnitude is held
for either, and the weighing of a colorectal benefit against a prostate risk is the person's, not the
evidence's ([[The Weighting Problem - Why Population Guidance Is Ill-Posed and Individual Advice Is Not]]).
Anyone using dairy to weigh a personal cancer decision needs the underlying reviews, which this cut does
not yet hold.

## Does the food matrix change what the saturated fat does?

The recurring hypothesis is that the same saturated fat behaves differently inside cheese than inside
butter — that the food's structure, not just its fat, drives the outcome. A controlled-feeding contrast
supports the *direction*: matched for fat, protein and lactose, butter raised LDL-cholesterol more than
cheese did, with milk in between [@thorning2017]. So the matrix
plausibly softens what the fat does.

Three limits keep this a framing, not a finding. The endpoint is LDL, a surrogate, not a heart attack, and
the review itself asks whether the effect is even large enough to matter in a whole diet
[@thorning2017]. It was also funded by a milk-industry body and
led by a known dairy-fat defender, so its favourable direction is exactly what such a sponsor would push.
It is credited as a mechanism to weigh, not as independent corroboration, and it does not lift the
confidence of anything above.

The broader reassessment of saturated fat argues the same thing — the health effect depends on the
whole food, not the isolated fat [@astrup2020] — and it
carries its own industry and design caveats. The saturated-fat verdict itself belongs to
[[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]] and [[Does Reducing Saturated Fat Reduce Cardiovascular Events]].
This cut carries only the dairy-specific matrix nuance, which for now points in a plausible direction on a
surrogate endpoint and no further.

## What to actually do about dairy

Dairy is a small lever, and the attention it gets runs far ahead of the effect. If the big rocks —
smoking, excess weight, inactivity, poor sleep, heavy alcohol — are still in play, dairy is not where the
gains are ([[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]]). Within dairy, the realistic moves net small:

- **Keep or add, without worry:** milk (causally null for diabetes, artifact-only for mortality), cheese,
  and full-fat dairy. The full-fat versus low-fat distinction guidance emphasizes is not supported by the
  hard-outcome evidence.
- **A mild nudge, if you want one:** yoghurt and low-fat dairy carry a small diabetes benefit that
  saturates around one pot of yoghurt a day. It is real but modest, and observational.
- **Swap for a better food where it competes:** replacing butter with plant oils tracks lower mortality —
  a substitution worth making, though it is the oil earning the credit as much as the butter losing it.
- **Do not eat dairy to build bone or protect the brain:** neither endpoint responds to it.

Cancer is the open question — two graded directions, no magnitude held here, and the trade-off is
personal. For everything else, the number worth watching is not your dairy intake. Every estimate above is
observational apart from the milk genetic study; no long-term trial of whole dairy on hard outcomes
exists, and none is likely, so these verdicts are calibrated small effects, not settled certainties.
