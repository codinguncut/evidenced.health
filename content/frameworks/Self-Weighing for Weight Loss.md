**The decision this page changes.** Whether someone trying to lose weight should step on a scale
regularly, how often, and whether a scale on its own, without a programme, does anything. The lever is
cheap and easy to stop, so the questions are direction, size and which claim the evidence actually
supports. The phrase *self-weighing works* runs together three different comparisons with three
different answers, and the decision depends on which one applies. [inferred from @madigan2015weigh]

## The evidence base

One systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of RCTs (Madigan 2015; searched to August
2014): 24 trials, 21 pooled, adults with overweight or obesity in **weight-loss** trials. Trials had
23 to 415 participants (median 110) and 15 of 24 ran in the USA; four enrolled only women and the
rest were 26 to 91% women (median 75%). Weight was measured objectively in all but one trial. Missing
weights were standardised to baseline weight carried forward (BOCF): «We used BOCF because this
mitigates bias that may arise because participants that do less well may be reluctant to be followed
up.» One trial was an exception: «One trial used a conservative method of imputation that was similar
to this imputation, by adding 0.5 kg to the last weight observed carried forwards. The trial was
included within the analysis as presented [21].» [@madigan2015weigh]

**What it cannot speak to: maintenance after loss.** The review excluded maintenance trials by design:
«Trials were excluded if they enrolled people after weight loss where the prime aim of the
intervention was to prevent weight regain or trials that enrolled people that aimed to help prevent
gradual weight gain.» [@madigan2015weigh]
Eleven full texts were excluded on these grounds. So *weighing helps you keep it off* is not tested
here; see the maintenance section below.

**Appraisal.** Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane tool; most trials gave too little
information to judge sequence generation or allocation concealment (low risk in 11 and 7 trials
respectively; 4 high risk on concealment), 6 were high risk on attrition (of 23 reporting trials, though the counts sum to 24; the Methods and Results also give different attrition thresholds —), and selective
reporting could be checked in only 4. The review gives no GRADE or other certainty rating and does
not assess publication bias. `[searched: GRADE / certainty / publication bias / funnel — zero across the source]`
[@madigan2015weigh]

## Three comparisons, three answers

All figures are between-group mean differences in weight at programme end (intervention minus
control). The text and Table 3 give no pooled control-arm weight change, so no absolute baseline sits
beside them.
[@madigan2015weigh]

| Comparison | Control arm | Trials (n) | MD kg (95% CI) | I² | 95% prediction interval | Timepoint |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scale alone | no intervention | 1 (183) | -0.5 (-1.3 to 0.3) | — | — | end of a 3-month intervention |
| Self-weighing plus other self-regulation parts, added to a programme | the same programme | 4 (274) | -1.7 (-2.6 to -0.8) | 0% | -7.5 to 4.1 | programme end, 10 weeks to 7.2 months |
| A programme that includes self-weighing | no or minimal intervention | 15 (2490) | -3.4 (-4.2 to -2.6) | 82% | -6.9 to 0.1 | programme end |

**Scale alone: insufficient evidence, not no effect.** One trial, run by the review's authors,
tested advice to self-weigh without a programme and found -0.5 kg (-1.3 to 0.3) after three months.
The authors' own reading moves in two steps: «How- ever, unlike Michie and colleagues, we found that
self- monitoring alone was ineffective for weight loss. However, only one study investigated this and
the estimate was imprecise enough to encompass effects that would be worthwhile.»
[@madigan2015weigh]

**Added to a programme: a direction, with the scale's share not isolated.** Four trials gave both
arms the same programme and added self-weighing to one arm. The review's Results text describes an extra
part in all four, though its sentence on three of them cites only two trials: one added training in using the scale like a blood glucose monitor plus weight feedback,
and «The other three trials gave participants the option to record their diet and physical activity
[38, 39].» The Methods had planned a split of this set: «Within this group, there were two subgroups: trials where self-weighing was the only addition to the behavioural programme and trials where several self-regulatory in- terventions including self-weighing were added to the behavioural programme.»
Neither the Results nor Fig. 2 reports that split (Fig. 2 pools the four as one set, and Table 2 codes
all four as the same programme plus «self-monitoring/ self-weighing techniques»), so whether any of the
four added the scale alone is unclear. The authors' summary: «There was evi- dence that adding advice to self-weigh to a
behavioural programme improves its effectiveness, but only four trials have assessed this, and the
estimate of effect was impre- cise and clouded by the use of other self-regulatory ele- ments.»
Heterogeneity was 0% (Fig. 2 prints tau² = 0.00) across the four, but the printed prediction interval
runs from -7.5 to 4.1 kg; an interval that wide is hard to square with a tau² of zero,
so the width may be a reporting error, though the authors' reading is: «in some
interventions participants would lose a considerable amount of weight but in others interventions
participants may gain weight.» Three of the four asked for daily weighing. In the forest plot (Fig. 2)
only one trial's interval excludes zero (Wing 2010, -1.9, -3.0 to -0.8); the other three cross it.
[@madigan2015weigh]
[@madigan2015weigh]

**A programme that includes it, against minimal care: the programme's effect.** The -3.4 kg compares a
whole multi-component programme that includes self-weighing with no or minimal intervention. The
authors' reading of it: «There was strong evidence that behavioural weight loss programmes that
incorporate self-weighing are more effective than minimal interventions.» Their scope statement covers
all the pooled numbers, though elsewhere they call the mean differences «the net effect of the
self-weighing inter- vention itself» and conclude «Self-weighing as part of a multicomponent programme
is effective in facilitating weight loss». The scope statement: «Our aim is not to produce a definitive estimate of the effect of self-
weighing on weight loss at a particular point in time; rather it is to find evidence that
self-weighing as a tech- nique is effective.» The next two sentences say how to read the numbers:
«As length of follow-up is the same in both intervention and control groups, any differences are down either to random variation or differences in effectiveness of self-weighing. Thus the estimates we produce should not be read as estimates of the effect of self-weighing, but as valid evidence that self-weighing can be effective in these contexts.»
They add: «It was impossible to separately code those
techniques used to promote self-weighing and those that related to other components of the
intervention.»
[@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] The -3.4 kg and the -1.7 kg come
from different trial sets, so the -3.4 kg cannot be split into a programme share and a scale share.
The closest estimate of adding self-weighing is the add-on comparison, -1.7 kg (-2.6 to -0.8), and it
too carries co-delivered parts, so it is not the scale's effect alone either; the authors' reading
above (evidence that self-weighing can be effective in these contexts, not an estimate of the effect of
self-weighing) applies to it too. Read -1.7 kg as the held magnitude for adding self-weighing, with its
co-delivered parts, to a programme like these, not as the effect of the scale by itself.

## How often to weigh — daily vs weekly is a between-trial contrast

Within the 15 programme-vs-minimal trials, seven asked for at least daily weighing (-3.6 kg, -5.4 to -1.8;
I² 91%) and eight for less than daily, seven of them weekly (-3.3 kg, -4.0 to -2.5; I² 65%); subgroup
difference p = 0.57. These are **different trials** with different programmes, not a randomised
comparison of frequencies: «Our analyses address- ing theoretical constructs were analyses across
trials and therefore observational, as no trial directly addressed these issues.» The authors
conclude: «There was no evidence that daily weighing led to greater weight loss than weekly weighing
and it ap- pears that both may be effective when combined with multi-component interventions.»
[@madigan2015weigh]

No difference shown is not equivalence. The daily subgroup's interval is wide (-5.4 to -1.8) and its
prediction interval (-10.2 to 3.0) crosses zero, while the less-than-daily prediction interval
(-4.6 to -1.0) does not. In the add-on set, weekly weighing rests on one trial (Allen); the other
three asked for daily. Adherence was measured in only three trials, all
daily (44%, 50%, 95%), so whether people keep up daily weighing as well as weekly is unknown. A
within-trial randomised daily-vs-weekly trial was in progress when the review was written.
[@madigan2015weigh] — closes when a pooled
or single randomised daily-vs-weekly contrast inside the same programme is held.

## Accountability — between-trial, and mostly about weight lost

Trials where participants «knew that they were accountable to a therap- ist/researcher» (14 trials,
-3.6 kg, -4.6 to -2.7; I² 83%, prediction interval -7.5 to 0.3) beat the two without (-2.3 kg; Table 3
prints -3.2 to -1.5, the text -3.1 to -1.5; I² 0%, no prediction interval printed), p = 0.03. Two trials with strong accountability, where the therapist contacted anyone who did
not weigh, found -5.6 and -8.8 kg. The authors add that elsewhere the accountability «was more closely
related to weight lost and not the act of weighing», and the comparison is between trials, with only
two trials on one side. [@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] Read it as a hypothesis that
someone seeing the numbers helps, not as an established modifier: between-trial subgroups are held as
hypotheses (CLAUDE.md, the over-personalization rule).

## Maintenance and durability — what is and is not held

[@madigan2015weigh] Three trials followed
people beyond the last contact: -8.0 kg (-12.5 to -3.5) about 18 months after, -0.3 kg six months
after a single-session programme, and -7.5 kg (-11.3 to -3.7) nine months after; pooled across 185
people, -5.5 kg (printed interval -11.4 to 4.7), I² 86%. The three come from
different comparison types (an add-on trial, a single-session programme, a programme vs minimal care).
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] So the pool does not estimate the durability of any one of the three comparisons above. Three small
trials with an interval crossing zero do not establish that any of these effects lasts.

The maintenance evidence the review cites is observational, and the authors lean toward it. They
suggest self-weighing may suit the long term because it is easy to keep up, and cite the National
Weight Control Registry: «Additionally because self-weighing is less cumbersome than recording diet and
physical activity it might be a behaviour that can be continued and therefore help weight control in
the longer term. The National Weight Control Registry has found that those who are successful at
preventing weight regain, after weight loss, weigh themselves on a regular basis [46].» On a separate
prospective study (ref 48; the quote's closing marker cites a different paper) they add a caveat: «Higher weighing frequency was associated with greater weight loss
and less weight regain at 24 months follow-up. However, greater motivation to maintain weight or
success in achieving weight mainten- ance may motivate people to weigh themselves frequently, which
makes observational data difficult to interpret [49].»
[@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] The authors' caveat names two
alternatives, motivation (a confounder) and success prompting the weighing (reverse causation); both
would apply to the registry association too, though they do not apply it there. The direction they suggest is plausible; no randomised maintenance estimate is
held. Self-weighing for maintenance is therefore insufficient evidence in the held fabric, not no
effect -> [[Weight-Loss Maintenance and Metabolic Adaptation]].

## The scale monitors the outcome, not a behaviour

Madigan separates self-weighing from the self-monitoring that behaviour-change reviews found effective
on its own: «Additionally self-weighing is different to the behaviours investigated by Michie and
colleagues, as self- weighing is monitoring the outcome rather than the be- haviour.» Their proposed
mechanism is that people must reflect on the reading and then change eating and activity, which a
scale alone may not prompt. [@madigan2015weigh]
[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] This is why a scale is not the
same tool as an activity tracker, which monitors the behaviour itself -> [[Wearable Activity Trackers and Physical Activity]].

## Open question — psychological harm

Whether regular weighing causes psychological harm (body dissatisfaction, low mood, disordered eating)
is **not settled by this review**. Most included trials did not report adverse effects; the three
that measured psychological outcomes reported no between-group differences, and the authors' own
caveat is that «few trials assessed whether self-weighing led to psychological problems». Two of
those three trials added non-randomised analyses that also found none: Steinberg found no difference
in body dissatisfaction or depressive symptoms between intervention participants instructed to weigh
daily who did and did not lose weight, and «Gokee LaRose and
colleagues found no relation- ship between change of frequency of self-weighing and disordered eating
[41].» [@madigan2015weigh]
 No excess detected in three small trials (two with non-randomised follow-up analyses) is not *shown safe*; the harm side needs its own
review and is held open here.

## Labelled default — what to do

[inferred from @madigan2015weigh] The action is cheap, reversible
and low-cost, so it gets a plain default with its basis stated (CLAUDE.md, firmness scales with
stakes; [[Acting on a Direction Without a Magnitude]]):

- **If you are in a weight-loss programme, weigh yourself regularly** — daily or weekly, whichever you
  will keep doing. Basis: four RCTs adding self-weighing to a programme, each with a co-delivered
  part per the review's Results text (an option to record diet and activity in three, self-regulation
  training with weight feedback in one; the Methods' planned self-weighing-only subgroup is never
  reported): -1.7 kg (-2.6 to -0.8) at programme end within about 7 months, printed prediction
  interval -7.5 to 4.1 (flagged above as possibly misreported); the four trials show no
  heterogeneity (I² 0%). The scale's own share is not isolated.
- **Frequency is your choice.** Basis: a between-trial comparison across programme trials
  (p = 0.57); in the add-on set weekly rests on one trial; no randomised daily-vs-weekly test is held.
- **Someone seeing the numbers may help** (a coach, a programme, a person). Basis: between-trial,
  two trials on one side; a hypothesis.
- **Without a programme, a scale on its own is untested beyond one trial.** Trying it costs little;
  pair it with a plan for eating and activity, since the authors' proposed mechanism is that the
  reading must lead to a change in behaviour. Basis: one 3-month trial, -0.5 kg (-1.3 to 0.3), which
  the authors call imprecise enough to include a worthwhile effect: insufficient evidence, not proof
  of no effect; the pairing is the page's inference from the mechanism, not a tested combination.
- **Stop if it distresses you.** The harm question is open, and stopping costs little.

**Where it ranks.** A small refinement inside a programme, not a big rock. The closest estimate of
adding it is -1.7 kg (-2.6 to -0.8) on a weight surrogate, at programme end within about 7 months, with
co-delivered parts, a printed prediction interval crossing zero (its width flagged above as possibly
misreported) and unknown durability
-> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].

## Limits

- **Weight is a surrogate.** Weight is the only efficacy outcome pooled; three trials measured
  psychological outcomes and none pooled a patient-important outcome. [@madigan2015weigh]
- **Short.** Programme-end figures; the add-on trials ran 10 weeks to 7.2 months. [@madigan2015weigh]
- **Mostly US trials, mostly women** (15 of 24 in the USA; four women-only trials, median 75% women
  in the rest; 11 internet-based or mixed). [@madigan2015weigh]
- **No certainty grading** and no publication-bias assessment; trial counts per comparison are small
  (1, 4, 15). [@madigan2015weigh]
- **Single source.** The page rests on one review; a second review of the same lever is the next
  source, not yet held.
