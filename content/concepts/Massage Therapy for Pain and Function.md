**Scope:** massage therapy as a modifiable, passive lever for *musculoskeletal pain* in the general
population (Part I of the SR series; cancer/surgery populations are separate). A function/QoL lever, in
scope. The patient-important outcomes here — self-reported pain, physical function/range of motion,
anxiety, HrQoL — are measured *worst* and the intervention is *unblindable*, so this page carries
**honest low certainty, not confident advice**. A **peripheral, small-effect lever** by the Layer-1
ranking (heavily used and discussed — attention is an anti-signal). A separate facet, massage for
soreness after an exercise session in healthy adults, is a different population, outcome and exposure
(one session) and has its own section below.

## The verdict (pain populations) — safe, small and short-term specific benefit, and the comparator sets the answer

[@crawford2016]
Gold-tier SR+MA of RCTs (99 studies qualitatively, 67 in the general-pain population, 32 pooled;
appraised on a SIGN 50 checklist *modified to exclude blinding*). The single most decision-relevant
pattern is that **one intervention gives three different answers depending on what it is compared to** —
the effect (standardized mean difference, Cohen's d; negative = massage better for pain) shrinks across
three different kinds of control (superseded 2026-10-22, Crawford re-read: *monotonically as the control
tightens* read them as three steps on one placebo scale, which they are not; see below):

| Pain comparison | Studies (pooled n) | SMD (95% CI) | VAS mm (95% CI) | I² | Confidence* | Recommendation† |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| vs **no treatment** (waitlist) | 4 (219) | -1.14 (-1.94, -0.35) | -28.58 (-48.48, -8.70) | 84% | A | Strong, in favor |
| vs **sham** (light/simple touch) | 5 (290) | -0.44 (-0.84, -0.05) | -11.10 (-20.88, -1.33) | 58% | B | Weak, in favor |
| vs **active comparator** | 24 (1349) | -0.26 (-0.53, 0.003) | -6.60 (-13.30, 0.08) | 80% | B | Weak, in favor |

*Samueli Institute «Overall Synthesis Evaluation Criteria», **not** GRADE: A = further research very
unlikely to change the estimate; B = *likely to have an important impact and may change it*. Nearly
every cell is B. †By the Evidence for Massage Therapy (EMT) Working Group.

**Reading the gradient — much of the no-treatment benefit may be nonspecific.** The large, grade-A,
strong-recommendation result is the one measured against the *weakest* control, and the SR says why:
«no treatment control groups do not control for nonspe-cific effects of attention and touch; resulting
in massage interventions tending to be more successful than such controls» [@crawford2016].
The review also notes «there is debate about whether touch control is a true placebo as touch can elicit
nonspecific psycho- logical effects», and that «treatment as usual controls may assign indi- viduals to
care that they may have already previously tried and found unsuccessful». [@crawford2016]
[inferred from @crawford2016] So the -1.14 vs no-treatment bundles the
specific massage effect with attention, touch, placebo and therapeutic alliance; the sham arm (-0.44)
removes some of that, though touch may not be a clean placebo. Against an active alternative massage has
a small edge of -0.26, with the CI touching null (upper bound 0.003). That contrast is comparative
effectiveness, not a tighter placebo: the comparators carry their own effects, and a treatment-as-usual
arm may be care that already failed, which could flatter massage. Only the no-treatment-to-sham step
speaks to nonspecific effects, and even that compares two different trial pools whose CIs overlap
(-1.94 to -0.35 vs -0.84 to -0.05). This is [[The Comparator Problem]] shown *within a single intervention* —
the kind of comparator was doing the work.

## The specific effect is below the review's own clinical-importance threshold

[@crawford2016]
The authors set a 20-mm VAS reduction as clinically important: «While the authors relied on a cut-off
point of 20-mm for the VAS as a clinically important reduction in pain, this was noted to be interpreted
with caution.» The placebo-controlled (vs-sham) pain reduction is **-11.10 mm** — statistically
significant but **below the 20-mm bar the review itself set**; vs an active comparator it is -6.60 mm.
Only the confounded no-treatment contrast (-28.58 mm) clears it. So the *specific* (beyond-placebo)
analgesic effect, where it is isolable at all, is real but **sub-clinically-important** on the authors'
own yardstick. [inferred from @crawford2016]
The comparison is indicative only: the pooled figures are between-group differences, while a 20-mm
threshold of this kind is usually a change within a patient, a different quantity.

## Function, mood, quality of life — weaker or no recommendation

[@crawford2016]

- **Activity / range of motion (positive = massage better): no recommendation, either comparator.** vs
  sham SMD 0.36 (95% CI -0.53, 1.25; 3 studies, n=211; I²=89%) — CI spans null; vs active comparator
  -0.23 (95% CI -0.50, 0.05; 7 studies, n=450) — *favors the comparator*. Function is the weakest signal,
  not the most robust one.
- **Anxiety (vs active comparator): weak, in favor** — SMD -0.57 (95% CI -1.06, -0.09; 6 studies,
  n=210) — but Egger's test for publication bias was **significant**, so read with caution.
- **HrQoL (SF-36/SF-12, vs active comparator): weak, in favor** — SMD 0.14 (95% CI **-0.09**, 0.36;
  4 studies, n=424) — the CI crosses null, so *in favor* is a generous call on a point estimate.

## Durability is unestablished

[@crawford2016]
Effects are measured almost entirely at post-treatment. The one durability window — pain vs active
comparator at 6 months (3 studies, n=136) — ran *against* massage: «All studies favored the active
comparator at a 6-month follow-up (SMD ¼ 0.49; 95% CI, 0.03 to 0.94...».
One no-treatment-controlled study also reported 6-month data, «with a SMD of -1.71 at 6 months compared
to -0.23 at post treatment»; against no treatment it cannot isolate a specific effect. [@crawford2016]
So there is **no evidence of a durable specific benefit**, and the 6-month data against active treatment
favor the alternative. Treat this as insufficient-evidence on durability, not established short-term-only benefit.

## Why the certainty is low — and safety is the load-bearing finding

- **Unblindable + blinding dropped from the appraisal.** The SIGN 50 checklist was «modified to exclude
  blinding» [@crawford2016] because massage
  cannot be blinded, so the *high/acceptable quality* ratings sit on a scale that removed the very axis
  massage fails — apparent quality is inflated relative to a drug trial. Heterogeneity is large for
  most pain and function pools (I² 58-89%), low for HrQoL (13.44%).
- ***Massage* is not one exposure.** «There is wide variety in the types, styles, dosaging, nam-ing
  conventions, and practitioner qualifications of inter-ventions labeled as "massage therapy."»
  [@crawford2016] — no standard definition or
  dosing (the SR proposes a STRICT-M reporting checklist), so a pooled estimate averages heterogeneous
  interventions, the same defect as a food *label* spanning heterogeneous items
  -> [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]]. The authors caution against building clinical guidelines
  until mechanisms and reporting standardize: «the development of clinical guide-lines is cautioned
  against for massage therapy» [@crawford2016].
- **Safety is the decision-changer.** «Based on a review of the literature, massage is generally a safe
  intervention. Most reported adverse events are minor and have low incident rates»
  [@crawford2016] — soreness, transient pain,
  stiffness; the review scores safety +2 (appears safe) for most comparisons. A safe, low-harm lever is
  attractive *as an adjunct* even at low certainty and small effect — judged against drugs/surgery with
  their harms.

## A different population: soreness after exercise in healthy adults

A meta-analysis of single post-exercise recovery sessions in healthy adults aged 18-65 ranks massage
first of ten techniques: DOMS SMD **-2.26 (95% CI -3.05 to -1.47)**, 14 groups, 158 people; perceived
fatigue -2.55 (-3.49 to -1.62), 7 groups, 64 people; still significant on DOMS at 96 h. «Massage was found
to be the most powerful procedure that induced signiﬁcant beneﬁts in DOMS and perceived fatigue,
regardless of the subjects (athletes, sedentary subjects).» (The review did find recovery techniques as a
group reduced DOMS more in males than in females.) The ranking compares technique pools drawn from
different trials, not head to head. For a protocol it cites an earlier
meta-analysis (Torres 2012, not held): «A 20–30 min massage that is performed immediately following or up
to 2 h after exercise has been shown to eﬀectively reduce DOMS for 24 h after exercise (Torres et al.,
2012).»
[@dupuy2018recovery]

The same review never states what its SMDs are measured against, includes within-person repeated-measure
designs, gives no certainty rating, and concedes that «the beneﬁcial eﬀect arising from the placebo eﬀect
of recovery techniques could not be eliminated». Its DOMS funnel plot leaned positive («23 studies out of
57 were distributed toward a positive eﬀect»), and the massage pool is small, about 11 people per group.
[@dupuy2018recovery]

Not a tension: different population, outcome and comparator. What carries over is the method lesson.
In the pain trials, massage's effect fell from -1.14 against no treatment to -0.44 against a sham, which
suggests much of the no-treatment contrast was attention, touch and expectation rather than massage
itself (the two are different trial sets, so this is a between-pool reading, not a within-trial
contrast). The soreness figure comes from comparisons whose control is not stated, though the review's
own examples of massage trials use passive rest, and in which placebo could not be removed, so -2.26 is
likely inflated: it probably compares against passive rest and bundles specific and nonspecific
effects. Touch and attention plausibly favour a therapist-delivered technique like massage more than
compression or immersion, so the ordering, not only the size, is exposed. Whether massage beats a sham for
soreness is not answered by any held source. Type-F: the pain review's comparator split bounds how far
the recovery review's ranking can be read.

## Decision relevance

[inferred from @crawford2016]

- **Better than nothing, not a substitute for active care.** vs doing nothing, massage helps pain and
  is safe — a reasonable adjunct or patient-preference option. But its beyond-placebo effect (vs sham)
  has a point estimate below the review's 20-mm bar (-11.10 mm, 95% CI -20.88 to -1.33; an indicative
  comparison, and touch may not be a clean placebo), and its edge over active treatments is small and, in
  the 6-month data, reversed, so it should **not displace
  a better-evidenced active lever** (exercise/PT for the same pain ->
  [[Chronic Pain and Physical Activity]]); frame it as an addition, not a replacement.
- **A peripheral lever, exactly as the ranking predicts.** Heavily used and discussed, mostly
  small-effect, short-duration — it earns a modest place, not a big-rock slot.
- **Self-report + unblindable → expect honest uncertainty.** The outcomes are the ones instruments
  measure worst -> [[Surrogate Outcomes]], [[Measurement Error in Dietary Assessment]]; the open loop
  (no long-term patient-important trajectory) stays open.
- **After a workout, for soreness: optional, not needed.** Massage tops a single-session ranking of
  recovery techniques for soreness and fatigue in the days after, but the size is unblinded and its
  comparator unstated, regular use is not tested in the held review, and it costs time and money. Use it
  if you like it; do not buy it as a training requirement.
  [inferred from @dupuy2018recovery] (operationalizes the
  post-exercise section above). For a new exerciser the more basic lever is the dose of new, especially
  eccentric, work: start low and build up. That is standard practice, not trial-tested, resting on the
  Cochrane stretching review's account of what causes soreness (carried on
  [[Flexibility Training and Stretching]]); no held source compares it with massage.
