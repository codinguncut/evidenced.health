A **modality-choice** page, not a fall-prevention page. That exercise improves balance and prevents
falls in community-dwelling older adults is high-certainty -> [[Exercise for Preventing Falls in Older Adults]]
(in care homes and after stroke or hospital discharge it is not established); the active
ingredient there is *safely challenging balance* at a sufficient prescribed dose (both between-trial
associations on that page), and water is one place to do the balance work. The question here is
narrow and peripheral: for an older adult, does doing that work **in water (AE)** beat doing it **on
land (LE)** — and when is the aquatic modality worth choosing? Single gold SR+MA (Deng 2024, 29 RCTs).
 (framing; the falls evidence and its strata are on the linked nucleus)

## The honest verdict — AE is roughly equivalent to, not clearly better than, LE

The source's own headline ("AE offers better benefits than LE") **overstates its data**. Of four pooled
outcomes, only one still favours water after the authors' sensitivity analysis
[@deng2024]. The authors state its purpose: «Sensitivity
analysis was performed to explore the stability of the results by removing one trial with a distinctly
different direction of change in each category of balance-related outcomes.»
[@deng2024]. The excluded trials are picked by
their direction of change, and the authors report how far each estimate moved: by -0.31 in the BBS,
-0.24 in the TUGT, +0.09 in the 6 MWT and -1.44 in the 30 CST
[@deng2024]. In three of four
outcomes the removal pulled the estimate toward no difference (the 6 MWT moved slightly toward water
and stayed non-significant), so for those three the exclusion worked against water, not for it: the BBS edge survives it at a smaller size (I² 87%, down from 94%), the chair-stand edge does not
 (corrected 2026-10-28: was *an AE edge that survives it is weaker confirmation than one
surviving a pre-specified exclusion*, which the reported shifts contradict):

| Outcome | AE vs LE, SMD (95% CI) | After sensitivity analysis | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BBS** (overall balance) | 1.13 (0.25 to 2.00), I²=94% | **0.82 (0.17 to 1.48), I²=87% — persists** | the one AE edge left after the authors' exclusion |
| **30 CST** (chair-stand) | 2.02 (0.50 to 3.54), I²=96% | **0.58 (-0.20 to 1.36), p=0.15 — flips NULL** | fragile; do not rely on |
| **TUGT** (dynamic balance) | 0.44 (-0.04 to 0.91), I²=85% | NS | no modality difference |
| **6 MWT** (walk capacity) | 0.13 (-0.16 to 0.43), I²=62% | NS | no modality difference |

- **Only BBS persists** as an AE advantage after the authors' sensitivity analysis, which excluded
  «trials with a dis- tinctly opposite direction of change» (the exclusion moved BBS toward null, so
  the surviving edge is not an artifact of it, above); the chair-stand result goes null, and the two dynamic-balance / walking outcomes
  show no modality difference at all [@deng2024]. BBS
  heterogeneity is still I² = 87% after the exclusion, so even that magnitude is not a stable number — report the
  *direction* (AE ≥ LE on overall balance), not the point estimate.
- This is **weak evidence (confidence: low)**: a single SR+MA, extreme heterogeneity, three of four
  outcomes null or fragile, no durability data. The authors' Introduction notes that prior studies split ("there are also studies
  with the oppo- site conclusion, suggesting that LE may be more effective than AE"), and their
  Discussion hedges their own result: «these results must be inter- preted with caution»
  [@deng2024]. The defensible claim is **equivalence with a
  possible small overall-balance edge for water**, not superiority.

## Why the modality can still matter — a route-(e) constraint lever, not a route-(b) effect edge

The decision-change is not *prefer water for the balance effect*. The relative effect is close to a
wash. The lever is **feasibility and safety for the stratum that cannot train well on land**
[@deng2024]:

- «AE provides a safer training environment for participants than LE», and «buoy- ancy counteracts the
  effects of gravity, which reduces the joint burden, alleviates pain, and promotes relaxa- tion»
  [@deng2024]. These are the Introduction's mechanism
  claims; the only safety data is «There were no adverse events reported among the included studies»
  [@deng2024], which is no reported harm, not shown safer.
  The authors' closing claim that AE «can achieve greater safety» [@deng2024]
  therefore has no comparative safety data under it.
- For a **joint-limited, deconditioned, or weight-bearing-painful** older adult (knee/hip OA, obesity,
  high fall-anxiety on uneven ground), buoyancy removes the pain and fall-hazard ceilings that cap
  land-based dosing — so water lets them *do* the balance work at all. Under *adherence-is-part-of-the-
  effect*, a sustained aquatic programme can beat a land one abandoned for pain. This is the same
  low-impact-substitution logic as [[Stationary Cycling for Knee Osteoarthritis]], applied to balance.
  [inferred from @deng2024]
- **The subgroup signals do NOT license effect-modification targeting.** AE "won" in different health
  subgroups on different outcomes (BBS in nervous-system/MSK; TUGT only cardiopulmonary; 30CST in
  healthy/cardiopulmonary), with no coherent pattern and "insufficient research to confirm this
  heterogeneity" [@deng2024]. These are the false-positive-
  prone route-(b) subgroup claims -> [[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]; treat them as
  hypothesis-generating, not as a map of who benefits more from water.
- **Whether water work reaches the falls *dose* is not shown in the held fabric (G-gap) `[2026-10-28]`.** In general
  community trials, the falls meta-regression ties the larger falls effects (potentially confounded,
  between-trial) to a *high* challenge to balance, meaning all three of
  «movement of the centre of mass, narrowing of the base of support and minimising upper limb support»,
  and to 3 or more hours a week of prescribed exercise (ratio of rate ratios 0.85, 0.73-1.00, and 0.77,
  0.65-0.91, both between-trial)
  [@sherrington2017falls].
  Deng pooled balance and walking tests, not falls. Its Discussion asserts a falls benefit on citation
  only («potentially mitigating the risk of falls» [13]; «a preventive approach for the older adults at
  risk of falling» [54, 69]), and it cites the 2017 finding as its reference 68 («exercise pro- grammes
  that challenge balance and are of a higher dose have larger effects»)
  [@deng2024]. But Deng does not code its programmes
  against those criteria `[searched: upper limb/base of support/center of mass/fall rate/number of falls, chunks 01-03; the base-of-support and center-of-mass hits are a definition of static balance, not programme coding]`, so the held fabric
  cannot say whether an aquatic programme counts as high-challenge balance work. Deng does report
  per-trial session length and frequency, «intervention duration (30–90 min), frequency (1–5 sessions
  per week), and total duration (4–40 weeks)» [@deng2024],
  so weekly session time can be read per trial; but the 2017 cut counts prescribed home exercise as
  well, so the two are not directly comparable. The equivalence above is on balance
  scores; the falls transfer is a named gap, which would need a trial of aquatic exercise with
  prospective fall counts.
  [inferred from @deng2024; @sherrington2017falls]

## Where this sits in the ranking — peripheral, by construction

For the healthy, mobile older adult, land exercise is cheaper, more accessible, roughly as effective on
balance scores, and the one with a falls-outcome estimate of its own in the held reviews: no held falls review
reports an aquatic-specific falls estimate, and Pillay lists aquatic only as an example inside its
«Exercise—other (e.g., square stepping, yoga, dance, aquatic)» intervention category (split into
network nodes by supervision, delivery and duration)
[@pillay2024falls],
so any aquatic trials it holds are pooled with yoga, dance and square stepping, and the held text does
not say whether any were included
`[searched: aquatic/aqua/water-based/hydrotherapy/water exercise across Sherrington 2017 (1 file), Sherrington 2019 (17), Racey 2021 (1), Pillay 2024 (5), Jimenez-Lupion (2); the only aquatic-relevant hit is Pillay's category definition, chunk 02 ('aqua' otherwise matches only artifacts such as 'a quantitative')]`.
So this is a **low-rank refinement** (*attention-is-an-anti-signal*: water-vs-land is a heavily-discussed
small-effect question). It rises only for the specific constraint stratum above, where it is not a
marginal gain but the difference between training and not training. It does **not** transport to the
middle-aged CRF/function decision — older-adult balance is a different support-factor profile — and
aquatic exercise is relevant there only as one low-impact modality option, not a general recommendation.
The loop stays open (R1): no operation here grades this against a realized fall or function trajectory.
