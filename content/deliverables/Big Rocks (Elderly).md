**A type of person, not a specific one — and not every older adult.** The person here is **around 70 and
carrying too much fat and too little muscle at once** (doctors call it *sarcopenic obesity*). Blood
pressure and blood sugar are usually up, the liver is fatty, the bones are thinning, ageing muscle
responds less well to protein and exercise, and there are often several medications already in play. This
is the *slowing-down-but-still-independent* older adult — not the frail or care-home resident, who is a
different case. It is the sibling of [[Big Rocks (Median)]], and the whole point of the pair is that
**several of the big levers flip** with age.

## Two things change, and they reorder everything

1. **What counts as a win changes.** At 70, **strength, muscle, bone, staying on your feet, and living
   independently** are outcomes that matter in their own right — not just how long you live. And with
   fewer years ahead, there is **less time for a slow-acting benefit to pay off**, so a lever that only
   helps decades later is worth less. Rank on *staying capable*, not on lifespan alone.
2. **The headline lever flips.** For the younger adult, the number-one move is *lose the belly fat*. Here,
   **aggressive dieting is a hazard** — it strips the very muscle and bone this person can least spare.
   Weight loss is now *conditional, and only if done in a muscle-sparing way*.

*One caveat runs through the whole page: most of the "this flips into a harm" reasoning comes from
biology plus how the evidence shifts with age — not from trials that measured the harm in older people.
Read it as "handle with care," not "proven to hurt."*

## (a) The big rocks — function first

**1. Strength training, with enough protein — the number-one lever here.**

- This is the strongest card the evidence holds for this group: it **builds and keeps muscle, strength
  and function**, and — with regular activity — cuts **falls, injurious falls, and probably fractures**
  ([[Muscle-Strengthening Activity and Mortality]], [[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]).
- **Protein target: about 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day** — the point where extra protein stops
  adding to training-built muscle in healthy adults (the evidence spans a wide ~1.0-2.2 range). The
  training does the work; protein is a supporting act ([[Protein and Resistance Training for Muscle and Strength]]).
- Two age-specific notes the evidence *does* support: the protein *supplement's* muscle boost is
  **smaller in older people**, and adequate protein plus training is **how you keep muscle while losing
  fat** — so protein is the safety rail on any weight loss (below).
- **What we do NOT have is a proven older-adult target.** Whether the ageing, less-responsive body needs
  *more* than 1.6 g/kg — and how to spread it across meals — is the top open question below, not a number
  to invent. (Strength training's own link to living longer is weakly evidenced; function, not lifespan,
  is why it tops the list.)

**2. Aerobic activity — for falls, function, and living longer.** The falls-and-fracture benefit above is
specifically shown in older adults; add the general survival benefit ([[Physical Activity Dose and Mortality]]). Note: *frailty itself is not a target* — the evidence base treats it as too loosely defined
to aim at directly.

## (b) The levers that flip or come with conditions

**Weight and belly fat — FLIPPED.**

- Do **not** push aggressive dieting: it burns muscle and bone. If weight loss is worth pursuing (for the
  fatty liver, blood sugar, blood pressure, or mobility), it must be **muscle-and-bone-sparing — paired
  with strength training and ~1.6 g/kg protein — and gradual**.
- On *how fast*: the UK guideline body (NICE) scrapped its old specific calorie-cut number as
  *arbitrary*, and deliberately made its aggressive-diet advice **weak**, citing the harm of *rapid
  regain and weight cycling* ([[Diets for Weight Loss - What NICE Recommends]]). So "slow, not fast" rests
  on that weak guidance plus biology (faster loss sheds more muscle and bone) — **not a measured speed
  limit**; a trial pinning down the right rate is a known gap (shared with the younger guide, Challenge
  #20).
- The honest bounds on weight loss itself: it did **not** cut heart attacks/strokes (Look AHEAD; a
  54-trial review agrees), though it lowers overall death rates by a non-heart route ([[Does Weight Loss Reduce Cardiovascular Events]]); **5%** loss clears liver fat, **7-10%** cools liver inflammation
  ([[Fatty Liver MASLD and Weight Loss]]). But here the muscle-and-bone cost can outweigh those — so the
  target is *body composition*, not the number on the scale.

**Blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol — treat by age and total picture.**

- Because this person's *absolute* risk is higher, lowering blood pressure buys real benefit
  ([[Blood Pressure Lowering and Cardiovascular Events]]) — intensive control cut events in older
  high-risk adults in the SPRINT trial, though at the cost of more fainting, low blood pressure, and
  kidney strain ([[Baseline Risk and the Relative-Absolute Split]]).
- On cholesterol: the artery-clogging particles genuinely *cause* heart disease, and the damage adds up
  over a lifetime ([[LDL ApoB and Cumulative Exposure]]) — **but a short runway blunts a lifetime
  payoff**, which is why guidelines pull back with age (European guidelines downgrade cholesterol drugs
  to "may be considered" at 70+, and the US task force finds *starting* a statin unsupported by evidence
  at 76+ — [[Statins for Primary Prevention and the Power of Zero CAC]], [[SCORE2 Baseline Risk and the ESC Treatment Thresholds]], [[Lifetime Benefit - The Frame for Younger Adults]]).
- **Being on several medications, plus fall and blood-pressure-on-standing risk, makes over-treatment its
  own harm** — so *appraise, don't prescribe* matters more here than anywhere.

## (c) Small or contested — further down the list than function

- **Saturated fat → unsaturated, sugar, and red/processed meat** are small levers here, below the
  muscle-and-function priority ([[Saturated Fat Intake and Replacement]], [[Free Sugars Intake]],
  [[Should Adults Reduce Red and Processed Meat]]). One twist: **meat is a protein source, which matters
  *more* at this age.**
- **Salt — with the emphasis reversed.** It is a small blood-pressure lever, but **cutting salt (or
  calories) too hard can worsen appetite, intake and frailty in the old** — so the "cut aggressively"
  framing of the younger guide is itself a risk here ([[Sodium Intake and Blood Pressure]]).
- **Alcohol** (interacts with medications and the liver) and **sleep** (~7-8 hours; long sleep is a *sign*
  of illness, not a target) are unchanged from the younger guide ([[Alcohol and Mortality and Vascular Disease]], [[Sleep Duration and Mortality]]).

## What we cannot yet say — this guide's main product

The evidence is genuinely **thin on the questions specific to older adults**, and naming those honestly
is much of this guide's value:

- **How much protein, and how to time it.** The mainstream idea — ageing muscle needs a *bigger* per-meal
  dose of protein (and its trigger amino acid, leucine) to respond, arguing for more, higher-quality,
  meal-spread protein (Challenge #22) — is **plausible but unproven in our evidence**. It even sits in
  tension with the one thing we do hold (that the protein *supplement* does *less* with age). Settling it
  needs a dedicated older-adult-dosing source. **Top gap to fill.**
- **Bones.** Which interventions (loading exercise, protein, weight) protect bone density is essentially
  unaddressed.
- **Losing fat while keeping muscle and bone** — the central trade-off, with no older-adult trial held.
- **How fast to lose, and how to maintain** — no trial on either (shared with [[Big Rocks (Median)]],
  Challenge #20).
- **The "obesity paradox"** — whether being mildly overweight is *protective* in the very old (reserve
  against illness) — unaddressed, and it bears directly on the weight lever.
- **Grip strength / muscle mass as warning signs** — a strong predictor for this group, staged but not
  yet ingested (Challenge #17).
- **Deprescribing** — reviewing and stopping medications is largely a doctor's job, out of scope here —
  named, not advised on.
- **Older-adult heart trials** (the elderly slice of SPRINT, plus HYVET and PROSPER) would firm up the
  age-adjusted section.

## The honest limits

- **This grades the evidence, not the outcome** — whether the reasoning is sound and faithful to its
  sources, never whether acting on it helped anyone.
- **It appraises, it does not prescribe — and that binds harder here:** juggling medications, stopping
  drugs, fall-risk and blood-pressure/sugar targets in older people are a clinician's calls, not this
  guide's.
- **A type, not a person, and not everyone:** the frail or care-home older adult is a different case, and
  the fit 70-year-old differs again; the flips above are the *starting point*, tuned per person.
- **Deliberately gap-heavy:** because the older-adult evidence is largely missing, much of the "this
  flips" reasoning is biology plus how the evidence shifts with age — not measured harm. Read it as
  conditionality.
