**A named diet is two interventions wearing one name: an *exclusion* (the foods it drops) and a
distinctive *inclusion* (the foods it makes you eat more of). Its measured or felt benefit is a sum of
the two, and the two often have opposite signs. The confound is that the diet's *identity* — its
memorable additions — collects credit that its *exclusions* earned.**

Keto is "eat fat"; carnivore is "eat meat"; Whole30 and paleo are "eat real food." Each name points at
what the diet *adds*. But each one also silently deletes ultra-processed food, sugar-sweetened
beverages, and refined-carbohydrate snacks — and the fabric holds that the deletion is a real lever
while the signature addition frequently is not. A person who improves on such a diet, and a testimonial
that credits the beef or the butter, are both committing the attribution the fabric already forbids for
single foods, run at the level of a whole pattern.

No source decomposes a real diet into these two arms and measures their relative share, so the claim
here is directional, not a quantity: the fabric's evidence *supports* the exclusion arm and often runs
against the distinctive addition. Which arm is larger for a given person is not something the fabric can
weigh.

## The decomposition



Split any named diet into two exposures and price them separately:

- **The exclusion arm.** What the diet removes — usually ultra-processed food, added sugar, and
  sugar-sweetened drinks, because almost every popular diet removes those regardless of its ideology.
  The fabric rates this lever as real: a controlled-feeding trial had people eat markedly more on an
  ultra-processed diet at matched macros -> [[Ultra-Processed Food and Health Outcomes]], and
  sugar-sweetened drinks carry a clean additive signal for diabetes and weight -> [[Free Sugars Intake]]. Whatever the diet is *called*, this is the arm the fabric's evidence actually supports —
  strongest on intake and weight surrogates, with the hard-outcome ultra-processed signal still
  composition-confounded.

- **The distinctive-inclusion arm.** What the diet's name actually promotes — the beef, the butter, the
  fat, the exclusion of plants. This is the *marginal* exposure, the part that separates this diet from
  another equally junk-free one, and it is where the fabric's directional evidence often runs the wrong
  way (saturated fat and LDL; a low-carbohydrate pattern raising LDL; fibre and plant foods removed with
  nothing substituted) -> [[Low-Carbohydrate vs Balanced-Carbohydrate Diets]].

## Why the comparator hides it



This is [[The Comparator Problem]] applied to a bundle, and the trick is in the *default* comparator.
Set the diet against a standard junk-heavy Western diet and the exclusion arm dominates, so the whole
bundle looks good — and its distinctive additions ride along on that verdict. Set it against the
*honest* comparator — an equally junk-free diet that also keeps fibre, plants, fish and plant oils — and
the exclusion arm is **matched out on both sides of the contrast**. What remains is the distinctive
inclusion alone, judged on its own evidence. The diet's apparent effect shrinks to the marginal
exposure, and that is usually the part the fabric is least happy about.

The move is the same one the fabric already makes for single foods: an observed healthy population is
not evidence for any one of its components, and the benefit of a fruit-and-vegetable pattern resists
being pinned to any single compound -> [[Is the Food Category Doing Any Work]], [[Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Health]]. Here the "population" is a diet and the "component" is its signature food.

## Not every diet is pure removal — the discriminator



The confound is a diagnostic, not a verdict that every named diet is only its exclusions. A diet earns
credit for its *additions* when a distinctive inclusion has its own evidence that survives the honest
comparator. The Mediterranean pattern is the nearest contrast case: its hard-endpoint trial signal comes
from named additions (olive oil, nuts) tested against a fat-reduction control -> [[Named Diet Programs Compared]], [[Dietary Patterns]]. That control is an imperfect comparator, not the fully junk-free one
this page defines, but it is non-trivial, because the control was not a junk-heavy diet either. So at
least part of the credit sits with the addition rather than a junk removal alone — even though the trial
cannot separate the oil from the nuts from the whole gestalt. The test is therefore per diet: **does a
distinctive addition survive a comparator that is not itself junk-heavy, or does only the exclusion?**

## Decision relevance



- **Separate the two arms before crediting a diet.** When someone reports a diet "worked," ask what it
  removed and what it distinctively added, then ask which arm the evidence supports. Most of the time
  the answer is: keep doing the removal, and the branded addition is optional at best.
- **The removal is the transferable lever.** Because almost any diet can drop ultra-processed food and
  sugar, the benefit is reachable without adopting the ideology attached to it — which is the Layer-1
  point that the big rock is the junk removal, not the diet's name -> [[Layer 1 - Ranking Interventions for a Stratum]].
- **The distinctive addition is where harm hides.** The marginal exposure that survives the
  matched-comparator is exactly where a diet can carry a route-(c) contraindication (a high-saturated-fat
  pattern for an atherogenic-lipoprotein responder), so it deserves the scrutiny the removal's halo would
  otherwise buy it off.
- **This is an open loop.** The fabric can say which arm the evidence supports; it cannot observe whether
  a given person, on a given named diet, was better off — only whether a well-informed advisor would
  credit the removal or the addition.
